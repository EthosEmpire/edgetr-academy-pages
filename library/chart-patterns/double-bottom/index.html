Double Bottom

A double bottom is two lows at a similar depth, separated by a bounce, that form after a decline. It is conventionally read as a possible reversal if the high between the lows breaks.

Why it matters

The double bottom is the mirror image of the double top: sellers pushed price down to an area twice and could not extend the decline either time.

It attracts attention because people like to identify the end of a fall. That is exactly why it deserves care. Every downtrend contains bounces, and many of those bounces are followed by a second low that looks like a floor for a few candles and then gives way.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a prior decline, so there is a move that could reverse;
  • a first low, followed by a bounce that leaves a clear swing high;
  • a second low at a similar depth to the first;
  • a neckline: a horizontal level drawn at the swing high between the lows;
  • a close above the neckline, which is the usual definition of completion.

Before that close, the chart shows two lows near support inside a decline that has not yet been broken.

How it forms

In plain words

A ball dropped on a floor bounces, falls again and lands at about the same height. Two landings in the same place suggest there is a floor there. They do not prove that the floor is solid.

The first low is ordinary. The bounce after it is also ordinary, since downtrends are built from lows and weak rallies. The pattern begins at the second low: a continuing downtrend would normally make a lower low, and here the selling stops near the previous one. Sellers were not able, or not willing, to push further.

The bounce high between the lows matters most. It is the last swing high of the decline. A close above it breaks the downtrend’s sequence of lower highs, which is why it serves as the neckline.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Two lows Demand appeared twice in one area How close the lows must be; wick lows or closing lows
Bounce between them Defines the neckline How large the bounce must be to count
Neckline break Completes the pattern A close above, or any trade above; how far above

Volume. Classic texts put more weight on volume at bottoms than at tops. A rise through the neckline on thin participation is described as less convincing than one on clearly higher volume. This is a convention in the literature.

Invalidation. A close below the lower of the two lows is commonly treated as invalidating the reading. The decline has made a new lower low, so the “floor” was not one.

Common false signals. The picture below is the main one.

Others include a neckline break that fails within a few candles, a fakeout to the upside, and a second low that dips under the first on a wick before recovering.

After the break. Price sometimes comes back to the neckline from above. The ready-made figure shows such a retest holding. Retests are not guaranteed, and one that falls back under the neckline weakens the reading.

The measured move. Some textbooks describe projecting the height of the pattern upward from the neckline. This is a convention for describing proportion. It is not a target or an expectation.

Context. A double bottom after a long decline on a higher timeframe, at a level that mattered before, has more around it than two lows on a short intraday chart. Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous: the same two lows can also be the start of a range or the middle of a larger fall.

A hypothetical double bottom, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price falls from 130 to a first low at 102, bounces to 116, and falls again to 101.5.

  • Difference between the lows: 102 minus 101.5 = 0.5, about 0.5% of the price.
  • Neckline: the swing high at 116.
  • Pattern height: 116 minus 101.5 = 14.5.
  • Measured-move convention: 116 plus 14.5 = 130.5.

If a candle closes at 118, above 116, the pattern meets the standard definition. The figure of 130.5 is only what the convention produces. If price instead closes below 101.5, the reading is invalidated and the chart shows a continuing downtrend.

What it does NOT mean

  • Two similar lows are not a double bottom. The standard definition needs a close above the neckline.
  • A completed double bottom does not mean the decline is over. Breaks above the neckline regularly fail.
  • The bullish label is a convention. Apparent double bottoms often resolve downward, especially inside strong downtrends.
  • The second low is not a floor. It is a reference point for invalidation, not a price that cannot be crossed.

Common mistakes

  1. Calling the bottom at the second low. At that point the downtrend is still intact by every structural measure.
  2. Ignoring the larger trend. A small W shape is weighed against a decline built from many more candles.
  3. Treating a wick above the neckline as completion. Most definitions use the close.
  4. Requiring perfect symmetry. Real lows differ in depth, width and shape.

Risks and limitations

  • The thresholds for “similar” lows and a “clear” bounce are matters of judgement.
  • Confirmation arrives only after price has already risen from the lows to the neckline.
  • Triple bottoms, rectangles and downtrend pauses all start with the same two lows.

Key takeaway

A double bottom is two failed attempts at a similar low, completed only by a close above the high between them. It describes a decline that stopped making lower lows, which is not the same as a decline that has ended.
