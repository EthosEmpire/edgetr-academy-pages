Rounded Top & Rounded Bottom

A rounded top or bottom is a slow, curved change of direction with no sharp turning point. It describes a trend that fades gradually, and it is usually clear only after it has finished.

Why it matters

Most reversal patterns are built from distinct peaks and troughs. The rounded top and rounded bottom are different. There is no single moment where the market turns, only a slow loss of momentum in one direction and a slow build in the other.

That makes them a useful study in what patterns cannot do. A shape with no sharp swing points has no obvious boundary, no obvious moment of completion, and is recognised almost entirely in hindsight.

What they look like

Conventional recognition rules for a rounded bottom:

  • a prior decline;
  • falls that become progressively smaller, until price moves roughly sideways;
  • a flat base of small candles;
  • gains that become progressively larger, tracing the right side of a bowl;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close above the level where the curve began, often called the rim.

A rounded top is the mirror image: a prior advance, a rise that slows into a flat crest, a decline that gathers pace, and completion on a close below the level where the dome began.

How it forms

In plain words

A large ship does not turn on the spot. It slows, drifts through a wide arc, and gradually picks up speed in the new direction. At the middle of the arc, an observer cannot say whether it is turning or just slowing down.

In a rounded bottom, selling pressure does not end with a dramatic final low. It thins out. Fewer participants are willing to sell at lower prices, the candles shrink, and volatility contracts. For a while nothing happens. Then buyers begin paying slightly more, and the candles grow again in the other direction.

How to read them

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Curve Momentum fading, then rebuilding Whether the curve is smooth enough to count
Flat middle Balance between buyers and sellers When it starts and ends
Rim The reference for completion Which price marks the start of the curve

Volume. For rounded bottoms, classic texts describe volume tracing a bowl of its own: shrinking as the decline slows, lowest in the flat base, and expanding as the rise builds. For rounded tops, the literature describes volume as much less regular. Neither description is a rule.

Invalidation. For a rounded bottom, a close below the lowest point of the bowl is commonly treated as invalidation, since the decline has resumed. For a rounded top, it is a close above the highest point of the dome.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The flat middle is the problem. A market going quiet after a decline may be forming a bowl, pausing before another fall, or settling into a long range. All three look identical while they are happening. The right half of a bowl can also stall partway up and turn into a lower high within a continuing downtrend.

Relation to other patterns. A rounded bottom followed by a small pullback near the rim is the shape called a cup and handle.

Timeframe and scale. Textbook examples are mostly long formations on daily or weekly charts. On a logarithmic or linear price scale the same data can look more or less curved, so the shape partly depends on chart settings.

The measured move. Some textbooks describe projecting the depth of the bowl, or the height of the dome, from the rim. This is a convention for describing proportion. It is not a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous, and rounded turns are among the most ambiguous of all, because the defining feature is the absence of clear turning points.

A hypothetical rounded bottom in numbersHypothetical illustration

Weekly closes run 130, 118, 108, 102, 100, 102, 108, 118, 130.

  • Weekly changes on the way down: 12, 10, 6 and 2, a total fall of 30.
  • Weekly changes on the way up: 2, 6, 10 and 12, a total rise of 30.
  • Rim: 130. Lowest close: 100. Depth: 130 minus 100 = 30.
  • Measured-move convention: 130 plus 30 = 160.

At the week that closed at 100, a reader had only a slowing decline to look at. The bowl existed only once the right side had formed. The figure of 160 is the output of a convention and nothing more, and a close below 100 would invalidate the reading.

What it does NOT mean

  • A slowing decline is not a bottom. Markets regularly go quiet and then continue in the same direction.
  • A rounded shape does not make a turn more durable. Gradual turns reverse like any other.
  • The measured move is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Naming it at the midpoint. Half a bowl is a decline that has slowed.
  2. Fitting the curve to selected candles. Ignoring the points that do not fit creates the pattern.
  3. Looking for it on short timeframes. Quiet intraday periods produce curves that reflect inactivity, not a change in behaviour.

Risks and limitations

  • There is no objective test for “rounded”, so identification relies on the eye.
  • Completion is defined late, after price has crossed the whole formation.

Key takeaway

A rounded top or bottom is a trend that fades and reverses gradually, without a sharp turning point. It is a description best made in hindsight, and during the flat middle it cannot be told apart from a pause.
