Cup and Handle

A cup and handle is a rounded dip and recovery to a prior high, followed by a smaller pullback. It is conventionally read as a pause within an advance, completed if price closes above the rim.

Why it matters

The cup and handle is a newer addition to the pattern catalogue than most on this shelf. The name is usually credited to the American investor William O’Neil, who described it on stock charts. It combines two ideas covered elsewhere: a rounded bottom and a small pullback that resembles a flag.

It is a helpful pattern to study because its story is easy to state in plain behaviour. Price returns to an old high, hesitates there, and either gets through or does not. Stripped of the name, it is a second test of resistance after a slow recovery.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a prior advance that ends at a high, the left rim;
  • a cup: a decline and recovery that is rounded, shaped like a U more than a V;
  • a right rim at roughly the same height as the left;
  • a handle: a smaller pullback or sideways drift, usually described as staying in the upper part of the cup;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close above the rim.

How it forms

In plain words

Imagine someone who failed a jump, stepped well back, and walked slowly up to the same bar again. Just before it they pause and shuffle back half a step. That pause is the handle. It shows hesitation at the bar. It does not show whether the next jump clears it.

The cup begins as an ordinary correction. Selling fades gradually near the bottom, and buying builds gradually on the way back. When price reaches the old high, it meets participants who bought there earlier and have waited to get out without a loss. That supply produces the handle. The conventional reading is that a shallow, quiet handle shows the selling being absorbed.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Cup shape Gradual turn, not a sharp spike How rounded is rounded enough
Rims At similar heights How much difference is allowed
Handle depth Small relative to the cup Where a handle becomes a new decline
Exit A close above the rim The rim itself, or the handle’s high; wick or close

Volume. The usual description has volume fading into the bottom of the cup, recovering on the right side, shrinking again in the handle, and expanding on a close above the rim. This comes from stock-market descriptions and is a tendency at best.

Invalidation. A close below the low of the handle is the usual first reference: the hesitation has turned into a decline. A close below the low of the cup invalidates the reading entirely.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The first is the same picture with a different ending. Two highs at a similar level with a dip between them is also the description of a double top. Whether the chart is called a cup and handle or a double top is decided by the exit, after the fact.

The second is the false breakout. The rim is an obvious level, and a close above it that falls back within a few candles is a common fakeout.

The third is the deep handle. A handle that retraces a large part of the cup is no longer a brief hesitation, and many descriptions stop using the name.

Context. The pattern was described on daily and weekly stock charts, inside established advances. Small cup shapes on short intraday charts, or with no prior advance, are a looser use of the name.

The measured move. Textbooks describe a convention in which the depth of the cup is projected upward from the rim. This is a way of describing proportion. It is not a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous, and here the name itself depends on the exit.

A hypothetical cup and handle, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price rises to 124, declines gradually to 102, recovers gradually to 124, and then drifts down to 119.

  • Cup depth: 124 minus 102 = 22.
  • Handle depth: 124 minus 119 = 5.
  • Handle as a share of the cup: 5 divided by 22 = about 23%.
  • Measured-move convention: 124 plus 22 = 146.

A close at 126 would complete the pattern by the usual definition, and 146 is only the output of the convention. A close below 119 would weaken the reading. A close below 102 would invalidate it, and the chart would then show two failed highs at 124.

What it does NOT mean

  • A cup and handle does not mean the rim gives way. The rim has stopped price at least once already.
  • The bullish label is a convention. The same shape regularly resolves as a double top.
  • The measured move is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Finding cups in every dip. The pattern needs a prior advance, a gradual turn and a clear rim.
  2. Ignoring handle depth. A handle that falls deep into the cup has changed the picture.
  3. Treating the rim as an exact price. The two rims rarely match, and resistance is an area.

Risks and limitations

  • “Rounded”, “shallow” and “short” have no agreed thresholds.
  • It was described for one market and era, and may not transfer cleanly to others.

Key takeaway

A cup and handle is a slow recovery to a prior high followed by a brief hesitation. It describes a second test of resistance, and the same shape with a different exit is called a double top.
