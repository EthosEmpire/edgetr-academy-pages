How to Read Chart Patterns

A chart pattern is a named summary of how price behaved across several swing points. Patterns describe structure; they are subjective, probabilistic and much clearer in hindsight.

Why it matters

Chart patterns are among the most copied drawings in trading education. A double top, a triangle or a flag is easy to recognise in a textbook, and that ease is misleading. The examples were picked after the outcome was known.

This page is the starting point for the shelf. It explains what a pattern is made of, how the patterns are grouped, and which questions apply to every one of them.

What a pattern really is

Price moves in swings. Each turning point is a swing high or swing low, a place where buying or selling pressure ran out for a while. A chart pattern is a short label for a recurring arrangement of those points.

Two highs at a similar price is a “double top”. Falling highs above rising lows is a “triangle”. The name saves words. It does not add information that the swing points did not already contain.

In plain words

Think of constellations. The stars are real, and the lines between them are drawn by people. Two observers can connect the same stars into different figures.

Behind each arrangement is repeated behaviour. Sellers appearing twice near the same price leave two similar highs. Buyers paying a little more on each dip leave rising lows. Reading a pattern means describing that behaviour, then admitting that it may not repeat.

The pattern families

Family Pages on this shelf
Reversal Double top, double bottom, triple top and bottom, head and shoulders, inverse head and shoulders, rounded top and bottom
Continuation Bull flag, bear flag, pennant, cup and handle
Triangles Ascending, descending, symmetrical
Wedges and channels Rising wedge, falling wedge, channels
Range, compression, expansion Rectangle, broadening formation

The grouping follows structure and context, not outcome. “Reversal” means the pattern forms after a trend and, if it completes, the prior trend has changed. “Continuation” means it forms as a pause. Both labels are applied with confidence only afterwards, which is why pattern failure and invalidation has its own page.

How to read any pattern

The same questions apply to every pattern on this shelf.

Question What it checks
What came before? A reversal pattern needs a prior trend to reverse. A continuation pattern needs a move to continue.
Where are the boundaries? The swing points that define the shape, and the line or zone they form.
What would complete it? Usually a close beyond a boundary, such as a neckline or a trend line.
What would invalidate it? The price action that would mean the description no longer fits.
What does volume show? Whether participation shrank during the pattern and grew on the exit.

Going deeper

Subjectivity. Boundaries can be drawn through wicks or through closes, and the two give different lines. “Similar highs” has no agreed tolerance. Two careful readers can label the same swings as a triangle and a wedge.

Timeframe. A pattern on a weekly chart summarises months of trading. The same shape on a one-minute chart summarises very little and is easily produced by noise. A pattern on one timeframe is often a single swing on a higher one, which is why multi-timeframe structure matters.

Volume. Classic texts describe volume fading while a pattern forms and rising when price leaves it. That is a tendency in the descriptions, not a rule.

The measured move. Many textbooks describe a convention: measure the height of the pattern and project that distance from the point where price leaves it. This is a way of describing proportion that some authors use. It is not a target, and nothing obliges price to travel that far, or at all.

Hindsight. Finished charts make patterns look tidy.

One set of swings, three labelsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market rises from 100 to 120, falls to 112, rises to 121, and falls to 113.

  • Reader A sees two highs at 120 and 121 and calls it a possible double top.
  • Reader B sees lows rising from 112 to 113 under a flat ceiling and calls it a possible ascending triangle.
  • Reader C sees a sideways band between roughly 112 and 121 and calls it a range.

All three descriptions fit the four swing points. None is wrong yet, and none can be confirmed until price leaves the area. Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous for exactly this reason.

What it does NOT mean

  • A pattern is not a forecast. It describes swings that have already formed. What follows is uncertain every time.
  • A family name is not a promise. Reversal patterns often resolve as continuations, and continuation patterns often reverse.
  • Spotting a pattern is not a reason to act. It is one observation among several, and this site does not give trading advice.

Common mistakes

  1. Seeing patterns everywhere. With enough lines, any chart can be made to fit a shape. The mind is very good at finding figures in noise.
  2. Skipping invalidation. A reading with no condition that would prove it wrong cannot be tested.
  3. Looking only for agreement. Noticing the shapes that fit an existing opinion is a form of confirmation bias.

Risks and limitations

  • Pattern definitions differ between authors, so the same name does not always describe the same shape.
  • Evidence on chart patterns is limited and mixed, and results depend heavily on how each pattern is defined.

Key takeaway

A chart pattern is a name for how price behaved at a handful of swing points. It is a description that helps organise a chart, with a stated condition that would prove it wrong, and never a prediction.
