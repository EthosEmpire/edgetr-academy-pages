Triple Top & Triple Bottom

A triple top is three peaks at a similar height; a triple bottom is three lows at a similar depth. Each is conventionally read as a possible reversal once the opposite side of the range breaks.

Why it matters

Triple tops and bottoms extend the idea behind the double top and double bottom: an area was tested three times and held each time.

They teach two things. A level that has been tested repeatedly attracts attention, and a market that keeps returning to a level is, structurally, a range. The reversal name is attached only after price leaves that range in one particular direction.

What they look like

Conventional recognition rules for a triple top:

  • a prior advance;
  • three peaks at a similar height, with two pullbacks between them;
  • a neckline at the lows of those pullbacks;
  • a close below the neckline. Standard references treat the pattern as complete only when both intervening lows have been broken.

A triple bottom is the mirror image: a prior decline, three lows at a similar depth, a neckline at the highs of the two bounces, and a close above it.

How it forms

In plain words

Think of waves reaching the same line on a beach three times. The line tells you how far the water has been coming. It does not tell you whether the tide is about to turn or rise further.

After the second test, the chart looks like a double top or double bottom that did not complete. Price returns to the middle, then tests the boundary a third time. Each failed test shows that one side could not extend the trend and the other could not reverse it.

Classic texts describe triple tops and bottoms as relatively uncommon. One standard reference treats the triple top as a variation of the head and shoulders in which the three peaks happen to be level.

How to read them

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Three tests Supply or demand appeared repeatedly in one area How close the three extremes must be
Two reactions between them Define the neckline Which reaction to use when their depths differ
Neckline break Completes the pattern A close beyond it, or any trade beyond it

Volume. The classic description has volume shrinking on each successive peak of a triple top and expanding when the neckline breaks. Real charts follow this loosely at best.

Invalidation. For a triple top, a close above the highest of the three peaks is commonly treated as invalidation. For a triple bottom, it is a close below the lowest of the three lows. In both cases the “failed” side has succeeded.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The first is the look-alike shown below. Three tests of a ceiling followed by an upward exit is a rectangle that resolved as a continuation.

The second is the fourth test. Nothing stops price from visiting a level again, and a pattern that keeps adding peaks is simply a range. The third is the false break of the neckline, a fakeout that returns inside the range within a few candles.

Repeated tests. One view holds that each rejection confirms a level. Another holds that each test uses up the orders resting there, so the level is closer to giving way. The pattern cannot settle the question.

The measured move. Some textbooks describe projecting the height of the pattern from the neckline, in the direction of the break. This is a convention for describing proportion, never a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous.

A hypothetical triple top, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price rises from 100 and peaks at 126, 126.5 and 126, with pullbacks to 114 after the first and second peaks.

  • Spread of the three peaks: 126.5 minus 126 = 0.5.
  • Neckline: 114, the low of both pullbacks.
  • Pattern height: 126.5 minus 114 = 12.5.
  • Measured-move convention: 114 minus 12.5 = 101.5.

A close at 112 would meet the standard definition of completion. A close at 128, above 126.5, would invalidate the reading, and the chart would then show a range that resolved upward. The mirror case, a triple bottom with lows near 102 and a neckline at 114, works the same way in the other direction.

What it does NOT mean

  • Three touches do not make a wall. Levels that held three times break on the fourth test regularly.
  • A triple top is not bearish until it completes. Before the neckline breaks, it is a range inside an uptrend. The same applies in reverse to a triple bottom.
  • The measured move is not a target. It is a textbook way of describing size.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting loosely. Small wiggles near a high are not separate peaks. Each test needs a clear reaction between it and the next.
  2. Renaming after the fact. Calling it a triple top when it falls and a rectangle when it rises is hindsight, not reading.
  3. Assuming the third test is the last. The pattern has no built-in limit on tests.
  4. Drawing the neckline to fit the outcome. The line should be chosen before price reaches it.

Risks and limitations

  • The pattern takes a long time to form, and most of that time it is indistinguishable from a range.
  • Confirmation comes late: by the time the neckline breaks, price has already crossed the whole pattern.

Key takeaway

A triple top or bottom is a range with three tests of one side, named a reversal only if the other side breaks. It shows where a market stalled, and leaves open which way it leaves.
