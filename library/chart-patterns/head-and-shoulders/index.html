Head and Shoulders

A head and shoulders is three peaks after an advance, with the middle one highest, and a neckline under the two lows between them. It is conventionally read as a possible reversal if the neckline breaks.

Why it matters

The head and shoulders is probably the best-known pattern in classical charting. Its fame comes from the shape, but its teaching value comes from the structure underneath: it is a picture of an uptrend losing its rhythm one step at a time.

Seen that way, it is less a special figure and more a specific case of a trend transition.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a prior advance;
  • a left shoulder: a peak followed by a pullback;
  • a head: a higher peak, followed by a pullback to roughly the same area as the first;
  • a right shoulder: a third peak that is lower than the head;
  • a neckline connecting the two pullback lows;
  • a close below the neckline, which most definitions require before the pattern counts as complete.

How it forms

In plain words

Picture someone climbing stairs who takes a big step up, slips back down to the previous step, and then manages only a small step. Nothing has gone wrong yet, but the climb no longer looks the way it did.

Two things separate this from a healthy uptrend. First, the pullback after the head falls all the way to the previous low, so the trend stops making higher lows. Second, the next rally stalls below the head, which is a lower high. A close under the neckline then adds a lower low, the final ingredient of a break of structure.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Shoulders Roughly similar in height and width How symmetrical they need to be
Head Clearly the highest peak How much higher counts as “clearly”
Neckline Drawn through the two lows; it can slope up or down Wick lows or closing lows; how to treat a steep slope
Break A close below the neckline How far below, and on which timeframe

Volume. The classic description has volume heaviest on the left shoulder, lighter on the head, lightest on the right shoulder, and expanding as the neckline breaks. This is a tendency reported in the standard texts, not a requirement.

Invalidation. Practitioners commonly use two references. A close above the right shoulder weakens the reading, because the lower high has been exceeded. A close above the head invalidates it, because the uptrend has made a new higher high. After a neckline break, a close back above the neckline is treated as a serious warning that the break failed.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The most common is the incomplete pattern.

A second is the false break: price closes under the neckline, then recovers above it, a downward fakeout. A third is the forced fit: any three peaks with a taller one in the middle can be given the label.

The return move. After a break, price sometimes rallies back to the neckline from below, a kind of retest. It does not always occur.

The measured move. Many textbooks describe a convention: measure the vertical distance from the head to the neckline and project it downward from the point of the break. This is a description of proportion that the literature uses. It is not a target or an expectation.

Context. The pattern is conventionally a top, so it needs something to top. Size matters as well: a formation that took months to build summarises far more trading than one that took an afternoon. Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous.

A hypothetical head and shoulders, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price rises from 100 to a left shoulder at 120, pulls back to 110, rises to a head at 132, pulls back to 110, and rallies to a right shoulder at 120.

  • Neckline: 110, through both pullback lows.
  • Head above the shoulders: 132 minus 120 = 12.
  • Pattern height: 132 minus 110 = 22.
  • Measured-move convention: 110 minus 22 = 88.

A close at 108 would meet the standard definition of completion. The figure of 88 is only what the convention produces. A close above 120 would weaken the reading, and a close above 132 would invalidate it.

What it does NOT mean

  • Three peaks are not a head and shoulders. Without a close below the neckline, the pattern is incomplete by most definitions.
  • A completed pattern does not mean a downtrend has begun. Neckline breaks regularly reverse.
  • The bearish label is a convention. The shape often resolves upward, as in the failed example above.

Common mistakes

  1. Naming it at the right shoulder. A lower high inside an uptrend is common and is often followed by a new high.
  2. Forcing symmetry. Real shoulders are uneven, and waiting for a perfect figure means fitting the chart to the drawing.
  3. Redrawing the neckline until it breaks. The line should be fixed by the two lows before price reaches it.
  4. Ignoring the prior trend. A head and shoulders inside a sideways range is three swings in a range.

Risks and limitations

  • Nearly every element involves judgement, so automatic detectors and human readers frequently disagree.
  • Completion comes only after price has already fallen from the head to the neckline.

Key takeaway

A head and shoulders is an uptrend that fails to hold a higher low, makes a lower high, and then closes below its neckline. Each step is evidence of weakening, and each can still be reversed by the next swing.
