Symmetrical Triangle

A symmetrical triangle is a series of lower highs and higher lows that converge toward a point. It shows a market in shrinking balance and has no built-in direction.

Why it matters

The symmetrical triangle is the plainest picture of a market making up its mind. Buyers are no longer willing to pay as much as they did on the last rally. Sellers are no longer willing to accept as little as they did on the last dip. The range of prices both sides accept is shrinking.

Because neither boundary is flat, neither side is visibly “holding”. That makes the pattern neutral in a way the ascending and descending triangles are not.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • at least two highs, each lower than the last;
  • at least two lows, each higher than the last, so there are four turning points in all;
  • an upper line falling and a lower line rising at roughly similar angles;
  • price swinging between the two lines as they converge;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close outside either line.

Older texts also call this shape a coil.

How it forms

In plain words

Two people haggling over a price start far apart. The seller comes down a little, the buyer goes up a little, and the gap narrows with each exchange. You can see that a decision is getting close. You cannot tell from the narrowing gap who gives in, or whether one of them walks away.

Each swing inside the triangle is smaller than the one before it, which means volatility is falling. The pattern ends when price leaves the triangle, either because one side finally pushes or simply because the lines run out.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Lower highs Buyers paying less on each rally Which highs the line goes through
Higher lows Sellers accepting less on each dip Which lows the line goes through
Exit A close outside a line Wick or close; how far outside

Volume. Classic texts describe volume shrinking steadily as the triangle narrows and rising when price leaves it. This is a tendency in the descriptions, not a requirement.

Invalidation. The neutral description, “price is compressing between these two lines”, stops applying when price leaves the triangle. A directional reading made after an exit is commonly treated as invalidated if price closes back inside the triangle, and more firmly if it closes beyond the opposite line.

Going deeper

Continuation by convention. Textbooks describe the symmetrical triangle as usually a continuation pattern: a pause after which the earlier trend resumes. They also accept that it marks reversals. In practice this means the prior trend is context to note, not a rule about the exit.

Common false signals. The triangle’s narrow end produces most of them. Close to the point where the lines meet, the boundaries are only a short distance apart, and ordinary noise crosses them. An exit near the tip, followed by a move back through the triangle and out of the other side, is a common fakeout. Classic texts treat a drift all the way into the tip as the pattern simply expiring.

A second false signal comes from the drawing. With only four turning points, a single wick can change a symmetrical triangle into an ascending or descending one, or into a wedge.

Compression and expansion. The shape is one form of the general tendency for quiet periods to be followed by more active ones.

The opposite shape, with swings that grow instead of shrink, is the broadening formation. A small, brief triangle after a sharp move is usually called a pennant.

The measured move. Textbooks describe measuring the height of the triangle at its widest point and projecting that distance from the place where price leaves it. This is a convention for describing proportion. It is not a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous, and here the ambiguity is part of the definition.

A hypothetical symmetrical triangle, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price swings between highs of 128, 124 and 120 and lows of 104, 108 and 112.

  • First swing range: 128 minus 104 = 24.
  • Second swing range: 124 minus 108 = 16.
  • Third swing range: 120 minus 112 = 8.
  • Height at the widest point: 24.
  • Measured-move convention for an exit upward at 119: 119 plus 24 = 143.
  • Measured-move convention for an exit downward at 113: 113 minus 24 = 89.

The ranges shrink from 24 to 16 to 8. Both projected figures come from the same triangle, which shows how little the shape says about direction. Neither number is a forecast.

What it does NOT mean

  • A symmetrical triangle does not favour either direction. Its two sides are, by definition, giving ground at a similar rate.
  • The first close outside is not the answer. Returns into the triangle are common, especially near the tip.
  • The measured move is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Drawing it from three points. Two lines need at least two points each.
  2. Choosing a direction in advance. The pattern gives no basis for one.
  3. Moving the lines to keep price inside. A triangle that is redrawn after every candle never ends.

Risks and limitations

  • Line placement depends on which wicks are used, and small changes alter the pattern’s name and its exit point.
  • The pattern gives no information about timing: compression can persist.

Key takeaway

A symmetrical triangle shows buyers and sellers converging on a narrower and narrower band of prices. It is a clear description of compression, and a clear example of a pattern that describes the present without indicating the outcome.
