Falling Wedge

A falling wedge is price declining between two downward-sloping lines that converge. Each new low gains less ground than the last, which is conventionally read as fading selling pressure.

Why it matters

The falling wedge is the mirror image of the rising wedge. Price is going down, and the conventional reading is bullish. The idea is that a decline in which each new low extends the previous one by less is a decline that is running out of sellers.

It deserves caution, because two converging lines are easy to draw on a downtrend that is merely slowing before its next leg.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a lower line that slopes down, through at least two lows;
  • an upper line that also slopes down, more steeply, through at least two highs;
  • the two lines converging, so the swings between them get smaller;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close above the upper line.

How it forms

In plain words

Picture a car rolling down a hill that gradually levels out. It is still moving forward and still losing height, but it covers less ground every second. Slowing down is not the same as stopping, and it is certainly not the same as turning around.

Inside the wedge, sellers still appear on every rally, and at lower prices each time, which is why the upper line falls steeply. Yet each push down from those rallies extends the previous low by less. Sellers are working harder for smaller results. The conventional interpretation is that supply is thinning at lower prices.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Falling lows The decline continues Which lows the line goes through
Steeper falling highs Rallies are sold quickly Which highs the line goes through
Convergence Each push gains less ground Whether the lines really converge
Exit A close above the upper line Wick or close; how far above

Volume. The classic description has volume shrinking as the wedge develops and rising on an upward exit. Momentum tools sometimes show bullish divergence over the same stretch: price makes a lower low while the indicator does not. Neither is part of the pattern’s definition.

Invalidation. A sustained close below the lower line is the usual reference. The shrinking losses that defined the wedge have been replaced by acceleration downward, and the description no longer fits.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The first is the wedge that becomes a channel. Price pokes above the upper line, stalls, and resumes falling at a shallower angle. The downtrend slowed without ending.

The second is the false exit upward: a close above the upper line that fails within a few candles, a fakeout. The upper line is steep and close to price, so it is crossed easily. A cross of a falling line is also a weaker event than a higher high. Until price exceeds a previous swing high, the downtrend’s structure of lower highs is still in place.

The third is the look-alike below.

As a pullback. A falling wedge inside an uptrend overlaps with the bull flag, which is briefer and has parallel lines.

The measured move. Sources differ. Some describe the start of the wedge as the reference level after an upward exit. Others project the height of the wedge’s wide end from the exit point. Both are conventions for describing proportion. Neither is a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous. A falling wedge and a downtrend taking a breath look the same from the inside.

A hypothetical falling wedge, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price makes lows at 120, 114, 110 and 107.5, with highs between them at 127, 119.5 and 114.

  • Losses from low to low: 120 minus 114 = 6, 114 minus 110 = 4, 110 minus 107.5 = 2.5.
  • Losses from high to high: 127 minus 119.5 = 7.5, 119.5 minus 114 = 5.5.
  • Width of the swings: 127 minus 120 = 7, 119.5 minus 114 = 5.5, 114 minus 110 = 4.

The highs fall faster than the lows, and the swings narrow from 7 to 5.5 to 4. That is the wedge. A close above the upper line would complete the pattern by the usual definition. A close above 114, the last swing high, would add a break of the lower-high sequence. A sustained close below the lower line would invalidate the reading.

What it does NOT mean

  • A falling wedge does not mean a bottom is in. Slowing downtrends frequently continue.
  • The bullish label is a convention. Wedges exit downward regularly, sometimes with a sharp acceleration.
  • Crossing the upper line is not a trend change. The structure of lower highs may still be intact.
  • Any projected level is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Drawing a wedge under every decline. With selective use of wicks, most downtrends can be made to converge.
  2. Confusing it with a descending triangle. A flat bottom and a falling bottom carry opposite conventional readings.
  3. Treating the shape as the outcome. Until price leaves the wedge, the decline is intact.
  4. Ignoring the setting. A wedge against a large downtrend and a wedge inside an uptrend are not the same situation.

Risks and limitations

  • The pattern rests on small differences in slope, which depend on subjective line placement.
  • Its conventional reading runs against the current direction of price, where being early is the same as being wrong.
  • Wedges can narrow for a long time without resolving.

Key takeaway

A falling wedge is a decline in which each low adds less than the one before. It describes selling pressure that is fading, which can precede a reversal, a pause, or a renewed fall.
