Channels

A channel is a trend contained between two roughly parallel lines: a trend line on one side and a parallel channel line on the other. It describes the rhythm of a trend, not its future.

Why it matters

A trend line shows one side of a trend: the rising lows of an advance or the falling highs of a decline. A channel adds the other side, so the chart shows both the slope of the trend and the typical size of its swings.

Channels are less a “pattern” with an outcome and more a way of framing a trend. When price stops behaving the way the channel describes, something about the trend is different.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a trend line through at least two swing points on the trend’s side: lows in an uptrend, highs in a downtrend;
  • a channel line, also called a return line, drawn parallel to it through the opposite swing points;
  • price swinging between the two lines with at least one later touch that respects the drawing.

An ascending channel frames an uptrend. A descending channel frames a downtrend. A horizontal channel is a rectangle.

How it forms

In plain words

Imagine a river running between two banks. The banks show which way the river is heading and how wide it usually is. They are a record of where the water has been, and after heavy rain the river can leave them.

A channel appears when a trend has a steady rhythm. In an advance, each rally extends about as far beyond the last high as the previous rally did, and each pullback retraces a similar amount. The parallel lines are a summary of that repetition.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Trend line The side that defines the trend Which swing points to use; wicks or closes
Channel line The usual extent of swings in the trend’s direction Often fitted loosely; carries less weight
Width The typical swing size Changes with how the lines are placed

What a miss can show. Texts describe a rally in an ascending channel that fails to reach the channel line as an early hint that the trend is losing pace. A move through the channel line is described as acceleration. A close through the trend line is described as a change in the trend’s rhythm. All three are observations about the past few swings.

Volume. Channels have no distinctive volume signature in the classic texts. The general idea applies: in an advance, volume that grows on rallies and shrinks on pullbacks agrees with the trend.

Invalidation. The description “price is trending inside this channel” is commonly treated as invalidated by a close outside either line that is not quickly reversed. A close beyond the trend line questions the trend’s pace. Price can then continue at a shallower slope, move sideways, or turn.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The first is the channel drawn from three points. Two lows and one high always produce a pair of parallel lines, so the drawing proves nothing until later swings test it.

The second is the brief exit. Price closes outside a line and returns within a few candles, a fakeout.

Chart settings. A channel that looks parallel on a linear scale can look curved on a logarithmic scale, and the reverse. The practical steps are covered in drawing trend lines.

The measured move. Some textbooks describe a convention in which, after price leaves a channel, the width of the channel is projected from the line that was crossed. This describes proportion. It is not a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous. Two readers will often draw two different channels on the same trend.

A hypothetical ascending channel in numbersHypothetical illustration

A trend line is drawn through a low at 105 and a later low at 113, sixteen candles apart.

  • Slope of the trend line: 113 minus 105 = 8, divided by 16 candles = 0.5 per candle.
  • A high at 120 occurs nine candles after the first low. The trend line is then at 105 plus 9 times 0.5 = 109.5.
  • Channel width: 120 minus 109.5 = 10.5.
  • Ten candles after the second low, the trend line is at 113 plus 10 times 0.5 = 118, and the channel line is at 118 plus 10.5 = 128.5.

The figures 118 and 128.5 are where the drawn lines sit at that moment. They are an extension of past rhythm, and price is under no obligation to respect either.

What it does NOT mean

  • A channel does not hold price in. The lines record past swings and have no force of their own.
  • A touch of a line is not an instruction. It is the point where the drawing gets tested.
  • A close outside is not a reversal. Trends change pace and carry on in the same direction.

Common mistakes

  1. Trusting a channel with no later touches. Three points define it. Only further swings test it.
  2. Forcing the lines to be parallel. If the swings are narrowing, the shape may be a wedge, which texts read differently.
  3. Extending a steep channel far ahead. Fast trends usually change pace.

Risks and limitations

  • Line placement is subjective, and small changes move both boundaries.
  • Channels describe a rhythm that is only visible after several swings, so they are always drawn late.

Key takeaway

A channel frames a trend between a trend line and a parallel channel line, showing its slope and typical swing size. Its value is in showing when price stops following that rhythm, and it does not say what follows.
