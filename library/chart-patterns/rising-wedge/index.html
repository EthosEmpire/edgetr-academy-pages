Rising Wedge

A rising wedge is price climbing between two upward-sloping lines that converge. Each new high gains less ground than the last, which is conventionally read as weakening momentum.

Why it matters

A rising wedge is unusual among the patterns on this shelf: price is going up, and the conventional reading is bearish. That makes it a useful lesson in the difference between direction and momentum. A market can keep making higher highs while each one is less convincing than the last.

It is also one of the most over-drawn patterns. Two converging trend lines can be fitted to many uptrends, and slowing uptrends often simply pause and carry on.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • an upper line that slopes up, through at least two highs;
  • a lower line that also slopes up, more steeply, through at least two lows;
  • the two lines converging, so the swings between them get smaller;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close below the lower line.

How it forms

In plain words

Think of a ball thrown up a slope. It keeps moving upward, but each bounce carries it a shorter distance than the one before. It is still gaining height. It is also clearly running out of energy.

Inside the wedge, buyers still step in on every dip, and they do so at higher prices each time, which is why the lower line rises steeply. Yet each rally from those dips extends the previous high by less. Buyers are working harder for smaller gains. The conventional interpretation is that demand is thinning at higher prices.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Rising highs The advance continues Which highs the line goes through
Steeper rising lows Dips are bought quickly Which lows the line goes through
Convergence Each push gains less ground Whether the lines really converge
Exit A close below the lower line Wick or close; how far below

Two contexts. Texts describe the rising wedge in two settings. At the end of an advance it is read as a possible reversal. Inside a downtrend, as a slow upward drift after a fall, it is read as a continuation of the decline, much like a large bear flag. The conventional bias is bearish in both, and both readings fail often.

Volume. The classic description has volume shrinking as the wedge develops, which fits the idea of fading participation. Momentum tools sometimes show the same thing as divergence: price makes a higher high while the indicator does not. Neither is a requirement of the pattern.

Invalidation. A sustained close above the upper line is the usual reference. The shrinking gains that defined the wedge have been replaced by acceleration, so the description no longer fits.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The first is the wedge that turns into a channel. Price dips under the lower line, holds, and continues upward at a shallower angle. The uptrend was slowing, not ending.

The second is the brief overshoot. Price pushes above the upper line for a candle or two and then falls back inside. Some readers treat that as a failed breakout, others as the pattern being invalidated.

The third is the false exit downward: a close below the lower line that recovers within a few candles, a fakeout. Because the lower line is steep, it sits close to price and is crossed easily.

The measured move. Sources differ. Some describe the start of the wedge as the reference level after a downward exit. Others project the height of the wedge’s wide end from the exit point. Both are conventions for describing proportion. Neither is a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous. A rising wedge and a healthy uptrend that is briefly slowing look the same from the inside.

A hypothetical rising wedge, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price makes highs at 114, 120, 124 and 126.5, with lows between them at 107, 114.5 and 120.

  • Gains from high to high: 120 minus 114 = 6, 124 minus 120 = 4, 126.5 minus 124 = 2.5.
  • Gains from low to low: 114.5 minus 107 = 7.5, 120 minus 114.5 = 5.5.
  • Width of the swings: 114 minus 107 = 7, 120 minus 114.5 = 5.5, 124 minus 120 = 4.

The lows rise faster than the highs, and the swings narrow from 7 to 5.5 to 4. That is the wedge. A close below the lower line would complete the pattern by the usual definition. A sustained close above the upper line would invalidate the reading.

What it does NOT mean

  • A rising wedge does not mean a fall is coming. Slowing uptrends frequently continue.
  • The bearish label is a convention. Wedges exit upward regularly, sometimes sharply.
  • Any projected level is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Drawing a wedge on every uptrend. Most advances can be squeezed between two converging lines with enough selective drawing.
  2. Confusing it with an ascending triangle. A flat top and a rising top carry opposite conventional readings, and the difference can be one wick.
  3. Reading the shape as the outcome. Until price leaves the wedge, the uptrend is intact.

Risks and limitations

  • The pattern depends on small differences in slope, which depend on subjective line placement.
  • Its conventional reading runs against the current direction of price, where being early is the same as being wrong.

Key takeaway

A rising wedge is an advance in which each high adds less than the one before. It describes fading momentum, which can precede a reversal, a pause, or a fresh acceleration.

Sources and further reading
