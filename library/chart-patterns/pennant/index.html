Pennant

A pennant is a sharp move followed by a brief pause in which highs fall and lows rise into a small triangle. It is conventionally read as a continuation pattern, in either direction.

Why it matters

The pennant is the close relative of the flag. Both describe a market catching its breath after a fast move. Where a flag drifts, a pennant tightens: each small swing covers less ground than the one before.

That tightening is a visible drop in volatility, and it links the pennant to the wider idea of compression. A market that has gone quiet after a burst of activity is balanced for the moment. The pattern describes that balance. It does not say how the balance ends.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a pole: a sharp, nearly straight move, up or down;
  • a short pause in which highs get lower and lows get higher;
  • two converging boundary lines through those highs and lows, forming a small triangle;
  • a pause that is small and brief compared with the pole;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close beyond the boundary in the direction of the pole.

A pennant after a sharp rise is often called a bull pennant, and one after a sharp fall a bear pennant. The structure is identical, which is why this page carries no directional label.

How it forms

In plain words

Think of a pendulum that has just been pushed hard. It swings wide, then less, then less again, until it is almost still. The shrinking swings tell you energy is draining away. They tell you nothing about where the next push comes from.

After the pole, the first reaction is usually the largest: some participants close positions and price snaps back. Each swing after that is smaller. Buyers and sellers are agreeing on a narrower and narrower band of prices. The conventional reading is that neither side has seen a reason to undo the pole, so the earlier pressure may return. It also may not.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Pole A burst of one-sided pressure How sharp it must be
Converging lines Shrinking swings, falling volatility Which wicks the lines go through
Size and duration Small and short Where a pennant becomes a triangle
Exit A close beyond a boundary Wick or close; how far beyond

Volume. Classic texts describe heavy volume on the pole, volume drying up as the pennant narrows, and volume returning on the exit. As with flags, these are tendencies in the descriptions.

Invalidation. A close beyond the boundary on the side opposite the pole is the usual reference. For a pennant after a rise, that is a close below the lower line. Texts also note that a pause which drifts all the way into the tip of the triangle without leaving has lost its shape as a pattern.

Going deeper

Common false signals. Because a pennant is narrow, its boundaries are close together, and small moves cross them easily. A close slightly outside one line, followed by a move back inside or out through the other side, is a routine fakeout.

A second false signal is the pennant without a pole. Small triangles form all the time in quiet markets. Without a sharp move before them, they are ordinary compression, closer to a small symmetrical triangle than to this pattern.

Context. A pennant early in a trend sits inside supporting structure. One that forms into a well-watched level, or after an extended run, has less behind it.

The measured move. Textbooks describe the same convention as for flags: the length of the pole is projected from the point where price leaves the pennant. This describes proportion in the literature. It is not a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous. Readers regularly disagree on whether a given pause is a pennant, a flag with untidy lines, or a small triangle.

A hypothetical pennant, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price rises quickly from 100 to 128, then swings between these points: down to 121, up to 126.5, down to 122, up to 125.5, down to 123.

  • Pole length: 128 minus 100 = 28.
  • First swing range: 128 minus 121 = 7.
  • Second swing range: 126.5 minus 122 = 4.5.
  • Third swing range: 125.5 minus 123 = 2.5.
  • Measured-move convention, if price leaves the pennant at 125: 125 plus 28 = 153.

The ranges shrink from 7 to 4.5 to 2.5, which is the narrowing that defines the shape. A close above the upper line would complete the pattern by the usual definition, and 153 is only the output of the convention. A close below the lower line would invalidate the continuation reading.

What it does NOT mean

  • A pennant does not point in a direction. The triangle is symmetrical. The directional reading comes entirely from the pole.
  • Continuation is a convention. Pennants regularly exit against the pole.
  • The measured move is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Skipping the pole. Without a sharp prior move, the shape is a small triangle with no implied direction.
  2. Reading the first candle outside the lines as the answer. Narrow patterns produce many false exits.
  3. Letting it run on. A “pennant” that lasts as long as the move before it is no longer a brief pause.

Risks and limitations

  • Few candles mean little data, so each one has an outsized effect on the drawing.
  • Sharp poles in thin markets can come from a few large orders, not broad participation.

Key takeaway

A pennant is a sharp move followed by a small, tightening pause. It shows a market that has gone quiet after a burst, and the direction in which it wakes up is not contained in the shape.
