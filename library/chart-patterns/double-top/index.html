Double Top

A double top is two peaks at a similar height, separated by a pullback, that form after an advance. It is conventionally read as a possible reversal if the low between the peaks breaks.

Why it matters

The double top is usually the first reversal pattern people learn, because the idea is simple: buyers tried twice to push through an area and did not manage it either time.

It is also a good first lesson in how patterns mislead. Two similar highs appear constantly in rising markets, and most of them are only pauses. The part that defines the pattern is the low between the peaks, not the peaks themselves.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a prior advance, so there is a trend that could reverse;
  • a first peak, followed by a pullback that leaves a clear swing low;
  • a second peak at a similar height to the first;
  • a neckline: a horizontal level drawn at the swing low between the peaks;
  • a close below the neckline. Standard references treat the pattern as complete only at this point.

Until the neckline breaks, the chart shows two highs near resistance and nothing more.

How it forms

In plain words

Someone tries a locked door, walks away, comes back and tries it again. Two failed attempts tell you the door is holding today. They do not tell you whether a third attempt will open it.

The first peak is ordinary: every uptrend is made of highs followed by pullbacks. The second peak is where the pattern begins. A healthy uptrend would normally make a higher high, and here the retry stops near the old one. Buyers were willing to pay about as much as before, and no more.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Two peaks Supply appeared twice in one area How close “similar” must be; wick highs or closing highs
Pullback between them Defines the neckline How deep the dip must be to count
Time between peaks More time is often treated as more meaningful No agreed minimum
Neckline break Completes the pattern A close below, or any trade below; how far below

Volume. Classic descriptions have lighter volume on the second peak than on the first, and heavier volume as the neckline gives way. Real charts often show neither.

Invalidation. A close above the higher of the two peaks is commonly treated as invalidating the reading: the “failed” high has been exceeded and the uptrend’s sequence of higher highs is intact.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The most frequent one is simply an incomplete pattern, shown below: two peaks, a neckline that holds, and a new high.

A second is the false break. Price closes under the neckline, then recovers above it within a few candles, which is a fakeout in the downward direction.

After the break. Price sometimes returns to the neckline from below, a retest. The ready-made figure above shows one. It does not always happen, and a retest that climbs back above the neckline weakens the reading.

The measured move. Some textbooks describe a convention in which the height of the pattern, from the peaks to the neckline, is projected downward from the neckline. This describes proportion only. It is not a target or an expectation.

Context. A double top at the end of a long advance on a daily or weekly chart summarises a lot of trading. Two similar highs on a five-minute chart form many times a day. Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous, and the double top is no exception.

A hypothetical double top, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price rises from 100 to a first peak at 128, pulls back to 114, and rallies to a second peak at 128.5.

  • Difference between the peaks: 128.5 minus 128 = 0.5, about 0.4% of the price.
  • Neckline: the swing low at 114.
  • Pattern height: 128.5 minus 114 = 14.5.
  • Measured-move convention: 114 minus 14.5 = 99.5.

If a candle closes at 112, below 114, the pattern meets the standard definition. The figure of 99.5 is what the textbook convention produces, nothing more. If instead price closes above 128.5, the reading is invalidated. Neither outcome is known while the second peak is forming.

What it does NOT mean

  • Two similar highs are not a double top. Without a close below the neckline, the pattern is incomplete by the standard definition.
  • A completed double top does not mean the advance is over. Breaks below the neckline regularly reverse.
  • The bearish label is a convention. Many apparent double tops resolve upward and become part of a continuing uptrend.
  • The measured move is not a destination. It is a way some textbooks describe proportion.

Common mistakes

  1. Labelling it at the second peak. At that moment the chart only shows price near an old high.
  2. Demanding identical highs. Markets are not that precise. Treating resistance as a zone is more realistic than a single price.
  3. Ignoring the size of the prior advance. A small rise has little to reverse.
  4. Reading wicks and closes interchangeably. A wick under the neckline and a close under it are different events.

Risks and limitations

  • Every threshold in the definition is a matter of judgement, so two readers can disagree about the same chart.
  • The pattern is only confirmed after a meaningful fall has already taken place.
  • Triple tops, rectangles and ranges all begin with the same two peaks.

Key takeaway

A double top is two failed attempts at a similar high, completed only by a close below the low between them. Until then it is a pause, and even afterwards it describes what happened, not what comes next.
