Descending Triangle

A descending triangle is a flat line of lows beneath a falling line of highs. It shows sellers accepting less on each rally while buyers hold one level, and is conventionally read as bearish.

Why it matters

The descending triangle is the mirror image of the ascending triangle. Its two boundaries again tell different stories. The flat bottom says buyers keep appearing at one level. The falling top says sellers are willing to accept lower prices on every rally.

The conventional reading sides with the sellers: supply is pressing down on a level that has to absorb it again and again. That is a fair description of the pressure. It leaves out the other half of the picture, which is a floor that has not yet failed.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a lower boundary that is flat, or nearly so, through at least two lows;
  • an upper boundary that falls, through at least two highs, each lower than the last;
  • price swinging between the two lines as they converge;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close below the flat line.

Texts describe the pattern mostly as a pause inside a downtrend, and sometimes as a top after an advance.

How it forms

In plain words

Think of a shelf with weights being added one at a time. The shelf has held so far, and each new weight is placed a little more carelessly than the last. You can see the load growing. You cannot see how strong the shelf is.

The flat line is an area of support: a price where enough buy orders have appeared to stop each decline. The falling line is a series of lower highs. After each bounce, sellers return sooner than before, so the rallies get weaker and price is pressed toward the bottom of the triangle.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Flat bottom Demand concentrated at one level How flat is flat; line or zone
Falling highs Supply appearing at lower prices Which highs the line goes through
Convergence Decreasing room and decreasing volatility Where the lines meet
Exit A close below the bottom Wick or close; how far below

Volume. Classic texts describe volume shrinking as the triangle narrows and picking up on the exit. They generally give less weight to volume on downward exits than on upward ones. These are conventions in the literature, and real charts often differ.

Invalidation. A close above the falling line is the usual first reference, because the sequence of lower highs has been broken. A close above the most recent swing high inside the triangle is a stronger one. As with all triangles, a shape in which price drifts into the point where the lines meet is generally treated as having lost its meaning.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The first is the upward exit.

The second is the false breakdown. The flat bottom is an obvious level with many orders resting just under it. A brief push below that recovers back into the triangle is a routine fakeout, and in crypto markets such dips can be sharp when leveraged positions are closed out. The third is the tilted bottom: if the lows are also drifting down, the shape may be a falling wedge, which carries the opposite conventional reading.

The measured move. Textbooks describe measuring the height of the triangle at its widest point and projecting that distance downward from the flat line. This is a convention for describing proportion. It is not a target or an expectation.

Context. A long-lived triangle on a higher timeframe summarises more trading than a small intraday one. Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous.

A hypothetical descending triangle, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price falls from 134 and turns up at 114 three times. The highs between and after those turns are 128, 123 and 118.5.

  • Fall between the highs: 128 minus 123 = 5, then 123 minus 118.5 = 4.5.
  • Room above the flat line at each high: 128 minus 114 = 14, 123 minus 114 = 9, 118.5 minus 114 = 4.5.
  • Height at the widest point: 14.
  • Measured-move convention: 114 minus 14 = 100.

The shrinking room, from 14 to 9 to 4.5, is the pressure building on the flat line. A close below 114 would complete the pattern by the usual definition, and 100 is only the output of the convention. A close above the falling line, and then above 118.5, would invalidate the reading.

What it does NOT mean

  • Falling highs do not mean the floor gives way. The flat line has held every time so far.
  • The bearish label is a convention. Descending triangles regularly exit upward.
  • A close below the bottom is not final. Recoveries back above the line are common.
  • The measured move is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Drawing it from too few points. One low and two highs do not make a flat bottom.
  2. Assuming repeated tests must end in a break. Levels also hold repeatedly and then send price the other way.
  3. Treating a wick below the line as the exit. Most definitions use the close.
  4. Redrawing the falling line after each rally. A boundary that keeps moving cannot be tested.

Risks and limitations

  • Whether the bottom is flat enough is a judgement that separates this pattern from others with different readings.
  • As the triangle narrows, price sits close to both lines and false exits become more frequent.
  • The pattern gives no information about the timing or size of any later move.

Key takeaway

A descending triangle shows sellers pressing at lower prices while buyers defend one level. It describes that pressure accurately, and it cannot say whether the level or the pressure gives out first.
