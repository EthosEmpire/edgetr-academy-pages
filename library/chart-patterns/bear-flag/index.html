Bear Flag

A bear flag is a sharp fall followed by a short, tight drift upward between two roughly parallel lines. It is conventionally read as a pause in a decline, and it often resolves the other way.

Why it matters

After a fast decline, price rarely keeps falling at the same speed. Selling slows, some short sellers close positions, a few buyers step in, and price drifts upward for a while. The bear flag is the name for a small, orderly version of that drift.

It is worth learning alongside the bull flag, and it is a common source of false confidence: a weak bounce after a fall and the first stage of a recovery begin in exactly the same way.

What it looks like

The conventional recognition rules are:

  • a pole: a sharp, nearly straight decline, much faster than the price action before it;
  • a flag: a short series of small candles drifting up, or sideways, against the pole;
  • flag boundaries that are roughly parallel;
  • a flag that is small relative to the pole, in both height and duration;
  • completion is commonly defined as a close below the lower flag line.

How it forms

In plain words

A ball rolling down a steep hill reaches a short ledge that tilts slightly upward. It slows and wobbles there. Whether it rolls off the ledge or settles on it depends on things you cannot see from the wobble alone.

The pole shows urgent selling. The flag shows that urgency fading, without urgent buying replacing it. Price rises, but in small candles and with little participation. The conventional reading is that the rise comes mostly from sellers pausing, not from strong new demand.

In leveraged crypto markets there is an extra wrinkle. A steep pole is sometimes driven by forced selling in a liquidation cascade, and the drift after it by short sellers closing positions. A chart alone cannot show who is behind each candle.

How to read it

Element Conventional reading What is subjective
Pole A burst of strong supply How steep and how long it must be
Flag slope Up or sideways, against the pole How much slope is too much
Flag height Shallow relative to the pole Where a “flag” becomes a recovery
Exit A close below the lower line Wick or close; how far below

Volume. The classic description has heavy volume on the pole and clearly lighter volume in the flag. Some texts add that volume matters less for downward exits than for upward ones. These are conventions, and real charts often differ.

Invalidation. A close above the upper flag line is the usual reference. Many descriptions also drop the label if the drift recovers a large part of the pole, for example more than half of it, or if it goes on so long that the fall is no longer the dominant feature of the chart.

Going deeper

Common false signals. The clearest is the flag that exits upward.

Others include the brief dip under the lower line that recovers back into the flag, a downward fakeout, and the “flag” drawn after a decline that was too slow to be a pole.

Context. A flag inside an established downtrend agrees with the larger structure. One that appears after a long, extended fall, or on a very short timeframe, has less behind it.

The measured move. Textbooks describe a convention in which the length of the pole is projected downward from the point where price leaves the flag. This is a way of describing proportion. It is not a target or an expectation.

Pattern recognition is probabilistic and often ambiguous. Nothing in the flag itself reveals its exit.

A hypothetical bear flag, measuredHypothetical illustration

Price sits near 141, then falls quickly to 116. Over the next several candles it drifts up to 123.5 between two parallel lines.

  • Pole length: 141 minus 116 = 25.
  • Flag height: 123.5 minus 116 = 7.5.
  • Height as a share of the pole: 7.5 divided by 25 = 30%.
  • Measured-move convention, if price leaves the flag at 119: 119 minus 25 = 94.

A close below the lower line would complete the pattern by the usual definition. The figure of 94 is only what the convention produces. A close above the upper line, as in the failed example above, would invalidate the reading.

What it does NOT mean

  • A bear flag does not mean the decline continues. Flags exit upward regularly.
  • The bearish label is a convention. It names the textbook outcome and says nothing about the odds in any given case.
  • The measured move is not a target. It is a textbook description of size.

Common mistakes

  1. Labelling every bounce in a falling market. The pattern needs a sharp pole and a small, orderly drift.
  2. Ignoring how much has been recovered. A drift that retraces most of the pole is no longer a pause.
  3. Fitting lines to a handful of candles. Parallel lines can be drawn over nearly anything.
  4. Judging before the close. A candle below the lower line can still close back inside.

Risks and limitations

  • “Sharp”, “small” and “brief” have no agreed thresholds.
  • Sudden reversals upward after sharp falls are common in volatile markets and can be violent.

Key takeaway

A bear flag is a sharp fall followed by a small, orderly drift upward. It describes a pause in selling, and only the exit shows whether that pause was a rest or the beginning of a recovery.
