Isolated & Cross Margin

Isolated margin normally confines a position's loss to the margin assigned to it, while cross margin lets positions draw on the whole account balance as shared collateral.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

High-risk topic

Both margin modes involve leverage. Leverage multiplies losses as well as gains, and a liquidation can take the entire margin of a position. Under cross margin, that margin is the whole account balance. This page explains the two modes. It does not recommend either.

Why it matters

On a leveraged account there is a setting, often a small toggle, that decides how much money stands behind each position. It does not change the position’s size or direction. It changes what can be lost if the position goes wrong.

Two traders with the same account, the same position and the same price move can end with very different balances because of this one setting.

How it works

Under isolated margin, a fixed amount of margin is assigned to one position. Only that amount backs it. If losses use it up, the position is liquidated and, on most exchanges, the loss stops there. In a very fast market the close can happen beyond that point, and who absorbs the shortfall depends on the venue’s rules. The remaining balance is normally untouched.

Under cross margin, the whole available balance acts as shared collateral for all open positions. A losing position draws on the entire account before it is liquidated. It survives a larger adverse move, and if liquidation does come, far more has been lost.

In plain words

Picture a ship. Isolated margin is a hull divided into watertight compartments: if one floods, the water stays there, and that compartment is lost. Cross margin is a hull with one open space: a leak takes much longer to matter, because the whole ship absorbs it, but if it is not stopped, the whole ship goes down.

How to read the difference

The position is identical in both columns. At any price, its unrealized loss is the same. The mode changes only two things: where the liquidation price sits, and what is gone when it is reached.

A side-by-side summary is on the isolated vs cross margin page.

Going deeper

Several positions under cross margin

With cross margin, unrealized gains on one position count as collateral for the others. Positions that offset each other, as in a hedge, therefore need less total margin.

The link works in both directions. One badly losing position drains the collateral that every other position relies on, so unrelated positions can be liquidated together.

Effective leverage

The leverage setting means different things in the two modes. In isolated mode it fixes the margin for that position. In cross mode the real backing is the account, so what matters is the total size of all positions against the total balance.

Adding margin to an isolated position

An isolated position can be topped up by hand, which moves its liquidation price further away and raises the amount at stake. Some exchanges offer an automatic top-up. With that switched on, an isolated position behaves increasingly like a cross position.

Exchange differences

Which mode is the default, whether the mode can be switched while a position is open, and how liquidation is carried out all differ between exchanges and products. See margin on derivatives and liquidation on perpetuals. Some venues also offer portfolio margin, a further model that sets requirements from the net risk of the whole account.

One hypothetical position in both modesHypothetical illustration

An account holds 1,000. A trader opens a long of 50 units at 100, a position of 5,000.

Isolated, with 500 assigned (10x).

  • Price falls 10%, to 90. The loss is 500, the whole assigned margin. The position is liquidated, in practice slightly before 90.
  • The other 500 in the account is untouched. The account is down about 50%.
  • If price then recovers to 100, nothing changes. The position no longer exists.

Cross.

  • Price falls 10%, to 90. The loss is 500. The position is still open, backed by the remaining 500.
  • If price recovers to 100, the unrealized loss is gone.
  • If price instead falls 20%, to 80, the loss is 1,000. The position is liquidated, in practice slightly before 80, and the account is close to empty.

Neither mode is better in general. Where price recovered, isolated mode had already closed a position that cross mode kept. Where price kept falling, cross mode lost twice as much.

What it does NOT mean

  • Isolated does not mean safe. The assigned margin can be lost completely, and at high leverage quickly.
  • Cross does not mean lower risk. The liquidation price is further away because more money is exposed, not because the position is less dangerous.
  • The mode does not change profit or loss. The same price move produces the same result on the position in either mode.
  • A distant liquidation price is not protection. It measures how much can be lost before the exchange steps in.

Common mistakes

  1. Not knowing which mode is active. Defaults differ between exchanges and sometimes between markets on the same exchange.
  2. Treating the free balance as separate under cross margin. It is collateral and can be consumed without any further action.
  3. Judging cross positions one at a time. Their liquidation depends on the combined result of everything open.
  4. Topping up an isolated position repeatedly. Each addition raises the amount at stake and removes the limit that isolation provided.

Risks and limitations

  • Under isolated margin, a brief adverse spike can liquidate a position that would otherwise have continued.
  • Under cross margin, one position can consume the entire account balance.
  • In extreme markets, forced exits can fill at poor prices in either mode.
  • Mode rules are set by each exchange and can change.

Key takeaway

Isolated margin normally confines a position’s loss to the margin assigned to it, though not in every market or on every venue. Cross margin shares the whole balance, which delays liquidation and raises what a liquidation can cost.
