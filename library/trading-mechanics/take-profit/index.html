Take Profit

A take profit is an order placed in advance to close a position at a favourable price, turning an unrealized gain into a realized one if the market reaches that level.

  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A gain on an open position is only a number on a screen. It becomes real when the position is closed. A take profit is the order that does this automatically at a price chosen beforehand.

It is the mirror image of a stop loss, with a different weakness. A stop loss may fill at a worse price than planned. A take profit, in its usual form, may not fill at all.

What it looks like

A take profit closes a position in the favourable direction.

  • For a long position it is a sell order above the entry price.
  • For a short position it is a buy order below the entry price.

If the market reaches the level and the order fills, the position ends and the gain is realized. If the market never reaches it, the order simply waits.

In plain words

Imagine telling an auction house, “If anyone bids 110 for my painting, sell it.” You do not need to be in the room. If the bidding reaches 110, the sale happens. If it stops at 109, the painting comes home with you, however close it was.

How it works

Most often a take profit is an ordinary limit order resting at the target price. It fills at that price or better, and it carries the usual limit-order uncertainty: price can touch the level without enough trading to fill the order.

Some exchanges, especially for derivatives, build it as a trigger instead. When the trigger price is reached, a market order is released. That order is very likely to execute, but its fill can slip, so the result may be slightly below the target on a long.

Fill Not certain Near certain once triggered

Going deeper

Pairing with a stop loss

A take profit and a stop loss are often placed together, one on each side of the entry. Many platforms link them as a bracket or one-cancels-the-other (OCO) pair: when one fills, the other is cancelled.

If the two are not linked, the survivor stays active after the position is gone. A leftover sell order can then open an unintended short on a derivatives account. A reduce-only setting prevents this. See order types.

Target distance and the stop distance

The distance from entry to target, compared with the distance from entry to stop, is the risk/reward ratio of a plan. In the chart at the top of this page the entry is 110, the stop 104 and the target 122: distances of 6 and 12, a ratio of 1:2.

The ratio describes the plan only. It says nothing about how likely price is to reach either level. In general a nearer target is reached more often and gains less each time, and a farther target is reached less often and gains more. That is a trade-off, not a rule for choosing one.

Partial targets

A position can be closed in pieces at different prices. Each fill realizes the gain on its own portion, and the average exit price is weighted by size. See entry and exit.

One hypothetical take profit, three outcomesHypothetical illustration

A trader is long 10 units from an entry of 100.

  • Filled. A sell limit for all 10 units rests at 110. Price trades up to 110.50. The order fills at 110. The realized gain is 10% of the position before fees.
  • Missed. The same order rests at 110. Price peaks at 109.80 and falls back to 100. Nothing fills. The unrealized gain of 9.8% at the peak is gone and the position is still open.
  • Two targets. Sell limits for 5 units rest at 106 and at 112. Price reaches 113, so both fill. The average exit is (106 + 112) / 2 = 109, a realized gain of 9%. Had price turned at 107, only the first half would have been closed, with a 6% gain on that half, and the rest would still be open.

What it does NOT mean

  • A take profit is not a forecast. Placing a target at a price says nothing about whether the market gets there.
  • A take-profit order does not secure a gain. Nothing is secured until it fills.
  • A touched target is not a filled target. A resting limit order may need price to trade through its level before it is reached in the queue.
  • A larger target is not a better plan. A ratio on paper carries no information about the chance of reaching either level.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting an unfilled target as a result. Until the order fills, the gain is unrealized and can disappear.
  2. Not knowing which design the exchange uses. A limit take profit and a trigger take profit behave differently in fast markets.
  3. Leaving unlinked orders behind. After one exit fills, the other can open a new position unless it is cancelled or reduce-only.
  4. Forgetting the fill in the ratio. Risk/reward is calculated from planned levels, while results come from actual fills on both sides.

Risks and limitations

  • A limit take profit may never fill, even when price comes very close.
  • A trigger take profit can slip in a fast market.
  • A fixed target closes the position there, whatever the market does afterwards.
  • Automated exits depend on the exchange working normally at the moment price arrives.

Key takeaway

A take profit is a pre-set order that closes a position at a favourable price. In its common limit form the price is controlled and the fill is not: the market has to reach it.
