Trading Fees: Maker & Taker

Exchanges charge a fee on each fill: the maker rate applies to orders that add resting liquidity to the book and the taker rate to orders that remove it.

  • Beginner
  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026
On this page
  1. Why it matters
  2. How it works
  3. How it is calculated
  4. Going deeper
  5. What it does NOT mean

Why it matters

Fees are a trading cost that is certain. A trade may gain or lose, but the fee is charged either way, on the way in and on the way out. Small percentages look harmless until they are multiplied by position size, by leverage and by the number of trades.

Knowing how fees are structured also explains why exchanges treat two orders at the same price differently.

How it works

Most order-book exchanges use a maker-taker model with two rates.

  • The maker is the side whose order was already resting in the order book. It made liquidity available.
  • The taker is the side whose order arrived and traded against it. It took that liquidity away.

Every fill has one of each. Exchanges usually charge takers more, because a busy book of resting orders is what attracts traders in the first place. Some venues go further and pay makers a small rebate.

In plain words

Think of a market stall. The stallholder who sets out goods and waits all day is the maker. The customer who walks up and buys on the spot is the taker. The market’s owner wants full stalls, so stallholders get the cheaper deal.

How it is calculated

Fee = fill size x fill price x fee rateThe fee is charged on the full value of the fill, on every fill. A round trip therefore pays at least twice.

Which rate applies depends on what the order did, not on its name:

Order Role Why
Market order Taker It always trades against resting orders
Limit order that rests, then fills Maker It was waiting in the book
Limit order that trades immediately Taker It crossed the spread on arrival
Triggered stop-market order Taker It becomes a market order

Fee schedules are set by each exchange. Common features include tiers, where rates fall as recent trading volume rises, different schedules for spot and derivatives, and discounts tied to the exchange’s own programmes. The published schedule of the venue in question is the only reliable source for actual rates.

Going deeper

Fees are charged on position size

On a leveraged position, the fee is calculated on the full position, not on the margin behind it. With leverage, a fee that is small against the position becomes large against the trader’s own funds.

Leverage Round-trip taker fees as % of position As % of margin
1x 0.20% 0.20%
5x 0.20% 1%
10x 0.20% 2%
20x 0.20% 4%

The table uses the hypothetical 0.10% taker rate from the figure above.

The other costs of a trade

The maker or taker fee is only the visible part.

Cost When it applies
Spread Whenever a trade is made immediately
Slippage Market-type orders, mostly in thin or fast markets
Funding Periodically while a perpetual futures position is open. It can be paid or received
Borrowing interest While a margin loan is open
Withdrawal and network fees When assets are moved off the exchange or on-chain
Liquidation fee If a leveraged position is force-closed

Decentralized exchanges

A swap on a decentralized exchange usually has no maker and taker rates. The trader pays a swap fee, most of which typically goes to the liquidity providers of the pool, plus the network fee for the transaction itself.

Fees on one hypothetical round tripHypothetical illustration

A trader opens a position of 10 units at a price of 100, worth 1,000, and later closes it at the same price. The exchange’s hypothetical schedule is 0.05% maker and 0.10% taker.

  • Both orders are market orders. Each fill costs 0.10% of 1,000, which is 1.00. The round trip costs 2.00, or 0.20% of the position. Price must move about 0.20% in the trade’s favour just to cover fees, before the spread.
  • Both orders are resting limit orders. Each fill costs 0.05%, which is 0.50. The round trip costs 1.00, or 0.10%.
  • The position is held with 10x leverage. The margin is 100. The same 2.00 in taker fees equals 2% of the margin.

What it does NOT mean

  • “Zero fee” does not mean zero cost. The cost may sit in a wider spread, in the price received, or in other charges.
  • A limit order does not always earn the maker rate. If it trades on arrival, it is a taker order.
  • The fee rate is not the whole cost of trading. Spread and slippage are often larger than the explicit fee.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting the fee once. Opening and closing are separate fills, each with its own fee.
  2. Measuring fees against the position only. With leverage, the same fee is a much larger share of the margin.
  3. Ignoring holding costs. Funding and borrowing interest accumulate for as long as a position stays open.
  4. Assuming all exchanges charge alike. Rates, tiers and what counts as a maker order differ between venues.

Risks and limitations

  • Fees are certain, while the outcome of a trade is not. Frequent trading raises the hurdle every trade has to clear.
  • Fee schedules can change, and promotional rates can end.
  • Pursuing the maker rate means using orders that may not fill.
  • Comparing venues on headline fees alone leaves out spread, depth and reliability.

Key takeaway

Makers add resting liquidity and usually pay less. Takers remove it and usually pay more. Fees apply to every fill and to the full position size, and schedules differ by exchange.
