Bid, Ask & Spread

The bid is the highest price a buyer is offering, the ask is the lowest price a seller will accept, and the spread is the gap between the two.

  • Beginner
  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026
On this page

Why it matters

A market never has one price. It has two: a price at which you can buy immediately and a lower price at which you can sell immediately. The difference between them is a cost that every trader who wants an instant fill pays, usually without seeing it listed anywhere.

Understanding the spread explains why a position usually shows a small loss the moment it is opened, and why the same asset can be cheap to trade in one market and expensive in another.

How it works

The bid is the highest price at which someone is currently waiting to buy. The ask, also called the offer, is the lowest price at which someone is currently waiting to sell. Both come straight from the top of the order book.

The spread is the ask minus the bid. The mid price is the point halfway between them and is often used as a neutral reference.

Spread = best ask - best bid Spread % = spread / mid price x 100With a bid of 100.00 and an ask of 100.10, the spread is 0.10 and the mid price is 100.05, so the spread is about 0.10%.

In plain words

A currency exchange desk at an airport shows two prices for the same currency: one at which it buys from you and a higher one at which it sells to you. Change your money and change it straight back, and you end up with less than you started with. The difference is the desk’s spread.

How to read it

Which side of the spread a trade happens on depends on who is in a hurry.

Action Fills at Why
Buy immediately (market order) The ask The buyer accepts a waiting seller’s price
Sell immediately (market order) The bid The seller accepts a waiting buyer’s price
Buy with a limit order at the bid The bid, if it fills The buyer waits for a seller to come to them
Sell with a limit order at the ask The ask, if it fills The seller waits for a buyer to come to them

Trading immediately is called crossing the spread. Waiting with a limit order avoids paying the spread but replaces a known cost with an unknown: the order may never fill.

Going deeper

Why a spread exists

Someone has to be willing to stand in the market and trade with whoever arrives. Market makers do this by quoting a bid and an ask at the same time. The spread is their compensation for the risk of holding an asset whose price may move against them, and for the risk of trading with someone better informed.

What makes it wider or narrower

  • Liquidity. Many competing orders push the bid and ask together. Few orders leave a wide gap. See liquidity.
  • Volatility. When price moves quickly, quoting is riskier, so spreads widen. This often happens around major news.
  • Time of day. Quiet hours tend to have wider spreads than busy ones.
  • Tick size. Each market has a minimum price step. The spread cannot be smaller than one step.

The spread and the chart

A chart usually plots the last traded price. That trade may have happened at the bid or at the ask, so in a quiet market the last price can flicker between the two without anything having changed. This is sometimes called bid-ask bounce.

Spread as a fee

The spread is separate from the explicit trading fee an exchange charges. Some brokers advertise zero commission and are paid through the spread instead. The cost has moved, not disappeared.

A hypothetical round trip with no price changeHypothetical illustration

The bid is 100.00 and the ask is 100.10. A trader buys 10 units with a market order and fills at 100.10. A moment later, with the quote unchanged, the trader sells the 10 units with a market order and fills at 100.00.

The market did not move, yet the trader gave up 0.10 per unit, about 0.10% of the position, before any fees. For the position to break even before fees, the bid would first have had to rise from 100.00 to 100.10.

In a thin market quoted at 99.50 bid and 100.50 ask, the same round trip gives up 1.00 per unit, about 1%.

What it does NOT mean

  • A narrow spread does not mean a large order fills at that price. The spread describes only the top of the book. Bigger orders reach deeper levels and suffer slippage.
  • The mid price is not a tradable price. It is a reference point between two real prices.
  • A wide spread is not a statement about the asset’s quality. It describes how the market for it is trading at that moment.
  • A spread seen now is not fixed. It can widen many times over within seconds when the market becomes stressed.

Common mistakes

  1. Ignoring the spread as a cost. It is paid on entry and again on exit whenever both are done with market orders.
  2. Comparing fees but not spreads. A venue with low fees and a wide spread can cost more per round trip than one with higher fees and a tight spread.
  3. Judging the spread in absolute terms. A spread of 0.10 is small on a price of 100 and enormous on a price of 1. The percentage is what compares across markets.

Risks and limitations

  • Spreads widen sharply in volatile or illiquid conditions, which raises the cost of entering and exiting at the same time.
  • Stop orders that trigger in such conditions fill across a wide spread.
  • The quoted spread applies to small sizes only. The effective cost of a large order is higher.

Key takeaway

Every market has two prices, and the gap between them is a real cost of trading immediately. It is small in deep markets, large in thin ones, and it changes with conditions.
