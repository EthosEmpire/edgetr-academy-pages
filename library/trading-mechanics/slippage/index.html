Slippage

Slippage is the difference between the price a trader expected when sending an order and the price at which the order was actually filled.

  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

The price on screen when an order is sent is an expectation. The price in the trade confirmation is the result. Slippage is the gap between them, and it is a real cost that never appears on a fee schedule.

It matters most at the moments traders care most about: large orders, fast markets and stop orders being triggered.

How it works

Slippage arises with any order that accepts the market’s price instead of naming its own. That means market orders and anything that turns into one, including a triggered stop order and a forced liquidation.

Slippage = fill price - expected price Slippage % = slippage / expected price x 100For a buy, a higher fill is unfavourable. For a sell, a lower fill is unfavourable.

In plain words

You see fuel advertised at one price from the motorway and take the exit. By the time you reach the pump, the cheap pump has a queue and the free one charges more. You still fill the tank, at a slightly different price from the one that made you turn off.

Where it comes from

Size against depth. An order book holds a limited quantity at each price. An order bigger than the quantity at the best price continues into the next levels, and its average fill is worse than the first price. The part of slippage caused by the order itself is often called price impact.

Movement while the order travels. Other traders’ orders reach the exchange in the same instant. The best price can be taken or cancelled before a new order arrives.

Jumps. When price moves from one level to another with no trades in between, an order that triggers in the jump fills on the far side of it.

Condition Typical effect on slippage
High liquidity, small order Very small
Large order relative to depth Larger, even in a calm market
High volatility, news Larger, and less predictable
Quiet hours, small or new markets Larger

Going deeper

Slippage and the spread are different costs

The spread is the known gap between the best bid and best ask before the order is sent. Slippage is whatever happens beyond that. A market buy expected at the best ask of 100.10 that fills at 100.10 paid the spread and had no slippage.

It can go either way

Slippage is usually discussed as a cost, but a fill can also be better than expected. This is called positive slippage or price improvement.

Limit orders trade slippage for something else

A limit order cannot fill at a worse price than its limit, so it has no unfavourable slippage. The cost is moved, not removed: the order may not fill, and the market may leave it behind.

Slippage tolerance on decentralized exchanges

On a decentralized exchange there is usually no order book. A swap is priced by a liquidity pool, and the price can change between signing a transaction and its confirmation. Wallets therefore ask for a slippage tolerance: the largest unfavourable change the trader accepts. If the price moves further than that, the swap fails instead of executing. See swaps and routing.

Two hypothetical cases of slippageHypothetical illustration

A large market buy. The best ask is 100.10 with 180 units waiting. Behind it are 260 units at 100.20 and 420 at 100.30. A trader sends a market buy for 500 units expecting about 100.10.

The fill is 180 at 100.10, 260 at 100.20 and 60 at 100.30. The average is 100.176. Slippage is 0.076 per unit, about 0.08%.

A stop in a fast market. A sell stop has its trigger at 95. Price drops quickly, the stop triggers, and the resulting market order fills at 94.50. Slippage is 0.50 per unit, about 0.53% of the trigger price.

Neither case involved an error. Both orders did what their type specifies.

What it does NOT mean

  • Slippage does not mean the exchange cheated. It is the ordinary result of a market-type order meeting a limited quantity of waiting orders.
  • Zero slippage on small orders does not carry over to large ones. Slippage grows with size relative to depth.
  • A slippage tolerance setting is not a fee. It is a limit on how far the price may move before a swap is cancelled.
  • Past slippage in a market is not a measure of future slippage. Depth can disappear within seconds when conditions change.

Common mistakes

  1. Planning with the screen price. Entry and exit calculations based on the last price leave out the spread and slippage on both legs.
  2. Assuming a stop fills at its trigger. Stops release market orders at the moments when slippage is largest.
  3. Setting a very wide tolerance on a swap. A wide tolerance accepts a correspondingly poor price if the market moves, or if another party exploits the allowance.

Risks and limitations

  • Slippage is largest in volatile, illiquid and news-driven conditions, which is when forced exits usually happen.
  • It compounds with leverage: a small percentage of the position can be a large percentage of the margin behind it.
  • It cannot be known in advance. Estimates shown before an order is sent are based on a book that may change.
  • Avoiding it with limit orders introduces the separate risk of not being filled.

Key takeaway

Slippage is the difference between the expected price and the actual fill. It comes from order size, speed and jumps, and it is greatest when the market is thin or moving fast.
