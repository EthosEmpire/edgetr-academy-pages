Leverage

Leverage is the ratio of a position's size to the margin backing it; it multiplies the effect of every price move on that margin, for losses exactly as much as for gains.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

High-risk topic

Leverage multiplies losses as well as gains. A leveraged position can be liquidated by a modest price move, and a liquidation can take the entire margin of that position. This page explains the arithmetic. It does not suggest using leverage or any particular level of it.

Why it matters

Leverage is the reason a small price move can produce a very large change in an account. It is built into margin trading and into derivatives such as perpetual futures, where exchanges offer it at the move of a slider.

The idea is simple, and its consequences are easy to underestimate: leverage does not change what the market does. It changes how much of the trader’s own money each move represents.

How it works

A leveraged position has two sizes. The position size, or notional value, is what the position is worth in the market. The margin is the trader’s own money backing it. Leverage is the ratio between them.

Leverage = position size / marginA position of 1,000 backed by 100 of margin has 10x leverage. Rearranged: margin = position size / leverage.

Gains and losses are produced by the full position and land on the margin. So a price change, seen from the margin, is multiplied by the leverage.

Change on margin (%) = price change (%) x leverageSimplified: ignores fees and funding.

In plain words

Leverage works like a long lever on a see-saw. A small push at one end moves the other end a long way. The lever does not care which direction the push goes. It makes every movement bigger, up and down alike.

How to read it

Leverage Margin as a share of the position Price move that changes margin by 10% Adverse move that would use up all the margin
1x 100% 10% 100%
2x 50% 5% 50%
5x 20% 2% 20%
10x 10% 1% 10%
20x 5% 0.5% 5%
50x 2% 0.2% 2%

The last column is the origin of a common rule of thumb: a position at leverage L is liquidated by an adverse move of roughly 100 / L percent.

In practice liquidation comes sooner than that. The exchange closes the position while a little margin is still left, at the maintenance requirement, and fees and funding also draw on the margin. Exact formulas differ between exchanges. See margin and collateral.

Going deeper

The setting and the effective leverage

The number chosen on an exchange’s slider sets how much margin is assigned to a position. Effective leverage is a broader measure: total position size divided by all the funds actually backing it.

Under cross margin the whole account balance backs the position, so a position opened at a “10x” setting in a large account may have low effective leverage. The reverse also holds: several positions at modest settings can add up to high effective leverage on the account. See isolated and cross margin.

Leverage is not constant

Leverage changes as price moves. When a position loses, the margin shrinks faster than the position, so the ratio between them rises. A 10x position that has lost half its margin is no longer 10x. It is close to 19x, and the next move counts for almost twice as much.

Costs scale with the position

Trading fees and funding are charged on the notional value, not on the margin. At 10x, a fee of 0.1% of the position is 1% of the margin.

Leverage and volatility

Whether a given leverage leaves a position near or far from liquidation depends on how much the asset normally moves. An adverse move of 5% is rare on some assets and an ordinary day on others. The same leverage therefore means very different things in different markets. See volatility.

Why it exists

Leverage also lets a hedger offset a large exposure without tying up its full value. The mechanism is the same whoever uses it. The broader discussion is under leverage risk.

A hypothetical 10x long, move by moveHypothetical illustration

A trader assigns 100 of margin to a 10x long at a price of 100. The position is 10 units, worth 1,000.

  • Price +2%, to 102. The position gains 20. That is +20% on the margin.
  • Price -2%, to 98. The position loses 20. That is -20% on the margin.
  • Price -5%, to 95. The position loses 50, half the margin. The position is now worth 950 and is backed by 50, an effective leverage of 19x.
  • Price -10%, to 90. The loss would be 100, the whole margin. The position does not get that far: with a hypothetical maintenance requirement of 0.5%, it is liquidated near 90.5.

With the same 100 at 2x, the position is 200. The same -10% move costs 20, which is 20% of the margin, and the position remains open.

What it does NOT mean

  • Leverage does not improve a trade. It does not change the price move, the timing or the odds. It changes only the size of the result.
  • Maximum leverage is not a recommended level. The figure an exchange allows is a limit set by the venue, not guidance.
  • A low setting does not mean a small position. What matters is the position’s size against the funds that back it.
  • The 100 / L figure is not the liquidation distance. For a position with its own fixed margin it is an upper bound. The real distance is shorter and differs by exchange.

Common mistakes

  1. Seeing the multiplier on gains only. The same factor applies to every loss.
  2. Reading the price chart without the multiplier. A move that looks like noise on the chart can be most of the margin at high leverage.
  3. Assuming the margin is the most that can be lost. Under cross margin, the rest of the account is also exposed.

Risks and limitations

  • A small adverse move can remove a large share of the margin, or all of it.
  • Liquidation ends the position at that moment. A later recovery in price does not restore it.
  • Slippage in fast markets can make forced exits worse than the calculated price.
  • Exchanges can change leverage limits and margin requirements, including for positions that are already open.

Key takeaway

Leverage is position size divided by margin. It multiplies every price move as seen from the margin, in both directions, and the higher it is, the smaller the adverse move that ends the position.
