Stop Orders

A stop order stays dormant until the market reaches a trigger price, then becomes a market order (stop-market) or a limit order (stop-limit).

  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A stop order lets a trader leave an instruction that acts only if the market reaches a certain price. It is the mechanism underneath every stop loss, and it is widely misunderstood: many people read the stop price as the price they will get.

It is not. Knowing the difference between the trigger and the fill explains most unpleasant surprises with stops.

How it works

A stop order has a stop price, also called the trigger price. Until the market reaches it, the order is inactive. On most venues it is not shown in the order book at all.

When the trigger is reached, the exchange releases a second order. What kind depends on the type of stop.

  • A sell stop is placed below the current price. It triggers if price falls to it.
  • A buy stop is placed above the current price. It triggers if price rises to it.

This is the reverse of a limit order, where buys wait below the market and sells wait above.

In plain words

A stop order is like a smoke alarm wired to a sprinkler. Nothing happens until smoke reaches the sensor. Then the sprinkler switches on. The alarm decides when the water starts. It has no control over how much damage has already been done by then.

Two kinds of stop

A stop-market order, often just called a stop order, becomes a market order when triggered. It will trade against whatever is waiting in the book, at whatever price that is.

A stop-limit order has two prices: the trigger and a limit. When triggered, it becomes a limit order at the limit price. It will not trade at a worse price, which also means that it may not trade.

Stop-market Stop-limit
Prices set Trigger only Trigger and limit
After trigger Market order Limit order
Fill price Unknown, can be worse than the trigger Limit price or better
Risk A poor fill No fill

Going deeper

Why a fill can be worse than the trigger

In an orderly market, price passes through the trigger one small step at a time and a stop-market order fills close to it. Two situations break that assumption:

  • Fast markets. Price moves faster than orders can be matched, and the book near the trigger is thin. The released market order walks through several levels. This is slippage.
  • Gaps. Price jumps from one level to another with no trading in between, typically across a market close or after sudden news. See gaps.

What counts as “reaching” the trigger

Exchanges differ in which price they watch. It may be the last traded price, the best bid or ask, or on derivatives a calculated mark price. Many platforms let the trader choose. The same stop can trigger on one setting and not on another.

Trailing stops

A trailing stop is a stop whose trigger follows the market at a fixed distance when price moves favourably and stays put when price moves back. Once triggered it behaves like any other stop.

A stop is also not only an exit. A buy stop above the market can open a position if price rises to it.

One hypothetical sell stop, three outcomesHypothetical illustration

A trader holds an asset bought at 100 and places a sell stop with a trigger at 95.

  • Orderly market, stop-market. Price slides down to 95. The stop triggers and the market order fills at 94.95, about 0.05% below the trigger.
  • Gap, stop-market. After sudden news, the last trade is 96 and the next is 92. The stop triggers and fills near 92, about 3.2% below the trigger.
  • Gap, stop-limit with a limit at 94.50. The same jump to 92 triggers the stop. A sell limit at 94.50 is now resting above the market. Nobody buys at 94.50 while price is 92, so nothing fills and the position stays open.

The trigger price was 95 in every case. The result was 94.95, about 92, and no exit at all.

What it does NOT mean

  • A stop price is not a promised exit price. It is the condition that releases the order.
  • A stop-limit is not a safer stop. It removes the risk of a poor fill by adding the risk of no fill.
  • A stop order does not mean an order is sitting in the book. Until triggered it usually exists only on the exchange’s or broker’s systems.
  • Having a stop does not cap the loss at a fixed number. In a gap, the realized loss can be larger than the distance to the stop.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading the trigger as the fill. The two are the same only in a calm, liquid market.
  2. Confusing a sell stop with a sell limit. A sell order entered below the market as a limit fills immediately. As a stop it waits.
  3. Not knowing the trigger source. Last price and mark price can differ, especially in thin markets.

Risks and limitations

  • Stop-market orders can fill far from the trigger in fast or gapping markets.
  • Stop-limit orders can be left unfilled while the market keeps moving.
  • A brief spike can trigger a stop and then reverse. The order still executes.
  • Triggered orders depend on the exchange operating normally at that moment. Rules and reliability differ between venues.

Key takeaway

A stop order is a trigger attached to another order. The trigger decides when it is sent. The market at that moment decides the price, or whether it fills at all.
