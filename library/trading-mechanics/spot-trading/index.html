Spot Trading

Spot trading is buying or selling the asset itself, paid for in full and delivered straight away, with no borrowing, no leverage and no liquidation.

  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Spot trading is the plainest form of trading: one asset is exchanged for another, now, at the current price. It is what most people picture when they think of buying something on a market.

It is also the baseline against which margin, leverage and derivatives are measured. Those products are easiest to understand as “spot, plus something borrowed or promised”.

How it works

On a spot market, a trade is settled “on the spot”. The buyer pays the full price and receives the asset. The seller hands over the asset and receives the payment. The price at which this happens is the spot price.

After a spot purchase the buyer owns the asset outright. It can be held, sold later, or, in the case of crypto, withdrawn to a personal wallet.

In plain words

Spot trading is like buying fruit at a market stall with cash. You hand over the money and walk away with the fruit. There is no loan, no contract and no later date. If the fruit’s price changes tomorrow, the only thing affected is the value of what is already in your bag.

How to read a spot market

Every spot market is a trading pair. To buy the base asset, an account must hold enough of the quote asset to pay for it in full. To sell, it must hold the base asset.

Orders are matched through an order book on most centralized exchanges, with the same order types, spread and fees described elsewhere on this shelf.

Feature Spot
What is held The asset itself
Payment In full, at the time of the trade
Borrowing None
Gains when Price rises after a purchase
Largest possible loss The amount paid, if the price fell to zero
Liquidation None
Ongoing cost of holding No funding and no interest

Going deeper

Spot compared with derivatives

A derivative such as a perpetual future tracks the spot price without delivering the asset. That makes short positions and leverage simple to offer, and it introduces margin, funding and liquidation. See spot vs perpetuals.

On plain spot there is no way to hold a short position. Selling only reduces or closes a holding. Shorting needs something borrowed, which is margin trading, or a derivative.

No liquidation is not the same as no risk

Because nothing is borrowed, nobody can force a spot holder to sell. A holding can fall 50% or 90% and still be there. That is a real structural difference from leveraged products. It does not limit how far the price itself can fall, and an asset can lose most or all of its value.

Who actually holds the asset

On a centralized exchange, a spot balance is an entry in the exchange’s ledger: a claim on the exchange, not coins in the user’s own possession. Whether that claim can be honoured depends on the exchange. This is counterparty risk. Withdrawing to a personal wallet changes who holds the keys. See custodial and non-custodial wallets.

On a decentralized exchange a spot trade is a swap that settles on-chain, directly between a wallet and a liquidity pool.

Settlement

On crypto exchanges, a spot fill updates balances at once. In traditional securities markets, final settlement takes place after the trade date under each market’s own rules.

A hypothetical spot holding through two price movesHypothetical illustration

An account holds 1,000 in cash. A trader buys 5 units at 100, paying 500. The account now holds 500 in cash and 5 units.

  • Price falls 50%, to 50. The 5 units are worth 250. The account is worth 750, down 25%. Nothing is closed and nothing is owed. The trader still owns 5 units.
  • Price rises 20%, to 120. The 5 units are worth 600. The account is worth 1,100, up 10%.

In both cases the account moved by half as much as the price, because half of it was in the asset. Had the price gone to zero, the loss would have been the 500 paid and no more. Fees are ignored.

What it does NOT mean

  • Spot does not mean safe. The price of the asset can fall a long way, and the whole amount paid is exposed.
  • No liquidation does not mean a loss cannot be total. It means a loss is not forced by a lender. The asset can still become worthless.
  • A balance on an exchange is not the same as holding the asset yourself. It is a claim on a company.
  • The spot price is not one global number. Each exchange has its own market, and prices differ slightly between them.

Common mistakes

  1. Equating “no leverage” with “no risk”. Spot removes forced closure, not price risk.
  2. Mixing up base and quote. An order for “100” can mean 100 units or 100 worth, depending on which field is filled in.
  3. Overlooking the costs around the trade. Spread, trading fees and withdrawal or network fees all apply.
  4. Assuming spot features on a margin account. Once borrowing is switched on, positions can be liquidated even if the screen looks similar.

Risks and limitations

  • The full value of a spot holding moves with the market, and there is no floor above zero.
  • Assets left on an exchange depend on that exchange’s solvency and security.
  • Small or new markets can be illiquid, with wide spreads and large slippage.
  • Plain spot offers no way to gain from a falling price.

Key takeaway

Spot trading exchanges one asset for another, paid in full. The holder owns the asset and cannot be liquidated, and the whole amount paid is still exposed to the price.
