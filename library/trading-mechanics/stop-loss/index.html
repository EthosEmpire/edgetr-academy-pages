Stop Loss

A stop loss is a stop order placed in advance to close a position if price moves against it to a chosen level, limiting the loss approximately but not exactly.

Why it matters

A stop loss turns “I would get out if price reached this level” into an instruction that an exchange carries out by itself. It works while the trader is asleep, away, or hesitating.

It is also one of the most misread tools in trading. A stop loss sets a level at which an exit is attempted. It does not set the price of that exit, and it cannot promise a maximum loss.

What it looks like

A stop loss is not a separate order type. It is a stop order used for one purpose: closing a position that is losing.

  • For a long position it is a sell stop below the entry price.
  • For a short position it is a buy stop above the entry price.

The order stays dormant until the market reaches its trigger. Then it becomes a market order, or a limit order if it is a stop-limit, and the position is closed at the prices available. When this happens the trader is said to be stopped out.

In plain words

A stop loss is like the circuit breaker in a house. It does nothing until the current passes a threshold, then it cuts the power. It does not prevent the fault, and it may trip a moment after the surge began. Its job is to stop a problem from growing without limit.

What a stop loss defines

Three numbers are tied together: the entry price, the stop level and the position size. Once all three exist, the planned loss is arithmetic.

Planned loss = position size x distance from entry to stopThis is the loss if the stop fills exactly at its trigger. The real fill can differ.

The same distance with a larger position is a larger loss, and the same loss can come from a small position with a distant stop or a large position with a close one. How these numbers are balanced is the subject of position sizing and risk per trade.

Tight and wide stops

Prices fluctuate constantly, even inside a move that continues afterwards. A stop close to the entry sits inside more of that everyday fluctuation and is triggered more often. A stop far from the entry is triggered less often but loses more per unit when it is.

This page describes only what the order does. How practitioners reason about the distance is covered in stop placement.

Going deeper

Slippage and gaps through a stop

A standard stop loss releases a market order. In a calm market that order fills close to the trigger. In a fast market it can fill lower, and if price gaps past the level it fills at the first price traded on the other side. The difference is slippage.

Stop-market or stop-limit

A stop-limit version will not sell below a stated limit price. That removes the poor fill, and it adds a different outcome: if price jumps past the limit, the order does not fill and the losing position stays open.

Which price triggers it

On derivatives exchanges a stop can usually be set to trigger on the last traded price or on the mark price. The two can differ during sharp moves, so the same stop may trigger under one setting and not under the other.

Stop loss and liquidation

On a leveraged position there is a second level at which the position closes: the liquidation price, set by the exchange. A stop loss has an effect only if its trigger is reached before that level. See stop loss vs liquidation.

Mental stops

A “mental stop” is an intention to exit at a level without placing an order. Mechanically it is not a stop. Nothing executes unless the trader is present and acts.

One hypothetical stop loss, three outcomesHypothetical illustration

An account holds 1,000. A trader buys 5 units at 100, a position of 500, and places a sell stop at 95.

  • Orderly fall. Price slides to 95 and the stop fills at 95. The loss is 5% of the position, which is 2.5% of the account.
  • Gap. Price jumps from 96 to 93 on news. The stop fills at 93. The loss is 7% of the position, which is 3.5% of the account.
  • Brief spike. Price dips to 94.90 for a moment, the stop fills, and price then climbs to 110. The exit stands. The position was closed at a loss of about 5%.

The same order produced three different results. In none of them did it malfunction.

What it does NOT mean

  • A stop loss does not guarantee a maximum loss. It is designed to attempt an exit once the trigger is reached.
  • Being stopped out does not prove the idea was wrong. Price may reverse straight afterwards. A stop that holds does not prove the idea right either.
  • A tight stop is not automatically lower risk. The loss depends on distance multiplied by size, and close stops are hit more often.
  • A stop loss does not protect against liquidation by itself. It helps only if it triggers, and fills, before the liquidation price.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating the stop level as the exit price. The two match only in calm, liquid conditions.
  2. Thinking about stop distance without position size. The loss is the product of both.
  3. Moving the stop further away as price approaches it. The order then no longer limits the loss it was placed to limit.
  4. Leaving a stop active after the position is closed. Without a reduce-only setting it can open a new, unintended position.

Risks and limitations

  • Gaps and fast markets can produce a loss larger than planned.
  • Ordinary volatility can trigger a stop shortly before price moves the other way.
  • A stop-limit can fail to fill and leave the position open.
  • Stops depend on the exchange operating normally. Outages and extreme conditions can delay or prevent execution.

Key takeaway

A stop loss is a pre-set stop order that tries to close a losing position at a chosen level. It limits loss approximately: the trigger is the plan, but whether and where it fills is not certain.
