Resistance

Resistance is a price area above the market where selling interest has repeatedly been strong enough to pause or reverse an advance. It may hold again, and it may not.

Why it matters

When a rising market stops rising, it often stops at a height it has struggled with before. That height is called resistance.

Resistance is the mirror image of support, and the two are usually taught together. It deserves a separate look because the selling behind it has its own logic, and because breaking through resistance is what every advance has to do in order to continue.

What it looks like

Resistance is an area above the current price where selling interest has repeatedly been strong enough to absorb buying. Advances that reach it have paused or turned down.

Like support, it is a band of prices and not one exact number. See zones, not lines.

In plain words

At an auction, bidding on similar items keeps stalling around the same amount. Nobody has set a maximum. It is simply the point where enough bidders drop out and enough owners are happy to sell. Next week, with different people in the room, the price might sail past it.

How it works

Price rises while buyers are more urgent than sellers. It stops when that balance changes.

The middle group is the one most particular to resistance. People who bought near a previous high and then watched price fall have spent the whole decline at a loss. When price finally returns to where they bought, many are relieved to exit without a loss, and they sell. Classical chart writers call this stock of potential sellers overhead supply. The behaviour is closely related to loss aversion.

Where resistance tends to form:

Source Why traders watch it
A previous swing high Price visibly turned down there before
A former support area A broken floor may act as a ceiling. See role reversal.
A round number Orders cluster at prices such as 100 or 1,000
A falling moving average or trend line A moving reference above price

How to read it

A test of resistance that fails to break through often leaves long upper wicks: price traded higher during the period and was pushed back before the close.

The shooting star is a named candle with this shape. It records one rejection. It does not say whether the next attempt fails too.

When price does close above a resistance area, the move is called a breakout.

Two things practitioners commonly compare between tests:

  • The lows between attempts. If each pullback from resistance ends higher than the last, buyers are returning sooner each time. This is the idea behind the ascending triangle pattern.
  • Closes versus wicks. A wick above the area that closes back below it is a different event from a close above.

Going deeper

Resistance in a trend. An uptrend is, by definition, a market that keeps breaking resistance, since each higher high exceeds a previous swing high. In a downtrend, each lower high forms a new resistance area at a lower price.

No overhead history. When a market trades at a price it has never reached before, there are no earlier buyers waiting to break even and no old swing highs above. Chart-based resistance does not exist there. Traders in that situation often turn to round numbers as reference points.

A return to an old highHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market rallied to 80 three months ago, fell to 62 and is now rising again.

  • Many trades took place between 78 and 80 on the first visit. Buyers from that visit have been at a loss ever since.
  • Price climbs back to 79.2. For three sessions the candles show long upper wicks, and then price falls to 74. The area acted as resistance.
  • A month later price returns and closes at 82.5, which is 2.5 above the top of the band. The area has been broken.

Both outcomes started the same way, with price entering the 78 to 80 band. After the second, the band is no longer overhead. It is now below price, where traders commonly watch it as possible support.

What it does NOT mean

  • Resistance is not a ceiling that cannot break. Every advance to a new high has passed through one.
  • Resistance is not a sell signal. Price reaching an area says where it is, not what to do.
  • A rejection does not mean the advance is over. Markets often need several attempts before an area gives way.
  • An old high is not automatically resistance. Overhead supply is thought to fade as trapped holders sell or stop watching.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating it as a wall. Resistance describes past selling. It does not prevent future buying.
  2. Counting a wick as a breakout. A brief push above the area that closes back inside it has not been accepted by the market.
  3. Ignoring the lows. Watching only the ceiling misses whether pullbacks from it are getting shallower or deeper.
  4. Forgetting the trend. In a clear uptrend, resistance areas have a recent record of giving way.

Risks and limitations

  • The explanation for resistance is a model of crowd behaviour. Charts cannot show who is actually selling or why.
  • Where the band is drawn is subjective, so whether a move counts as a break can depend on the reader.
  • Moves above resistance that quickly fail are common. See fakeouts.

Key takeaway

Resistance is an area where selling has repeatedly been enough to stop an advance. It marks where a reaction may occur, and every uptrend is a sequence of resistance areas that eventually gave way.
