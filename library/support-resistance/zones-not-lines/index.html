Zones, Not Lines

Support and resistance are better drawn as zones, bands of price, than as single lines, because markets rarely turn at exactly the same price twice.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Charting tools make it easy to click once and draw a perfectly straight horizontal line. The line looks precise, and that is the problem. Markets are not precise. Price turns near a level, a little above it one time and a little below it the next.

A reader who expects exact prices ends up confused by ordinary behaviour: every small overshoot looks like a break, and every turn that stops short looks like a different level. Thinking in zones removes most of that confusion.

What it looks like

A zone is a band of prices, with an upper edge and a lower edge, that contains the cluster of turning points at a support or resistance area.

In plain words

“Meet me at the station” works. “Meet me at the third paving stone from the left of the main entrance” does not, because nobody arrives with that kind of precision. A level on a chart is a meeting place for many buyers and sellers. They gather around it, not on a single spot.

Why turning points scatter

Several ordinary features of markets spread the turns across a range of prices:

  • Orders are spread out. Different participants place their orders at different prices around the same idea. One buys at 101, another waits for 100, a third for 99.5.
  • Wicks and closes disagree. The lowest traded price of a turn and the lowest close are different numbers, and both are meaningful.
  • Prices differ between venues. The same asset trades at slightly different prices on different exchanges, so the “exact” low depends on the data feed.
  • Conditions change. A calm market might turn within a fraction of a percent of the last low. A volatile one can overshoot by much more before reversing.

How to read it

The same three lows tell two different stories depending on how the level is drawn. The zone reading is usually closer to what happened: buying interest appeared in the same area three times.

Drawing a zone. There is no single correct method. Two common conventions are:

Convention Lower and upper edge (for support) Character
Wick to body From the lowest wick of the turns to the lowest candle closes Uses the candles themselves to set the width
Cluster From the lowest to the highest of the turning points Simple, and widens as more tests are added

Reading price inside and outside it. While price is inside the zone, the area is being tested and the outcome is open. A close beyond the far edge is commonly read as a break. A wick beyond the far edge that closes back inside is commonly read as a test that held, though not everyone agrees.

Width. A zone should be wide enough to contain the turns and narrow enough to mean something. Many traders scale it to recent volatility, for example by comparing it with the ATR, which measures the typical size of recent candles. Zones on higher timeframes are naturally wider, because the candles that formed them are larger.

Going deeper

A zone has an inside. With a line, price is either above or below. With a zone there is a third state, inside, and it deserves its own patience. A great deal of indecisive, choppy trading happens within zones.

Zones make breaks harder to call, on purpose. Requiring price to clear the whole band before calling a break filters out many of the brief spikes that reverse. It does not filter out all of them. See fakeouts.

Lines still have a use. A single line is a reasonable shorthand for the middle of a zone, as long as it is remembered that the line stands for an area. Most charting platforms also offer a rectangle tool. See horizontal levels and zones.

From three lows to a zoneHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market turns up at 100.5, later at 99.0, and later still at 101.5.

Drawn as a line at 100.5:

  • The second low is 1.5 below the line, which looks like a break.
  • The third low is 1.0 above the line, which looks like the level was never reached.

Drawn as a zone from 99.0 to 101.5:

  • The zone is 2.5 wide, about 2.5 percent of the price (2.5 divided by 100).
  • All three lows fall inside it.

Now suppose a later candle closes at 98.2. That is 0.8 below the lower edge of the zone. Under the common close-based convention, this is the first event that counts as a break. Under the line reading, the “break” would have been called at the second low, two tests too early.

What it does NOT mean

  • A zone is not more accurate than the market. It is an honest way of drawing something that is imprecise by nature.
  • A wider zone is not a safer one. Make it wide enough and price is always inside it, which says nothing.
  • Price inside a zone is not a signal. It means the area is being tested and the result is not yet known.
  • A zone holding does not mean it holds next time. Every test is a separate event.

Common mistakes

  1. Expecting a turn to the exact tick. This turns normal variation into a string of imagined breaks and misses.
  2. Drawing zones so wide they overlap. If support and resistance bands cover most of the chart, they no longer distinguish anything.
  3. Using one fixed width everywhere. A width that suits a quiet market is far too narrow for a volatile one.
  4. Redrawing after every candle. Adjusting the edges to fit each new wick means the zone can never be broken, which makes it useless.

Risks and limitations

  • Zone edges are subjective. Two readers can draw different bands and disagree about whether a break occurred.
  • A zone makes break calls later, which avoids some false breaks and also delays recognition of real ones.
  • No method of drawing changes the underlying uncertainty about whether an area holds.

Key takeaway

Support and resistance are areas, so they are best drawn as zones with an upper and lower edge. A zone tolerates the small overshoots and near misses that are normal, without pretending to a precision the market does not have.
