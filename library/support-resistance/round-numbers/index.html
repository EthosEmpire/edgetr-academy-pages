Round Numbers & Psychological Levels

Round numbers such as 100 or 50,000 are prices that attract attention and orders simply because they are easy to think in. Traders often watch them as possible support or resistance.

Why it matters

Most support and resistance comes from the chart’s own history: places where price turned before. Round numbers are different. A price such as 100, 1,000 or 50,000 can attract attention before the market has ever traded there, purely because of how people think about numbers.

They are worth understanding because they are the one kind of level that exists without any price history, and because they are a clear example of psychology showing up on a chart.

What it looks like

A round number is a price with trailing zeros or another simple form: 10, 100, 1,000, or halves and quarters of those. When traders watch such a price as possible support or resistance, it is called a psychological level.

In plain words

Shops price things at 9.99 because 10 feels like a different category of expense. People set alarms for 7:00, not 6:53. Nobody is forced to treat round numbers as special. Almost everybody does, and markets are made of people and the orders they place.

How it works

Three ordinary behaviours stand behind the effect:

  • Mental shortcuts. People set goals, limits and expectations in round figures. This is related to anchoring, the tendency to lean on a prominent reference number.
  • Order placement. Someone deciding where to place a limit order, a stop or a profit target tends to choose a simple price. Thousands of such choices pile up at the same few numbers.
  • Attention. News headlines and social media mark the moment a well-known asset crosses a big round figure, which draws in still more participants.

Academic studies of order and price data in several markets have reported that orders and transaction prices do cluster at round numbers more than chance would suggest. Clustering of orders is not the same as a dependable price reaction, and the size of the effect varies between markets and over time.

How to read it

Roundness is relative. For an asset trading near 100, the numbers 50, 100 and 150 stand out. For one trading near 0.37, the round numbers are 0.35, 0.40 and 0.50. For one near 64,000, they are 60,000, 65,000 and 70,000. The rounder the figure, the more widely it tends to be watched.

They are zones. Reactions seldom occur at the exact figure. Some participants deliberately place orders a little before the round number in order to be filled ahead of the crowd, and stop orders are often placed a little beyond it. Reactions are therefore spread around the number. See zones, not lines.

They need evidence. A round number is a candidate level, not a proven one. It earns a place on the chart when price visibly reacts there. Many round numbers are crossed with no pause at all.

They combine with other levels. A round number that coincides with an old swing high or another reference is a case of confluence, and is generally given more weight than a round number with nothing else around it.

Going deeper

Uncharted territory. At prices a market has never reached, there is no chart-based resistance above, and round numbers are among the few references available.

Brief moves through them. Because stop orders tend to sit just beyond round numbers, a push through one can trigger a burst of orders and then fade. Round numbers are a common location for fakeouts.

Dependence on currency and unit. A price that is round in one currency is not round in another. An asset at exactly 100 in US dollars is at some untidy figure in euros. Roundness also depends on arbitrary features such as how a token’s supply was set or whether a stock was split. The effect belongs to whichever quote most participants are watching.

Three reactions around 100Hypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset rises from 88 toward 100 for the first time.

  • First approach. The high is 99.6. Price never trades at 100, and it falls back to 93.
  • Second approach. Price trades up to 101.1 and closes the candle at 99.2. It then falls to 96.
  • Break. Some time later a candle closes at 102.3, and price runs to 104.5.
  • Return. Price drifts back. The low is 99.7 and the close is 100.9. Price then rises to 109.

The reactions were at 99.6, 101.1 and 99.7. All are within about 1.1 of the round number and none is on it. A zone from roughly 99 to 101 describes what happened far better than a line at 100. In another version of this chart, price runs from 88 to 109 without pausing at 100 at all, and nothing beforehand could say which version was coming.

What it does NOT mean

  • A round number is not a barrier. It is a price that more people than usual are thinking about.
  • A round number is not a prediction. Reaching or crossing one says nothing about where price goes next.
  • Crossing a round number is not a buy or sell signal. The milestone is psychological. The market’s supply and demand are unchanged by a digit.
  • Not every round number matters. Most are crossed without any visible reaction.

Common mistakes

  1. Marking every round number. A chart ruled at every ten is as uninformative as one with no levels.
  2. Expecting the turn on the exact figure. Orders are spread around the number, and so are reactions.
  3. Giving a milestone meaning it does not have. A price of 100 is not fundamentally different from 99.
  4. Getting carried along by headlines. Attention around a big figure tends to peak exactly when price is there, which is a setting for FOMO.

Risks and limitations

  • The effect is a tendency in crowd behaviour and is inconsistent from one market, period and level to the next.
  • Because the levels are obvious to everyone, sharp short-lived moves through them are common.
  • A round number depends on the quote currency and the unit, both of which are arbitrary.

Key takeaway

Round numbers attract attention and orders because people think in simple figures. They can act as support or resistance, usually as a zone around the number, and many are passed without any reaction at all.
