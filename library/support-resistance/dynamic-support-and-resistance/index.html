Dynamic Support & Resistance

Dynamic support and resistance are levels that move with time, such as moving averages and trend lines, in contrast to horizontal levels that stay at a fixed price.

Why it matters

The support and resistance described so far on this shelf sit at fixed prices. Trending markets do not stay near fixed prices for long. In a steady advance, the old levels are soon far below, and chart readers look for something that travels with the trend.

Dynamic support and resistance fill that gap. They are reference levels that move: a moving average that rises under price, or a trend line that slopes along the lows.

What it looks like

A dynamic level acts as support when it is below price and pullbacks turn up near it. It acts as resistance when it is above price and rallies turn down near it. The two most common kinds are:

  • Moving averages, lines that plot the average price over a chosen number of recent candles. See moving averages.
  • Trend lines, straight lines drawn through successive swing lows or swing highs. See trend lines.

In plain words

A handrail on a staircase rises with the stairs. At every step it is at a different height from the ground, and at every step it is in about the same place relative to you. A horizontal level is a shelf fixed to the wall. A dynamic level is the handrail.

How it works

Moving averages. A moving average is recalculated on every candle, so its value keeps changing. In an uptrend it rises, lagging behind price. When price pulls back, it often meets the average coming up.

Two explanations are commonly offered for reactions there. The first is that the average represents the typical price paid over the period, so a return to it is a return to where recent participants, on average, stand at break-even. The second is simpler: certain averages are watched by very large numbers of traders, who place orders near them. Both explanations are interpretations.

Widely watched lengths include 20, 50, 100 and 200 periods. These are conventions, not optimised values. The simple (SMA) and exponential (EMA) versions of the same length sit at different prices, and no version is the correct one.

Trend lines. A rising trend line gives a different price on each candle because of its slope. It is drawn by hand, so two people often produce slightly different lines from the same chart.

Other moving references. VWAP, the volume-weighted average price, and the bands of channel indicators are read in a similar way by some traders.

How to read it

Situation Commonly read as
Price above a rising average, pullbacks turning near it The average has been acting as dynamic support
Price below a falling average, rallies turning near it The average has been acting as dynamic resistance
Price closing through an average it had respected A change of pace, or possibly of direction
Average flat, price crossing it back and forth A range. The average is not acting as a level at all.

Two points help keep the reading honest:

  • Dynamic levels are zones too. Price routinely dips a little through an average or a trend line and recovers. See zones, not lines.
  • Shorter means closer and weaker. A short average stays near price, is touched often and is broken often. A long average is touched rarely and is generally given more weight when it is.

Going deeper

Lag. A moving average is built entirely from past prices. It turns after price has turned. A dynamic level based on it is therefore always a description of where the trend has been.

Role reversal applies. An average or trend line that supported price and is then broken is often watched as resistance on the way back up. See role reversal.

Meeting a horizontal level. When a rising average or trend line passes through a horizontal support zone, two separate references coincide at one price. Traders call this confluence.

The settings problem. A reaction at a 37-period average found by trial and error means little. This is one reason practitioners tend to stick to a few conventional lengths.

How far a level can move in one candleHypothetical illustration

Take a hypothetical 5-period simple moving average. The last five closes are 100, 102, 104, 106 and 108.

  • Sum: 100 + 102 + 104 + 106 + 108 = 520.
  • Average: 520 divided by 5 = 104.

The dynamic level sits at 104. The next candle closes at 110. The oldest close, 100, drops out and 110 comes in.

  • New sum: 102 + 104 + 106 + 108 + 110 = 530.
  • New average: 530 divided by 5 = 106.

The level has moved from 104 to 106 in one candle, without any decision by anyone. A horizontal level drawn at 104 would not have moved at all.

What it does NOT mean

  • A moving average is not a floor or a ceiling. It is an arithmetic summary of past prices, and price crosses it freely.
  • A touch of an average or trend line is not a buy or sell signal. It is a location where a reaction has sometimes occurred.
  • Past reactions at an average do not make it special. With many possible lengths, some will always fit the recent past.
  • A break of a dynamic level is not a trend reversal. The trend is defined by swing highs and lows, not by one line.

Common mistakes

  1. Using them in a range. A flat average in a sideways market is crossed constantly and marks nothing.
  2. Fitting the average to the chart. Adjusting the length until past pullbacks line up proves only that the adjusting worked.
  3. Stacking many averages. A chart with eight lines guarantees that price is always “at” one of them.
  4. Forgetting the lag. The level reflects past prices and reacts slowly to a sudden change.

Risks and limitations

  • Moving averages are calculated from price, so they do not add independent information to the chart.
  • The same average can differ slightly between platforms, because of different data feeds, session times or calculation details.
  • Trend lines are subjective, and their exact price on a given candle depends on how they were drawn.

Key takeaway

Dynamic support and resistance are moving levels, mainly moving averages and trend lines, that travel with a trend. They are useful references in trending markets, approximate by nature, and no more binding on price than any other level.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of Stock Trends, Robert D. Edwards and John Magee. First published in 1948; trend lines and channels as moving support and resistance
