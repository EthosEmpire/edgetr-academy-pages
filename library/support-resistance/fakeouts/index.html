Fakeouts

A fakeout is a move beyond a watched support or resistance area that quickly fails, with price returning back through the level. It can only be identified after it has happened.

  • 5 min read

Why it matters

Every page about breakouts carries the same warning: many of them fail. A fakeout is what that failure looks like. Price pushes beyond a level that everyone was watching, appears to have broken it, and then comes straight back.

Fakeouts are not rare exceptions that spoil an otherwise reliable idea. They are a normal and frequent part of how markets behave around obvious levels, and understanding why they happen is as important as understanding breakouts themselves.

What it looks like

A fakeout is a move beyond a watched area that quickly fails. Price trades, or even closes, beyond the level and then returns back through it.

  • A failed break above resistance is often called a bull trap, because buyers who acted on the breakout are caught when price falls.
  • A failed break below support is often called a bear trap, because sellers are caught when price recovers.

“Quickly” has no fixed definition. Most people have in mind a reversal within a few candles on the timeframe they are watching. Other names include false breakout and failed break.

In plain words

A crowd waits at a pedestrian crossing. One person steps out, a few follow, and then a car appears and everyone scrambles back to the kerb. The step forward was real. It simply was not the start of everyone crossing.

Why fakeouts happen

Several ordinary explanations are offered, and more than one can apply at once.

  • No follow-through. The break is powered by stop orders and breakout orders clustered just beyond the level. Once they are filled, no further buying or selling arrives at the new prices, and the move runs out.
  • Orders gather at obvious places. Because so many orders sit just beyond well-known levels, those areas offer a lot of liquidity. A large participant who wants to sell in size can do so more easily into a burst of breakout buying. Traders describe the resulting spike as a stop hunt or liquidity sweep. The chart cannot show anyone’s intent, so this remains an interpretation.
  • Thin markets. When few orders are resting in the book, a modest order can push price through a level without any real change in supply and demand.
  • News. A headline can drive price through a level and be reversed minutes later as the market reconsiders.

How to read it

A fakeout and a retest begin the same way. In both, price breaks a level and comes back to it. The difference is what happens there: in a retest the level holds from its new side, and in a fakeout price goes back through.

Conventions that traders use to reduce the number of fakeouts they are caught by:

Convention Idea Cost
Wait for the close A wick beyond the level does not count Later recognition of real breaks
Use a zone Price has to clear the whole band Later recognition of real breaks
Ask for more than one close The move has to persist Later still

Each filter removes some fakeouts and delays the reading of genuine breaks. None removes them all, because a fakeout can last several candles before it fails.

Going deeper

Trapped traders. People who entered on the break are at a loss once price returns through the level. Many exit, and their exits push price further in the direction of the failure. For this reason, moves that follow a failed break are often described as sharp. That is a tendency traders report, not a dependable outcome.

The same event on two timeframes. A fakeout that takes three candles on an hourly chart may be a single candle with a long wick on a daily chart. Much of what is called rejection on a higher timeframe is a fakeout on a lower one.

Patterns fail the same way. See pattern failure and invalidation.

A breakout that lasts two candlesHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical resistance zone sits between 114 and 116. It has turned price back three times.

  • Candle A closes at 119.5, above the zone. By the close-based convention this is a breakout.
  • Candle B closes at 117. Still above the zone, though lower.
  • Candle C closes at 113, which is 1 below the lower edge of the zone.

Within two candles of the break, price is back under the area it broke. Candle A is now described as a fakeout. Over the following sessions price falls to 104.

A reader who required a close beyond the zone was still caught, because candle A did close beyond it. A reader who required two closes beyond the zone would also have counted candle B. Only hindsight labels candle A correctly. At the time it was a valid breakout by every common rule.

What it does NOT mean

  • A fakeout is not proof of manipulation. Breaks fail for mundane reasons, and a chart cannot show intent.
  • A fakeout is not a signal to trade the other way. Price that fails at a level can turn around and break it on the next attempt.
  • A fakeout does not mean the level is strong. It means one attempt failed.
  • Fakeouts cannot be identified in advance. The label is applied after price has already returned through the level.

Common mistakes

  1. Believing a rule can remove them. Closes, zones and volume improve the context and do not eliminate false breaks.
  2. Calling every failed move a stop hunt. Attributing intent to each wick explains nothing and cannot be checked.
  3. Confusing a retest with a fakeout. A return to the level is not a failure until price passes back through it.
  4. Judging on a timeframe that is too small. Very short timeframes produce constant false breaks of minor levels.

Risks and limitations

  • A fakeout is defined only in hindsight, so the concept has no predictive content on its own.
  • There is no agreed threshold for how far or how long a break must last before a reversal stops being called a fakeout.
  • Around fakeouts, price moves quickly in both directions, and orders can fill at prices well away from the level.

Key takeaway

A fakeout is a break of a watched level that quickly reverses. It is common, it has ordinary causes, and it is recognised only afterwards, which is why a single move beyond a level is weak evidence on its own.
