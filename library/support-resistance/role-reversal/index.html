Role Reversal

Role reversal is the tendency for a broken resistance area to act later as support, and for a broken support area to act later as resistance. It is a common observation, not a rule.

Why it matters

A resistance area that has just been broken does not stop mattering. Chart readers have long observed that it often comes back into play from the other side: price returns to it and finds buyers where it used to find sellers.

This idea, role reversal, connects several pages on this shelf. It is the reason traders pay attention to a retest, and one of the ways people judge whether a breakout is holding.

What it looks like

Role reversal has two forms:

  • a broken resistance area may later act as support;
  • a broken support area may later act as resistance.

The area is the same. What changes is which side of it price is on, and therefore which role it plays. Other names for the idea include the polarity principle and an S/R flip.

In plain words

Climb through a hatch from one storey of a building to the next. The surface that was the ceiling above your head is now the floor under your feet. It is the same slab of concrete. Your position relative to it is what changed.

How it works

Classical technical analysis explains role reversal through the positions people hold. Take a resistance area that breaks to the upside:

  • People who sold or went short there are now losing or regretting it. If price returns to their entry, many are glad to buy and get out near break-even.
  • People who wanted to buy and missed the breakout get a second chance at the price they were watching.
  • People who already bought the breakout may add at a level that has just proved significant.

All three groups have a reason to buy at the old resistance area. Flip every word and the same reasoning explains why broken support may act as resistance: buyers who are now at a loss sell to get out even, and new sellers join them.

How to read it

Before the break The break After the break
Area is above price and has turned advances back Price closes above the area Area is below price and is watched as possible support
Area is below price and has turned declines back Price closes below the area Area is above price and is watched as possible resistance

A few points that practitioners commonly consider:

  • How clear the break was. A decisive close well beyond the area leaves more participants on the wrong side than a marginal one. Some traders ask for a minimum distance or more than one close before treating the area as broken.
  • How significant the area was. An area that turned price many times, on a higher timeframe, involved more people and so is thought to leave a stronger memory.
  • How price returns. A slow, small-candled drift back is often read differently from a fast, heavy move back, which may suggest the break itself is failing.

Going deeper

When the reversal fails. If price returns to a broken resistance area and closes back below it, the area has not acted as support. The breakout has failed, at least for now, and the move is often labelled a fakeout in hindsight. This outcome is common.

No return at all. Price frequently breaks a level and never comes back to it. Role reversal describes what may happen if price returns. It does not say that it returns.

In trends. Role reversal is part of how trends are built. In an uptrend, a broken swing high often becomes the area where the next higher low forms. It is one of several observations that can coincide at a single price, a situation described as confluence. Role reversal is also applied to trend lines and moving averages.

One level, two roles, and a failure caseHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market is rejected at 115 and again at 115.5. The band from 114.5 to 117 is marked as resistance.

  • Price then closes at 120, above the band, and runs to 124.
  • It drifts back down and reaches 116.5, inside the old band. Candles there show long lower wicks, and price rises to 128.

The area that turned price down twice has now turned it up once. That is role reversal.

Now take the alternative. Instead of holding at 116.5, price closes at 113, which is 1.5 below the lower edge of the band. The old resistance did not act as support. Price is back where it was before the breakout, and the area is overhead again. Both paths looked identical until the moment price reached the band.

What it does NOT mean

  • Role reversal is not a law. Broken levels fail to hold from the other side all the time.
  • A return to a broken level is not a buy or sell signal. It is a test whose result is not known in advance.
  • A successful flip does not make the level permanent. It can be broken again on the next visit.
  • The explanation is not proof. The story about regretful sellers is plausible and cannot be verified from a chart.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming every broken level flips. The tendency is real enough to have a name and unreliable enough to fail often.
  2. Expecting an exact touch. The reversed area is still a zone. Price may turn short of it or push some way into it.
  3. Applying it to trivial levels. A minor high from an hour ago has few participants attached to it and little memory.
  4. Forgetting the failure case. A close back through the area changes the reading entirely.

Risks and limitations

  • Whether an area “held” in its new role depends on how the zone was drawn and whether wicks or closes are used.
  • Fast markets can pass back through a broken level with no pause.
  • The idea is widely known, so many orders gather around obvious broken levels, which itself can produce brief moves through them.

Key takeaway

A broken resistance area may later act as support, and a broken support area may later act as resistance. The tendency is common enough to watch for and unreliable enough that each return to the level remains an open test.
