Retests

A retest is price returning to a level it has just broken, approaching it from the other side. It shows whether the broken area holds in its new role, and it does not always happen.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A breakout answers one question, whether the level gave way, and immediately raises another: does the break hold? A retest is the market asking that second question out loud. Price comes back to the level it broke and tests it from the other side.

Many traders pay close attention to retests because they offer a second look at a level under new conditions. It is equally important to know that plenty of breaks are never retested, and that a retest can fail.

What it looks like

A retest is price returning to a broken level:

  • after a break above resistance, price comes back down to the area, which is now below it;
  • after a break below support, price comes back up to the area, which is now above it.

The idea rests on role reversal: a broken resistance area may act as support, and a broken support area may act as resistance. The retest is the moment that possibility is tested.

In plain words

You step onto a frozen pond and the ice holds. Before walking further, you shift your weight and press down again on the same spot. If it holds a second time you have learned a little more. You still have not learned anything about the ice ten metres ahead.

Why retests happen

Several ordinary forces can pull price back toward a level it has just broken:

  • The burst fades. Stop orders and breakout orders that powered the break have been filled.
  • Profit taking. Traders who were positioned before the break close some of their positions into the move.
  • Counter-trend interest. Others regard the move as overdone and trade against it.

If the same level then attracts the buyers (or sellers) described on the role reversal page, price turns there. If it does not, price continues back through.

How to read it

Things chart readers commonly look at during a retest:

Observation Commonly read as
Price reaches the area and closes back outside it, in the direction of the break The level held on this visit
Long wicks into the area. See wicks and rejection. The prices inside the area were rejected
Small candles and lighter volume on the way back An unhurried return, not aggressive counter-pressure
Large candles on the way back and a close through the area The break is failing. See fakeouts.

The level is a zone on the way back just as it was on the way out. A retest may stop a little short of the area or push some way into it.

Going deeper

Not every break is retested. Strong moves frequently leave a level and do not return to it for a long time, if ever. Anyone who waits for a retest as extra context accepts that some moves proceed without one. No rule says how often retests occur, and it varies with the market and the timeframe.

Timing varies. A retest can come on the very next candle or weeks later, and a level can be revisited more than once.

Retests and structure. In an uptrend, the retest of a broken swing high is often where the next higher low forms. In that sense a retest is a pullback with a specific destination.

Other names. Some classical texts call a return to a level broken upward a throwback and a return to a level broken downward a pullback. Modern usage often says “retest” for both.

Confirmation. Many traders wait for a candle to close away from the level before treating a retest as having held, the same idea as confirmation in candlestick reading.

The same retest with two endingsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market has been turned back twice near 113.5. It then closes at 118 and runs to 121. The level is broken.

Price drifts back. A candle trades down to 113.1 and closes at 114.6.

Ending one. The next candles close at 116 and 119, and price goes on to 129. The level was approached from above and held. With hindsight this was a successful retest.

Ending two. Price bounces only to 117 and then closes at 111.5, which is 2 below the level. The old resistance did not act as support. The breakout has been undone, and the earlier move to 121 is now described as a fakeout.

In both endings the retest candle is the same: a low of 113.1 and a close of 114.6. One candle holding at a level is a single observation, and the following candles decided which story it belonged to.

What it does NOT mean

  • A retest is not a second chance that the market owes anyone. Many breaks never come back to the level.
  • A retest that holds is not a guarantee. The level can fail on a later visit, or price can stall soon after.
  • A retest is not a buy or sell signal. It is a test of a level, and tests have two possible results.
  • Touching the level is not the same as holding it. The outcome is only known once price has moved away again.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming a retest always comes. Expecting one after every break leads to waiting for something that often never arrives.
  2. Calling the hold too early. A single candle with a wick at the level has not yet shown that the level held.
  3. Demanding an exact touch. Returns that stop slightly short of the level, or dip slightly through it, are normal.
  4. Ignoring how price returns. A fast, heavy move back into the level is a different situation from a slow drift.

Risks and limitations

  • A retest can only be judged after the fact. While price sits at the level, both outcomes remain open.
  • Whether the level “held” depends on how the zone was drawn and on whether wicks or closes are used.
  • Because the break-and-retest idea is widely known, many orders gather around retest areas, which can itself produce sharp moves through them.

Key takeaway

A retest is price returning to a broken level from the other side. It may show the level holding in its new role, it may fail, and it may never occur at all.
