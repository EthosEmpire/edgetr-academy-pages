Long Position

A long position is one that gains value if the price rises and loses value if it falls. It can be as simple as owning an asset, or it can be a leveraged contract with very different risks.

Why it matters

A long position is the most familiar position there is. Anyone who has bought something hoping it would be worth more later has been long. In market language, being long an asset means holding a position that gains value when the price rises and loses value when it falls.

The idea is simple. The detail that matters is that “long” covers very different arrangements. Owning a coin outright and holding a leveraged long contract point the same way, and they do not carry the same risk.

How it works

A long has three stages. It is opened by buying. It is held while price moves. It is closed by selling the same amount. The result is the difference between the two prices, multiplied by the quantity.

Profit or loss = (exit price - entry price) x quantityBefore fees and any funding or interest.

In plain words

Buying a bicycle for 100 and selling it later is a long. Sell it for 130 and the result is a gain of 30. Sell it for 80 and the result is a loss of 20. Until it is sold, the gain or loss exists only on paper.

While the position is open, the gain or loss is unrealized. It becomes realized only when the position is closed. The difference is explained in P&L: Realized & Unrealized.

How to read it: spot long and leveraged long

Spot long. In spot trading, the buyer pays the full price and receives the asset. There is no borrowing. The worst outcome is that the asset becomes worthless, so the largest loss is the amount paid. Nobody can force the position to close.

Leveraged long. With leverage, the trader puts in a fraction of the position’s value, called margin, and the rest is borrowed or provided through a contract. Gains and losses are calculated on the full position. If losses use up the margin, the exchange closes the position. This forced close is liquidation.

Perpetual long. A long on perpetual futures is a contract that tracks the price. No asset is delivered, so there is nothing to withdraw to a wallet. Longs and shorts exchange periodic funding payments. When the funding rate is positive, longs pay shorts. When it is negative, shorts pay longs. Exact rules differ between exchanges.

Going deeper

Leverage shrinks the room for error. The higher the leverage, the smaller the fall that uses up the margin. This is why a leveraged long can be closed at a loss during an ordinary pullback, even if the price later rises above the entry.

Being long is not an opinion. A person can be long because of a view, by habit, as part of a savings plan, or as one half of a hedge. The position says which way the account is exposed. It does not say why.

Costs reduce the result. Every long pays trading fees on the way in and out. Leveraged and perpetual longs add interest or funding for as long as they stay open.

A hypothetical long, with and without leverageHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset trades at 100. A trader has 200 to use.

Spot. The trader buys 2 units for 200.

  • Price rises to 115: unrealized gain of 15 x 2 = 30, or 15% of the 200.
  • Price falls to 90: unrealized loss of 10 x 2 = 20, or 10% of the 200.

5x leverage. The same 200 is posted as margin for a position of 1,000, which is 10 units.

  • Price rises to 115: gain of 15 x 10 = 150, or 75% of the 200.
  • Price falls to 90: loss of 10 x 10 = 100, or 50% of the 200.
  • A fall of about 20%, to 80, would produce a loss of 200, the whole margin. In practice the exchange liquidates earlier, because it keeps a safety buffer and charges fees.

The price path is the same in both cases. Only the structure of the position differs.

What it does NOT mean

  • Long does not mean bullish. Bullish is a view or a market condition. Long is a position. See the four words explained.
  • Long does not mean long-term. A long can be held for seconds or for years.
  • A long is not the safe direction. A spot long can lose most of its value, and a leveraged long can be liquidated quickly.
  • A perpetual long is not ownership. It is a contract that follows the price. No asset is held.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating a leveraged long like a spot long. A spot holder can wait through a deep fall. A leveraged position may not survive it.
  2. Counting open gains as banked. Until a position is closed, the number on the screen is provisional.
  3. Ignoring costs. Fees, funding and interest all reduce the result, and they add up over time.

Risks and limitations

  • A spot long is exposed to the full fall in price, and to the risk that the asset never recovers.
  • Leveraged and perpetual longs add liquidation risk, funding or interest costs, and dependence on the exchange’s rules.
  • In fast markets, a position may be closed at a worse price than expected because of slippage.

Key takeaway

A long position gains when price rises and loses when it falls. A spot long can lose at most what was paid, while a leveraged or perpetual long can be liquidated on a much smaller move, so the word “long” alone does not describe the risk.
