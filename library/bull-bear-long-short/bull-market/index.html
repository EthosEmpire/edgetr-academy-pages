Bull Market & Bullish

A bull market is a sustained period of generally rising prices, and bullish describes an upward condition or interpretation. Both are descriptions, not instructions to hold a long.

Why it matters

“Bull market” is one of the first phrases a newcomer hears, and it is usually said with excitement. The phrase itself is plain. It describes a market in which prices have been rising, broadly and for a sustained period.

The word matters because of what people attach to it. A description of conditions is easily heard as a reason to act, and it is not one. This page explains what the term describes, how loosely it is defined, and where its meaning stops.

What it looks like

On a chart, a bull market is a long uptrend: a series of higher highs and higher lows that persists for months or longer on a daily or weekly chart. The rise is never a straight line. It is interrupted by pullbacks and corrections, some of them sharp.

In plain words

Think of a rising tide. Every wave runs up the beach and slides back again, so at any moment the water may be going out. Over a few hours, the waterline still ends up higher. A bull market is the tide. The daily moves are the waves.

How to read it: two uses of one word

The noun and the adjective are used differently.

Term What it describes Scale
Bull market A sustained, broad rise in prices Months to years
Bullish (condition) Price behaviour that points upward Any timeframe, which should be stated
Bullish (opinion) Someone’s expectation or interpretation Personal, and open to disagreement

A single candle can be called bullish. So can a chart pattern, a week, or a person’s mood. None of these is a bull market. The difference is covered further in Bullish vs Bearish.

The 20 percent convention

In traditional markets such as stock indexes, a widely repeated rule of thumb calls a rise of 20 percent or more from a recent low a bull market, and a fall of 20 percent or more from a recent high a bear market.

This is a convention used by commentators. It is not a law and no authority announces the start or end of a bull market. It also fits some markets poorly. A volatile crypto asset can move 20 percent in a few days, so in crypto the term is normally used more loosely, for a rise lasting many months.

Going deeper

It is recognised late. A bull market is identified by looking back at what price has done. By the time the label is widely used, much of the rise it describes has already happened. The label says nothing about how much longer the conditions will last.

It is not uniform. In a broad bull market many assets rise, and some do not. A rising index or a rising Bitcoin price does not mean every stock or token is rising with it.

Sentiment tends to follow price. Rising prices attract attention, and attention often brings optimism and later FOMO, the fear of missing out. These moods are described in Fear & Greed and in the model on the Market Cycles page.

Leverage often builds. In derivative markets, long rises are often accompanied by more borrowed exposure. That can make the pullbacks inside a bull market faster and deeper, because leveraged positions are closed by force when price falls.

Pullbacks inside a hypothetical bull marketHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset moves through these swing points over a year: low 50, high 80, low 68, high 110, low 92, high 130.

  • From 50 to 130 the rise is 80, or 160%.
  • The fall from 80 to 68 is 12, or 15%.
  • The fall from 110 to 92 is 18, or about 16%.

Each low is higher than the previous low and each high is higher than the previous high, so the structure fits the description of a bull market. Someone who looked only at the weeks from 110 to 92 saw a market falling by about 16%. Both descriptions are true at their own timeframe. Nothing in the first five swing points made the sixth one certain.

What it does NOT mean

  • A bull market is not an instruction to open a long. It describes conditions. Taking a long position is a separate decision with its own risk.
  • Bullish does not mean prices rise every day. Falls of 10 to 20 percent, and larger ones in crypto, occur inside periods later called bull markets.
  • A bull market does not make risk smaller. A leveraged long can be liquidated during a pullback while the larger trend stays intact.
  • The label does not say how long conditions will last. It is a description of the past and the present.

Common mistakes

  1. Confusing a condition with a position. “The market is bullish” and “I am long” are different statements.
  2. Mixing timeframes. A bullish hourly chart can sit inside a weekly decline, and the reverse.
  3. Reading the label as a forecast. A trend is in place until it is not, and the change is clear only afterwards.
  4. Mistaking a rising market for skill. When most prices rise, many long positions show gains whatever the reasoning behind them. This feeds overconfidence.

Risks and limitations

  • There is no single agreed definition. Two analysts can label the same period differently.
  • The term is usually applied in hindsight, which limits its use for decisions in the moment.
  • Conventions built for stock indexes transfer poorly to assets with much higher volatility.

Key takeaway

A bull market is a sustained, broad rise in prices, and bullish is the matching adjective for conditions or opinions. Both describe. Neither says what will happen next, and neither is a reason by itself to hold a long.
