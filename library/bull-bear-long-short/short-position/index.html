Short Position

A short position is one that gains value if the price falls and loses value if it rises. It relies on borrowing or on a derivative contract, and its possible loss is not capped.

Why it matters

A short position gains value when the price falls and loses value when it rises. It is the mirror of a long position, and it is how a market allows people to express a negative view or to offset something they own.

Shorts confuse beginners for two reasons. The first is the mechanics: selling something that is not owned. The second is the risk, which is shaped differently from the risk of buying. This page explains both.

Risk

A short position can lose more than the money put into it, because a price has no upper limit. Shorts almost always involve margin, borrowing or leverage, and they can be closed by force at a loss. This page explains the mechanism. It is not a suggestion to short anything.

How it works

A short reverses the usual order. It is opened by selling. It is closed by buying back the same amount. If the buy-back price is lower than the sale price, the difference is a gain. If it is higher, the difference is a loss.

Profit or loss = (entry price - exit price) x quantityBefore fees, borrowing costs and funding.

In plain words

A friend lends you a textbook that sells for 100. You sell it today for 100, promising to return an identical copy next month. If the book then costs 70, you buy one, hand it back, and keep 30. If it costs 150, you still owe the book, and replacing it costs you 50 more than you received.

How to read it: two ways to be short

Borrowing. In traditional short selling, the trader borrows the asset from a broker or lender and sells it. Later the trader buys the same amount and returns it. A borrowing fee is charged while the loan is open, and the lender can set conditions, including asking for the asset back. The same idea exists in crypto through margin trading.

Perpetual contracts. On perpetual futures, nothing is borrowed. The trader opens a contract whose value rises as the price falls, and posts margin as collateral. This is how many crypto shorts are held.

Funding

Perpetual contracts use funding payments to keep the contract price near the spot price. When the funding rate is positive, longs pay shorts. When it is negative, shorts pay longs. A short can therefore receive funding in some periods and pay it in others. Rates and intervals differ between exchanges.

Margin and liquidation

The exchange holds the trader’s margin to cover losses. As price rises, the loss on a short grows and the remaining margin shrinks. If it falls below the exchange’s required minimum, the position is closed by force. This is liquidation, and for a short the liquidation price sits above the entry.

Going deeper: the asymmetry

A long bought without leverage can lose, at most, what was paid, because price cannot fall below zero. A short has the opposite shape. Its largest possible gain is fixed, reached if price goes to zero. Its possible loss has no ceiling, because price can keep rising.

In practice a short is closed or liquidated long before an extreme loss. Liquidation is still a loss, and in a fast market the closing price can be worse than expected. When many shorts are closed by force at once, their buying pushes price higher. That chain reaction is described on the Short Squeeze page.

A hypothetical perpetual shortHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset trades at 100. A trader opens a short of 1 unit on a perpetual contract and posts 20 as margin, which is 5x leverage.

  • Price falls to 90: gain of 10, which is 50% of the margin.
  • Price rises to 110: loss of 10, which is 50% of the margin.
  • Price rises to about 120: the loss would equal the whole margin. The exchange liquidates before that point, because it keeps a safety buffer and charges fees.

What it does NOT mean

  • Short does not mean bearish. Bearish is a view or a market condition. Short is a position, and it may be a hedge.
  • Short does not mean short-term. The word describes direction, not duration.
  • A falling market does not make a short safe. Sharp rallies occur inside declines and can liquidate leveraged shorts.
  • A stop order does not cap the loss with certainty. In a fast move, the closing price can be well beyond the stop level.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming the risk mirrors a long. The gain is capped and the loss is not.
  2. Forgetting the carrying costs. Borrowing fees, and funding when it runs against the short, add up for as long as the position is open.
  3. Looking for the liquidation price below the entry. For a short, it is above.
  4. Ignoring how crowded a short is. When many traders hold the same short, a rise can force them all to buy at once.

Risks and limitations

  • The possible loss is not capped, and leverage brings liquidation closer.
  • Costs are ongoing and can change: borrowing fees and funding rates move with market conditions.
  • Rules on short selling vary by market and jurisdiction, and some venues restrict it.

Key takeaway

A short position gains when price falls and loses when it rises. It depends on borrowing or on a derivative contract, it carries ongoing costs and liquidation risk, and unlike a spot long its possible loss has no upper limit.
