Why it matters

Four words carry most everyday market talk: bull, bear, long and short. Beginners often treat them as two ways of saying “up” and “down”. They are two different pairs that answer two different questions.

Bull and bear, with the adjectives bullish and bearish, describe a market’s condition or someone’s interpretation of it. Long and short describe a position, meaning something a person actually holds. Mixing the pairs leads to a quiet chain of errors, where “the market is bullish” turns into “so a long is the obvious thing” without anyone examining the step in between.

The four words in one picture

Read the figure by columns. The left column is about the world outside: what price has been doing, or what someone believes about it. The right column is about one person’s account.

In plain words

A weather report and an umbrella are related, and they are not the same thing. “Rainy” describes the sky. “Carrying an umbrella” describes what one person did. Someone can read a rainy forecast and stay indoors, and someone else can carry an umbrella on a sunny day.

How it works: three separate questions

What is the market doing? A bull market is a sustained period of generally rising prices. A bear market is a sustained period of generally falling prices. Markets also spend long stretches moving sideways in a range, which is neither.

What does someone think? Bullish and bearish are also used for opinions and interpretations: a bullish view, a bearish candle, a bullish reading of some news. People looking at the same chart regularly disagree. See Bullish vs Bearish.

What does someone hold? A long position gains value if price rises. A short position gains value if price falls. Holding neither is called being flat, which is covered in Cash & No Position.

How to read the words in a sentence

Sentence What kind of statement it is
“This has been a bull market for a year.” A description of past price behaviour
“I am bullish on the daily chart.” An opinion, tied to a timeframe
“That is a bearish candle.” A conventional label for a shape
“She is long.” A fact about a position
“They are flat.” A fact: no position is open

Bull and bear statements also belong to a timeframe. A market can be in a bull phase on the weekly chart while it falls for three days on the hourly chart.

Going deeper: every combination exists

Because conditions and positions are independent, all of the combinations occur:

  • Long in a bear market. Someone bought earlier and still holds, or bought during the decline.
  • Short in a bull market. Someone holds a short as a hedge against something they own, or holds a view that differs from the crowd’s.
  • Bullish and flat. An opinion with no position attached.

One more fact keeps the pairs apart. In a derivative market such as perpetual futures, every open contract has a long side and a short side. A bull market therefore cannot mean that “everyone is long”. The number of contracts held long always equals the number held short. What changes is the price, and which side is gaining.

One hypothetical market, three peopleHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset has risen from 100 to 160 over several months, with higher highs and higher lows on the daily chart. Most observers would describe that as bull-market behaviour.

  • Ana bought at 110 and still holds. She is long.
  • Ben thinks the rise has gone too far. He is bearish and has opened a short at 158.
  • Caro agrees with the bullish description and holds nothing. She is flat.

Over the next week price falls to 150. The daily structure is intact, so the description “bull market” has not changed. Ana’s open gain has shrunk from 50 to 40 per unit. Ben’s short shows a gain of 8 per unit. Caro’s account is unchanged.

One market and one description produced three positions and three results. Nothing in the word “bull” decided any of them.

What it does NOT mean

  • Bullish does not mean long. One is a view or a condition, the other is a position. A person can hold either without the other.
  • A bull market does not mean prices rise every day. It describes the larger direction over a stated period, with falls inside it.
  • Bearish does not mean bad. It is a direction. Falling prices hurt some positions and help others.
  • Short does not mean short-term, and long does not mean long-term. Both words describe direction only.
  • None of the four words is an instruction. They describe. They contain no entry, exit, size or risk limit.

Common mistakes

  1. Turning a description into a decision. “It is a bull market” describes the past and the present. Whether to hold anything is a separate question.
  2. Dropping the timeframe. “Bullish” on a five-minute chart and “bullish” on a weekly chart are different claims.
  3. Assuming there are only two states. Markets can move sideways and accounts can be flat.
  4. Confusing selling with shorting. Selling something already owned closes a long. A short is a new position that gains from falling prices.

Risks and limitations

  • The words are conventions. Different sources draw the lines between bull, bear and sideways in different places.
  • Labels lag. A market is usually called a bull or bear market after a large part of the move has already happened.
  • None of the words measure risk. The risk of a position comes from its size, its leverage and its exit plan, as described in What Is Risk Management?.

Key takeaway

Bull and bear describe what a market is doing or how someone reads it. Long and short describe what someone holds. Flat is always a third option, and no description of a market is an instruction to take a position.
