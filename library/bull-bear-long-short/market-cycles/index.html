Market Cycles

A market cycle is a descriptive model with four phases: accumulation, advance, distribution and decline. The phases are clear in hindsight and cannot be timed reliably.

Why it matters

Markets do not rise or fall in straight lines forever. Looking back over long charts, people noticed a rough rhythm: quiet periods, rises, noisy tops, falls. The market cycle is a model that gives each part of that rhythm a name.

The model is useful as shared vocabulary, because words such as accumulation and distribution appear constantly in market commentary. It is far less useful as a clock, for reasons this page sets out.

What it looks like

The figure is tidy because it was drawn with the ending known, which is the most important fact about any cycle diagram.

How it works: the four phases

Phase Price behaviour How it is usually described
Accumulation A range after a decline Interest is low. Some buyers build positions gradually.
Advance An uptrend More participants join as prices rise. Also called markup.
Distribution A range after an advance Interest is high. Earlier buyers sell to later ones.
Decline A downtrend Selling spreads as prices fall. Also called markdown.

The advance is the core of a bull market and the decline is the core of a bear market. Accumulation and distribution are the sideways stretches in between.

In plain words

The seasons are a familiar cycle, with one large difference. Seasons follow the calendar, so winter can be predicted to the day. Market phases have no calendar. They resemble weather more than seasons: patterns are recognisable afterwards, and the turn is hard to call in advance.

The model is old. Dow theory, one of the earliest frameworks in technical analysis, describes a major uptrend in three phases: accumulation, public participation and distribution. The four-phase version adds the decline. It is a descriptive tradition built from observation of charts, not a law of nature.

Going deeper

The emotional version

A popular version of the model attaches moods to the phases. Sentiment tends to be most optimistic near highs and most pessimistic near lows, because mood follows price. The psychology is covered in Fear & Greed and Herd Behavior.

Why hindsight is required

A sideways range after a decline can be accumulation. It can also be a pause before a further decline. While the range is forming, the two look the same. The label depends on which way price eventually leaves, so the label arrives after the information would have been useful.

The same applies at the top. A range after an advance is called distribution if price then falls, and consolidation if price then rises.

Cycles are irregular

  • Length varies. One advance may last months and another years.
  • Size varies. Nothing fixes how far a phase travels.
  • Cycles nest. A daily chart can show a full four-phase sequence inside one advance on the weekly chart.
  • Not every asset completes the loop. Some assets decline and never advance again.

In crypto, cycles are often discussed in connection with Bitcoin’s halving. The halving schedule is a fact of the protocol. The idea that prices follow it is an interpretation built on a small number of past occurrences.

One hypothetical range, two labelsHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset falls from 150 to 100 and then moves between 95 and 105 for three months.

Outcome A. Price leaves the range upward and reaches 130. Looking back, commentators call the three months accumulation.

Outcome B. Price leaves the range downward and reaches 80. Looking back, the same three months are called a pause in the decline.

The candles inside the range are identical in both outcomes. During those months, an observer who said “this is accumulation” was stating a hypothesis. It would be invalidated by a sustained move below the range, and it was not knowable from the range alone.

What it does NOT mean

  • A cycle model is not a clock. It names phases. It does not say when one ends or the next begins.
  • Naming a phase is not a signal. “This looks like accumulation” is an interpretation that can be wrong.
  • Accumulation does not mean informed buyers know the outcome. The word describes a price pattern. It does not reveal anyone’s intentions.
  • Past cycles do not set the length or size of the next. A handful of earlier examples is a small sample.

Common mistakes

  1. Pinning “you are here” on the diagram. The neat curve exists only for finished cycles.
  2. Expecting the next cycle to copy the last. Durations and sizes have varied widely.
  3. Reading intent into ranges. Sideways price is compatible with many explanations.
  4. Using the model to justify a view already held. Any chart can be fitted to the diagram by someone who wants it to fit, a form of confirmation bias.

Risks and limitations

  • The model is descriptive. It organises the past and has no demonstrated power to time turns.
  • Phase boundaries are subjective, and real markets skip, repeat and blur phases.
  • Popular cycle charts leave out the many assets that did not recover, which makes the pattern look more dependable than it is.

Key takeaway

The four-phase cycle of accumulation, advance, distribution and decline is a useful vocabulary for describing what a market has done. The phases are visible in hindsight, and the model cannot time them reliably in advance.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of Stock Trends, Robert D. Edwards and John Magee. Classic description of accumulation and distribution in trend reversals
