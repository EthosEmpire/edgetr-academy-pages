Hedging Basics

Hedging means reducing exposure to a price move by holding an offsetting position. It lowers both losses and gains, and it has costs and risks of its own, so it is not free protection.

Why it matters

A hedge is a position opened to reduce the effect of a price move on something already held. The word is often used as if it meant “protection”, with the suggestion that risk has been removed.

It has not. A hedge exchanges one set of risks and costs for another.

How it works

The idea is to hold two positions whose results move in opposite directions. The most common form combines a long position in an asset with a short position in the same asset or a closely related contract.

If price falls, the holding loses and the short gains. If price rises, the holding gains and the short loses. The combined result moves less than the holding alone would.

In plain words

Home insurance is a familiar hedge. The premium is paid every year whether or not anything goes wrong. In a good year it is simply a cost. The buyer has accepted a certain small expense in exchange for a smaller worst case.

How to read it: the vocabulary

Exposure. How much a portfolio’s value changes when the price changes. Holding 2 units is an exposure of 2 units.

Hedge ratio. The size of the hedge divided by the size of the holding. Short 1 unit against 2 held is a ratio of 0.5, a partial hedge. A ratio of 1 is a full hedge.

Net exposure. What remains after the hedge: holding minus short. With a full hedge the net exposure to price is about zero, a state often called delta neutral.

Basis risk. The risk that the hedge and the holding do not move together exactly. A perpetual contract can trade slightly above or below the spot price, and a hedge using a different but related asset can drift much further. See Basis, Contango & Backwardation.

Common instruments

Instrument How it hedges a holding Main cost
Short perpetual or futures contract Gains as price falls Funding or basis, margin, liquidation risk
Put option Gains value below a chosen price A premium paid in advance
Selling part of the holding Reduces exposure directly Fees, and possibly tax

Options are introduced in Options Basics. Selling part of a holding is not usually called a hedge, though it reduces exposure most directly.

Going deeper: why it is not free

Gains are given up. A full hedge removes the loss from a fall and removes the gain from a rise. The cost of protection in one direction is the benefit in the other.

Carrying costs accumulate. On perpetual futures, the short side pays funding when the rate is negative and receives it when the rate is positive. The rate changes, so the cost of holding a hedge is not known in advance.

The hedge can be liquidated. A short perpetual is backed by its own margin. If price rises sharply, the short can be liquidated even though the spot holding has gained by as much or more. After that, the holding is unhedged again and the margin is gone. Keeping a hedge alive in a strong rise can require adding collateral.

It adds another venue. A hedge held on an exchange introduces counterparty risk.

Hedging is also different from diversification, which spreads exposure across assets that may not move together.

A hypothetical half hedgeHypothetical illustration

Someone holds 2 units of a made-up asset bought at 100, worth 200. They open a short of 1 unit on a perpetual contract at 100, posting 25 as margin. The hedge ratio is 0.5.

Price falls to 80. The holding loses 40. The short gains 20. Net result: -20, where the unhedged result would be -40.

Price rises to 120. The holding gains 40. The short loses 20. Net result: +20, where the unhedged result would be +40. The short’s margin has fallen from 25 to 5, close to liquidation. A further rise to about 124 or 125 (the exact point depends on the exchange’s maintenance margin) would trigger it unless margin is added.

Funding. If funding averages -0.01% per interval for 90 intervals, the short pays about 100 x 0.0001 x 90 = 0.90.

The hedge halved the swing in both directions and added a funding cost, a margin requirement and a liquidation risk.

What it does NOT mean

  • A hedge is not free protection. It is paid for through given-up gains, fees, funding or premiums.
  • Hedged does not mean risk-free. Basis risk, liquidation risk and platform risk remain.
  • A hedge is not a profit strategy. Its purpose is to narrow outcomes. A full hedge earns nothing from price moves.
  • A short used as a hedge is not a bearish opinion. It can be held by someone who expects prices to rise and wants a smaller swing meanwhile.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting only the downside case. The hedge’s cost appears when price rises.
  2. Ignoring the hedge’s own margin. The protective leg can be the one that gets liquidated.
  3. Hedging with a loosely related asset. Two assets that usually move together can separate in a crisis.
  4. Adding complexity where a smaller position would do. Reducing the holding and hedging it have similar effects on exposure, with very different moving parts.

Risks and limitations

  • A short hedge held with leverage can be liquidated, and it needs monitoring.
  • Funding rates, option premiums and basis vary with market conditions and can turn sharply against a hedge.
  • Margin rules, available instruments and tax treatment differ between exchanges and jurisdictions.

Key takeaway

A hedge reduces exposure by pairing a holding with an offsetting position, which narrows both losses and gains. It always costs something, in given-up upside, fees, funding or premiums, and it brings margin, liquidation and basis risks of its own.

Sources and further reading

  • Key Terms for Tough Times: The Vocabulary of Stressed Markets (opens in a new tab), FINRA. Defines hedging as investments intended to offset possible losses, that is, to mitigate risk
