Bear Market & Bearish

A bear market is a sustained period of generally falling prices, and bearish describes a downward condition or interpretation. Both are descriptions, not instructions to open a short.

Why it matters

A bear market is the mirror image of a bull market: a sustained, broad fall in prices. It is the condition in which most long positions lose value, which is why the phrase carries so much emotion.

The word needs the same care as its opposite. “Bearish” describes falling conditions or a pessimistic interpretation. It does not tell anyone to sell, and it does not tell anyone to open a short.

What it looks like

On a chart, a bear market is a long downtrend: lower highs and lower lows that persist for months or longer on a daily or weekly chart. The fall is not a straight line. It is interrupted by rallies, and some of them are large and fast.

In plain words

Picture someone walking down a long hillside path. The path has short uphill stretches, and on those stretches the walker really is climbing. Seen from across the valley, the walk is still downhill. A bear market is the hillside. The rallies are the short climbs.

How to read it: two uses of one word

Term What it describes Scale
Bear market A sustained, broad fall in prices Months to years
Bearish (condition) Price behaviour that points downward Any timeframe, which should be stated
Bearish (opinion) Someone’s expectation or interpretation Personal, and open to disagreement

A single candle, a pattern or a person’s mood can be called bearish without a bear market being present.

The 20 percent convention

In traditional markets such as stock indexes, a widely repeated rule of thumb calls a fall of 20 percent or more from a recent high a bear market. A smaller fall, often put at around 10 percent, is commonly called a correction.

These thresholds are conventions used by commentators. No authority declares a bear market. They were built for stock indexes and fit volatile assets poorly. A crypto asset can fall 20 percent in days inside a period that is later described as a rise, so in crypto the term is normally used for a decline lasting many months.

Going deeper

Rallies are normal. Sharp rises inside a decline are often called bear market rallies. In the figure, the rise from 140 to 168 is 20 percent, which would meet the rule-of-thumb threshold for a new bull market when measured from the low, and the larger decline continues afterwards. Whether a rally is a pause or a trend transition is known only later.

Percentages are not symmetrical. A fall and the rise needed to undo it are different numbers.

A fall of 50 percent needs a rise of 100 percent to return to the starting price. This arithmetic, covered in Drawdown, is why deep bear markets take so long to recover from when they recover at all.

Sentiment follows price down. Falling prices tend to bring anxiety, then loss of interest. Lower attention can mean thinner liquidity, so the same order moves price further. The emotional side is described in Fear & Greed.

Rallies inside a hypothetical bear marketHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset moves through these swing points over a year: high 200, low 140, high 168, low 110, high 135, low 90.

  • From 200 to 90 the fall is 110, or 55%.
  • The rally from 140 to 168 is 28, or 20%.
  • The rally from 110 to 135 is 25, or about 23%.
  • From 90, a return to 200 would need a rise of 110, or about 122%.

Each high is lower than the previous high and each low is lower than the previous low, so the structure fits the description of a bear market. Someone who looked only at the weeks from 110 to 135 saw a market rising by about 23%. Both descriptions are true at their own timeframe.

What it does NOT mean

  • A bear market is not an instruction to open a short. It describes conditions. A short position has its own costs and its own risks, and sharp rallies are part of every decline.
  • Bearish does not mean prices fall every day. Rallies of 20 percent or more occur inside periods later called bear markets.
  • A bear market does not mean an asset is finished. It also does not mean recovery is certain. Some assets recover and some never do.
  • A lower price does not mean cheap. A price that has fallen 70 percent can fall 70 percent again.

Common mistakes

  1. Confusing a condition with a position. “The market is bearish” and “I am short” are different statements.
  2. Calling the end at every rally. Rallies in a decline look the same, while they happen, as the start of a recovery.
  3. Measuring from the old high. Anchoring on a past peak makes every lower price look like a discount.
  4. Forgetting the recovery arithmetic. A 55% fall is not undone by a 55% rise.

Risks and limitations

  • There is no single agreed definition. Two analysts can label the same period differently.
  • The term is usually applied in hindsight, so it is of limited use in the moment.
  • A broad label hides differences. In a bear market some assets fall far more than others, and a few rise.

Key takeaway

A bear market is a sustained, broad fall in prices, and bearish is the matching adjective for conditions or opinions. Both describe. Neither forecasts what comes next, and neither is a reason by itself to sell or to short.
