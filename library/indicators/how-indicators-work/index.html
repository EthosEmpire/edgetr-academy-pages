How Indicators Work

A technical indicator is a calculation applied to past price and volume data, drawn on the chart to make one aspect of market behaviour easier to see.

Why it matters

Open any charting platform and you will find hundreds of indicators. They look like extra information, and many beginners treat them as a machine that announces what happens next. They are neither.

Every indicator is a formula. It takes numbers the chart already contains and rearranges them into a line, a band or a histogram. Understanding that one fact makes every other page on this shelf easier to read, and it stops you expecting an indicator to know something that price does not.

How it works

The line above is a simple moving average. It looks like a new piece of information, but it was built entirely from the candles underneath it.

In plain words

Think of last week’s average temperature. It is a real, useful number that summarises seven days of weather in one figure. It is also built only from days that are over. It describes the week you just had, not the one that is coming.

The four families

Indicators are usually grouped by the question they try to answer.

Family What it describes Pages on this shelf
Trend The general direction of recent prices Moving averages, MACD
Momentum The speed and force of recent moves RSI, Stochastic
Volatility The size of recent price swings Bollinger Bands, ATR
Volume The amount traded Volume, VWAP

Overlays and separate panels

An overlay uses the same vertical scale as price, so it can be drawn over the candles. A moving average of 100 sits at the 100 mark on the chart.

A panel indicator has its own scale. RSI runs from 0 to 100 whatever the asset costs, so it needs a separate box. Indicators that swing back and forth around a middle value in this way are often called oscillators.

Going deeper

Lag

Because indicators are built from past data, they react after price does. This delay is called lag. More data in the calculation means a smoother line and more lag. Less data means a faster, jumpier line. No setting removes the trade-off. It only moves along it.

Why an average is slowHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset closes at 100 for five candles in a row. Its 5-period average is 100.

On the next candle the close jumps to 110. The five closes in the window are now 100, 100, 100, 100 and 110, which add up to 510. The average is 510 / 5 = 102.

Price moved 10. The average moved 2. Only after five closes at 110 would the average also read 110.

More indicators is not more information

Most indicators are calculated from the same few inputs, mainly the closing price. RSI, Stochastic and MACD on one chart are three views of the same recent prices, so they often agree. That agreement can feel like three independent confirmations, but it is closer to one opinion repeated three times.

Indicators from different families tend to add more than several from the same family. The page on combining indicators covers this in detail.

Overfitting

Every indicator has settings, and it is always possible to adjust them until they line up neatly with a past chart. This is called overfitting: tuning a tool so closely to old data that it captures noise instead of anything lasting. A setting that looks perfect on last month’s chart was chosen with hindsight, and it frequently stops fitting as soon as new candles arrive.

How practitioners use them

Many traders treat indicators as a supporting layer. Market structure, key levels and risk come first; an indicator is then used to describe one thing, such as momentum or volatility, more precisely. Used this way, an indicator sharpens a description. It does not make a decision.

What it does NOT mean

  • An indicator is not a forecast. It summarises candles that have already closed. It has no access to the next one.
  • A crossing, level or band touch is not a signal. These are events in past data. What follows them varies from one occasion to the next.
  • Several indicators agreeing is not proof. If they share the same input, agreement is expected and adds little.
  • A setting that fitted the past is not a setting that fits the future. Good historical fit is the easiest thing in technical analysis to manufacture.
  • Default settings are not “correct” values. They are conventions, often chosen decades ago for other markets.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading indicators before price. The candles are the source data. The indicator is a summary of them.
  2. Stacking tools from one family. Three momentum oscillators crowd the chart without adding a new viewpoint.
  3. Tuning settings until the past looks perfect. This is overfitting, and it tends to fail on new data.
  4. Ignoring timeframe. The same indicator on a 5-minute chart and a daily chart describes very different stretches of time.
  5. Forgetting what the tool measures. A volatility indicator such as ATR says nothing about direction, however it is read.

Risks and limitations

  • All indicators lag, because all of them are calculated from past data.
  • Indicators contain no information about news, liquidity, fees or an asset’s value.
  • An indicator that describes one market condition well can mislead in another. Trend tools struggle in a range, and oscillators struggle in strong trends.
  • A cluttered chart can create false confidence and hide the price action it was meant to clarify.

Key takeaway

An indicator is past price and volume, rearranged by a formula. It can make one feature of the market easier to see, but it cannot see anything the chart does not already contain.
