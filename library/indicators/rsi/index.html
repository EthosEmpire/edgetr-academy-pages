RSI (Relative Strength Index)

RSI is a momentum indicator that compares recent gains with recent losses and shows the result on a scale from 0 to 100.

Why it matters

Price tells you where the market is. RSI tries to describe how forcefully it got there. It answers one narrow question: over the last few periods, have the up-moves been bigger than the down-moves, or the other way around?

RSI is one of the most widely used indicators, which also makes it one of the most widely misunderstood. Learning what the number actually measures protects you from the most common beginner belief in all of technical analysis: that “RSI above 70 means sell”.

What it looks like

RSI appears as a single line in a separate panel, moving between 0 and 100. Three reference levels are usually drawn: 70, 50 and 30.

How to read the scale

In plain words

Think of RSI as a scoreboard for the last 14 candles. If buyers won almost every round, the score is high. If sellers won almost every round, the score is low. If the rounds were shared, the score sits near 50. The scoreboard tells you who has been winning. It does not tell you who wins the next round.

How it is calculated

You never need to calculate RSI by hand, but knowing the recipe explains its behaviour.

  1. Look at the last 14 periods (the default setting).
  2. Work out the average gain on the up-periods and the average loss on the down-periods.
  3. Divide one by the other. This ratio is called RS (relative strength).
  4. Convert RS to a 0-100 scale.
RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + RS) where RS = average gain / average lossIf the average gain is twice the average loss, RS = 2 and RSI = 66.7. If there are no losses at all, RSI = 100.

Wilder’s original method smooths the averages so that each new candle only nudges the value. That is why RSI moves fairly smoothly instead of jumping.

Settings and what they change

Setting Effect Trade-off
Shorter period (for example 7) Line reacts faster and reaches 70/30 more often More false alarms
Default period (14) The value Wilder proposed; the most common A middle ground
Longer period (for example 21) Line is smoother and rarely reaches the extremes Slower to react

Changing the period does not make RSI “more accurate”. It only trades speed for smoothness. Adjusting settings until past charts look perfect is called overfitting, and it usually fails on new data.

Going deeper

RSI in trends

This is the part most beginners miss. In a strong uptrend, RSI can stay above 70 for a long time, because gains really do keep outweighing losses. In a strong downtrend it can stay below 30 for just as long. A high reading in a strong trend is a description of strength, not a warning.

Many practitioners therefore read RSI differently depending on market structure:

  • In an uptrend, some authors describe RSI as often swinging between roughly 40 and 80, and dips toward 40-50 are commonly watched.
  • In a downtrend, some authors describe RSI as often swinging between roughly 20 and 60, and bounces toward 50-60 are commonly watched.
  • In a range, the classic 70/30 reading tends to be most useful, because price really is moving back and forth.

The 50 line

Whether RSI is above or below 50 is a simple, slow read on momentum: above 50, recent gains outweigh recent losses; below 50, the opposite. Some traders treat a cross of 50 as more informative than a touch of 70 or 30.

Divergence

A divergence is a disagreement between price and RSI:

  • Bearish divergence: price makes a higher high, RSI makes a lower high.
  • Bullish divergence: price makes a lower low, RSI makes a higher low.

Divergence is commonly interpreted as momentum fading. It is a warning light, not a turn signal: price can keep trending through several divergences in a row.

The same reading, two situationsHypothetical illustration

Situation A. A market has moved sideways for weeks between two levels. Price reaches the top of the range and RSI reads 74. Here the reading matches the structure: price is at the upper edge of a range and recent gains are stretched.

Situation B. A market has just broken out of a long range on high volume and RSI reads 74. Here the reading reflects a strong new move. RSI may stay above 70 for many candles while price keeps climbing.

The number is identical. The context is not.

What it does NOT mean

  • RSI above 70 does not mean “sell” or “short”. It means recent gains have been much larger than recent losses. In strong trends this can last a long time.
  • RSI below 30 does not mean “buy”. Markets that are falling hard can stay “oversold” while price keeps dropping.
  • “Overbought” does not mean “too expensive”. RSI knows nothing about value. It only compares recent up-moves with recent down-moves.
  • Divergence does not predict a reversal. It shows weakening momentum, which sometimes leads to a turn, sometimes to a pause and sometimes to nothing.
  • RSI is not a measure of relative strength against another asset. Despite the name, it compares an asset only with its own recent past.

Common mistakes

  1. Trading the 70/30 lines mechanically. This fights every strong trend.
  2. Ignoring market structure. The same reading means different things in a trend and in a range.
  3. Stacking similar indicators. RSI, Stochastic and other oscillators are built from the same price data. Three of them agreeing is one opinion repeated three times.
  4. Tuning settings to fit the past. A period that looks perfect on last month’s chart was chosen with hindsight.
  5. Forgetting timeframe. RSI on a 5-minute chart and RSI on a daily chart describe completely different things.

Risks and limitations

  • RSI is a lagging calculation. It is built entirely from prices that have already printed.
  • It can give many false readings in strong trends and in very choppy markets.
  • It contains no information about volume, news, liquidity or value.
  • Like every indicator, it is a context tool. It can support a view built on structure and risk; it cannot replace one.

Key takeaway

RSI is a 0-100 scoreboard of recent gains versus recent losses. High readings describe strength and low readings describe weakness. Neither is an instruction.
