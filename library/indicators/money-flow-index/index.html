Money Flow Index (MFI)

The Money Flow Index is a 0-100 oscillator that applies the RSI idea to price multiplied by volume, so periods with heavy volume count for more than quiet ones.

Why it matters

RSI compares recent gains with recent losses, and treats a gain on a sleepy afternoon exactly like the same gain on the busiest day of the month. The Money Flow Index, or MFI, changes that one thing. It weights each period by its volume.

The result is often described as a volume-weighted RSI. Studying it is a good way to see what adding volume to a momentum tool changes, and what it leaves untouched.

What it looks like

MFI is a single line in a separate panel, moving between 0 and 100.

In plain words

Imagine a show of hands in which each voter’s hand counts in proportion to how much they brought to the table. RSI counts hands. MFI weighs them. A day when few people traded barely moves MFI, while a crowded day moves it a lot. As with any vote count, it tells you how past rounds went, not how the next one goes.

How it is calculated

Typical price = (high + low + close) / 3Raw money flow = typical price × volume.
MFI = 100 - 100 / (1 + money ratio)Money ratio = positive money flow / negative money flow, each summed over the last 14 periods.

A period’s raw money flow counts as positive when its typical price is higher than the previous period’s, and negative when it is lower. If the typical price is unchanged, the period is left out.

From one candle to a readingHypothetical illustration

All numbers are hypothetical.

One candle. High 104, low 100, close 102. Typical price = (104 + 100 + 102) / 3 = 306 / 3 = 102. Volume is 1,000, so raw money flow = 102 × 1,000 = 102,000. The previous typical price was 101, so this counts as positive flow.

Fourteen candles. Suppose positive money flow adds up to 600,000 and negative money flow to 200,000. Money ratio = 600,000 / 200,000 = 3. MFI = 100 - 100 / (1 + 3) = 100 - 25 = 75.

The volume effect. Keep the same price moves, but imagine the falling periods had three times the volume, so negative flow is 600,000. Money ratio = 1 and MFI = 100 - 100 / 2 = 50. RSI, which ignores volume, would not have changed.

Settings and what they change

Setting Effect Trade-off
Shorter period (for example 7) Line swings widely and reaches 80/20 often One high-volume candle can dominate the reading
Default period (14) The most common value A middle ground
Longer period (for example 21) Line is smoother and rarely reaches the extremes Slow to reflect a change

The reference levels can be moved as well. Shifting the period and the levels until past extremes line up with past turns is overfitting: with two adjustable settings, a good-looking fit to history is easy to find and means little.

How to read it

  • Rising MFI: money flow has been shifting toward periods when price rose.
  • Falling MFI: money flow has been shifting toward periods when price fell.
  • Above 80 or below 20: flow has been strongly one-sided. In a strong trend, MFI can stay beyond either level for a long time.
  • Around 50: flow is balanced, which is common in a range.
  • Divergence: price makes a new high while MFI makes a lower high, or the reverse. This is commonly interpreted as participation fading. See divergence for why it is a description and not a forecast.

Going deeper

MFI differs from RSI in a second way that is easy to miss. RSI uses smoothed averages, so old data fades gradually. MFI uses plain sums over a fixed window, so each period carries full weight for 14 periods and then vanishes. When a very high-volume candle leaves the window, MFI can move sharply although nothing notable happened in the latest candle.

MFI overlaps with both of its relatives. It shares its price logic with RSI and its volume data with OBV. Seeing all three agree is weaker evidence than it looks, a point explored in combining indicators. Practitioners who use MFI generally read it against market structure, for example noting whether a one-sided reading appears at the edge of a range or in the middle of a trend.

What it does NOT mean

  • MFI does not measure money entering or leaving a market. Every trade has a buyer and a seller, so no money “flows in”. The name describes a calculation: price times volume, sorted by direction.
  • Above 80 does not mean price is about to fall. It means recent volume-weighted moves were mostly upward.
  • Below 20 does not mean price is about to rise. Heavy selling can keep the reading low while price continues down.
  • MFI does not reveal who is trading. It cannot separate large participants from small ones.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating 80 and 20 as triggers. They are reference lines on a description of the past.
  2. Trusting the volume blindly. In crypto, each exchange reports its own volume and some reported volume is unreliable, so MFI can differ between venues. See trading volume.
  3. Overlooking the drop-off. A sharp change may be caused by an old high-volume candle leaving the window.
  4. Using MFI and RSI together as two votes. They are close relatives and mostly repeat each other.
  5. Tuning period and levels to fit history. The best past fit is not evidence about the future.

Risks and limitations

  • MFI is calculated only from past prices and past volume.
  • It inherits every flaw in the volume data it is fed.
  • A single extreme-volume period can distort it for the whole lookback window.
  • It contains no information about news, liquidity or value, and it cannot say what happens next.

Key takeaway

MFI is an RSI-style oscillator in which each period is weighted by its volume. Readings above 80 or below 20 describe one-sided past flow, and they are context, not signals.
