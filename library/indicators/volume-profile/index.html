Volume Profile

Volume profile is a sideways histogram that shows how much volume traded at each price level over a chosen period, instead of how much traded in each time period.

Why it matters

The ordinary volume bars under a chart answer the question “how much traded during this candle?”. Volume profile answers a different one: “how much traded at this price?”.

That change of axis shows something the usual bars hide: the price areas where most of the business was done, and the areas the market crossed with almost no trading. Many traders use these areas as extra context when they think about support and resistance.

What it looks like

The profile is a histogram turned on its side. Each horizontal bar belongs to a price row, and its length shows the volume traded inside that row. Three features are usually marked:

  • Point of control (POC): the single row with the most volume.
  • Value area: the band of rows around the POC that holds a chosen share of all volume, by convention about 70%.
  • Value area high and low: the top and bottom edges of that band.

In plain words

Think of a worn carpet in a hallway. The most faded patches show where people stood and walked most often. The carpet does not tell you where the next visitor will stand. It only records where the traffic has been.

How it is calculated

  1. Choose the period to study: a session, the visible chart, or a hand-picked range.
  2. Divide the price range of that period into equal rows.
  3. Assign each trade’s volume to the row containing its price.
  4. Add up each row.
Volume of a row = sum of all volume traded at prices inside that rowPlatforms without trade-by-trade data estimate this by spreading each candle's volume across the rows its range touched, so profiles are approximations.

Finding the point of control and value areaHypothetical illustration

In the hypothetical profile above, 10,000 units traded in total: 500 + 1,800 + 3,500 + 2,500 + 1,200 + 500.

  • The point of control is row 103, with 3,500 units.
  • One common way to build a 70% value area (target 7,000 units) starts at the POC and repeatedly adds the larger neighbouring row. Row 102 (2,500) is larger than row 104 (1,800), so the total becomes 3,500 + 2,500 = 6,000.
  • Next, row 104 (1,800) is larger than row 101 (1,200), so the total becomes 7,800, which passes the target.

The value area is rows 102 to 104 and holds 78% of the volume. Exact methods differ between platforms.

Settings and what they change

Setting Effect Trade-off
Period (session, visible range, fixed range) Decides which trades are counted A different window gives a different POC
Row size or number of rows Smaller rows show more detail Fine rows look jagged and noisy; coarse rows hide structure
Value area percentage 70% is conventional A larger share widens the band

There is no neutral setting. On a “visible range” profile, the POC can jump simply because you scrolled or zoomed the chart. Picking the window and row size that make past turns line up with thick rows is overfitting: the profile was shaped to fit the story.

How to read it

Thick areas are often called high-volume nodes and thin areas low-volume nodes. A common interpretation is that a thick area marks prices many participants accepted, while a thin area marks prices the market rejected quickly.

Price trading above the value area, inside it or below it is sometimes used as a rough description of where the market sits relative to its recent business. This is a description of position, similar to asking whether price is above or below VWAP.

Going deeper

Volume profile grew out of Market Profile, a method developed for futures exchanges that counts time spent at each price. Volume profile counts traded volume instead.

Practitioners tend to treat profile levels as zones, not lines, and to give them more weight when they overlap with structure seen on the chart itself. That overlap is a form of confluence. A thick row that sits at an old swing level is two observations pointing at one area. It is still not a promise about the next move.

What it does NOT mean

  • The point of control is not a magnet. Price often revisits heavily traded areas, and often does not.
  • A high-volume node is not guaranteed support or resistance. It shows where trading happened, not where orders are waiting today.
  • The profile does not show buyers versus sellers. Every unit of volume had both. It shows quantity at a price and nothing about intent.
  • A profile is not a fact about the asset. It is a fact about one data source, one window and one row size.

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting the window. A session profile, a monthly profile and a visible-range profile can all show different points of control on the same chart.
  2. Reading levels as exact prices. Rows are bins. The true heavy area is a zone at least one row wide.
  3. Trusting estimated profiles too precisely. When volume is spread across each candle’s range, fine detail is partly an artefact of the estimate.
  4. Combining exchanges without noticing. In crypto, each venue has its own volume, so profiles differ between exchanges.
  5. Re-drawing until it fits. Changing the range after the fact can easily produce a convincing picture.

Risks and limitations

  • The profile is built only from past trades. Old volume says nothing certain about current liquidity.
  • It removes the time dimension: you cannot see in what order the volume arrived.
  • Quality depends on the underlying data, which varies by platform and venue.
  • A developing profile changes as new trades arrive, so today’s POC can move.

Key takeaway

Volume profile shows how much traded at each price, with the point of control as the busiest level. It maps where past business was done and cannot say what happens when price returns there.

  • Mind Over Markets, James F. Dalton, Eric T. Jones and Robert B. Dalton (1990). Standard text on value area and point of control concepts
