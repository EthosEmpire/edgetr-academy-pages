Parabolic SAR

Parabolic SAR is a trend-following indicator that plots trailing dots below or above price; the dots speed up as a move extends and switch sides when price reaches them.

Why it matters

Parabolic SAR turns a trend into a row of dots that chase price. It was introduced by J. Welles Wilder Jr., who also created RSI, ATR and ADX. SAR stands for “stop and reverse”, and “parabolic” describes the curved path the dots trace as they speed up.

It is worth studying for one feature that most indicators lack: it has time built in. The dots tighten simply because a move has continued, which shows clearly how a design choice shapes what an indicator “says”.

What it looks like

The indicator is drawn on the price chart as one dot per candle.

  • Dots below price: the calculation is in a rising phase.
  • Dots above price: the calculation is in a falling phase.
  • A switch of sides: price reached the dot, so the calculation reversed.

There is never a neutral state. The dots are always on one side or the other, even when the market has no direction at all.

In plain words

Picture someone following a hiker up a hill. At first the follower takes small steps and hangs well back. Each time the hiker reaches a new high point, the follower lengthens their stride. If the hiker slows down or turns around, the follower catches up, and at that moment they swap roles. The follower’s position shows where the hiker has been. It does not show where the hiker goes next.

How it is calculated

Next SAR = current SAR + AF × (EP - current SAR)EP is the extreme point: the highest high of a rising phase or the lowest low of a falling phase. AF is the acceleration factor.

Two further rules apply. A dot in a rising phase is not placed above the lows of the previous two candles (and the mirror rule holds in a falling phase). When a reversal happens, the first dot of the new phase is placed at the old extreme point.

Two steps of a rising phaseHypothetical illustration

All prices are hypothetical, using the common settings: a start of 0.02, a step of 0.02 and a maximum of 0.20.

Period 1. The dot is at 100.00, the highest high so far (EP) is 110 and AF is 0.02. Next SAR = 100.00 + 0.02 × (110 - 100.00) = 100.00 + 0.20 = 100.20.

Period 2. Price makes a new high at 112. EP becomes 112 and AF rises to 0.04. Next SAR = 100.20 + 0.04 × (112 - 100.20) = 100.20 + 0.472 = 100.67 (rounded).

The second step is more than twice the first, because the gap is larger and the fraction has doubled.

Settings and what they change

Setting Common value Effect of changing it
Start 0.02 Higher: the dots begin closer to price
Step (increment) 0.02 Higher: the dots tighten faster and reversals come sooner and more often
Maximum 0.20 Higher: the dots can hug price very closely late in a move

Larger values make the indicator more sensitive, which means more reversals, many of them short-lived. Smaller values make it slower, which means fewer reversals that arrive later. Neither is more “accurate”. Adjusting the three numbers until the dots would have reversed near past turning points is overfitting.

How to read it

  • Wide gap between dots and price: the phase is young or price has been moving quickly away from the dots.
  • Narrowing gap: the acceleration factor has grown, or price has stalled. Even a market going sideways after a rise will eventually be caught, because the dots keep closing a fixed share of the gap.
  • A reversal: price traded through the dot. It is a record that the trailing calculation was reached.

Going deeper

Wilder presented the indicator as an always-in-the-market system: every reversal closed one position and opened the opposite one, which is where “stop and reverse” comes from. Standard references describe it as suited to trending markets. In sideways conditions it reverses repeatedly, as the figure above shows. The directional movement tools from the same book, including ADX, are often used to judge whether a trend is present at all. That history explains the design. It is not a recommendation to use either tool that way.

Supertrend is a modern relative: also a trailing line that changes sides, but spaced by ATR instead of by acceleration. Supertrend’s distance depends on volatility. Parabolic SAR’s distance depends on time and on new extremes.

Because the dots trail price, they are often discussed alongside trailing stop placement. A dot is the output of a formula. Whether any level is a sensible place for a stop is a separate risk question that the indicator cannot answer. Many practitioners also compare the dots with market structure, since a swing low is a feature of the market itself and a dot is not.

What it does NOT mean

  • A switch of sides is not a signal. It records that price reached a trailing calculation. In sideways markets this happens constantly.
  • Dots below price do not mean price is in an uptrend. The dots are always on one side, trend or no trend.
  • Tightening dots do not mean the move is ending. They tighten because time has passed and new extremes were made.
  • The dots are not a safe distance. Price can gap or spike far beyond a dot in a single candle.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading the dots in a sideways market. Without a trend, the indicator has nothing to trail and simply flips.
  2. Treating every reversal as meaningful. Late in a move the dots sit so close that an ordinary pullback reaches them.
  3. Assuming the dot is where price “should” stop. The market has no knowledge of the calculation.
  4. Raising the step to make it “faster”. Faster means more reversals, not better ones.
  5. Fitting the settings to one chart. Three adjustable numbers are enough to match almost any past chart by accident.

Risks and limitations

  • The dots are calculated only from past highs and lows. They follow price and cannot anticipate it.
  • The indicator has no neutral state, so it always appears to have an opinion.
  • It performs poorly in ranges and in choppy, gapping markets.
  • It ignores volume and volatility. The same settings behave very differently on quiet and on violent markets.

Key takeaway

Parabolic SAR trails price with dots that accelerate as a move extends and switch sides when price reaches them. It describes trending phases after the fact and flips repeatedly in sideways markets, so a switch is never an instruction.

Sources and further reading

  • New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, J. Welles Wilder Jr. (1978). The book that introduced the Parabolic Time/Price System
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999)
