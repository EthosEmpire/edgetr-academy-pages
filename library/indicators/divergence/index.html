Divergence

Divergence is a disagreement between price and an indicator: price makes a new high or low, but the indicator does not, which is commonly read as momentum fading.

Why it matters

Most of the time price and momentum indicators move together: price makes a new high and so does the indicator. Divergence is the name for the moments when they stop agreeing.

It is one of the most talked-about ideas in technical analysis and one of the most overstated. Divergences are easy to find on any past chart next to every major turn, which makes them look far more decisive than they are. This page covers what a divergence measures and why it so often leads to nothing.

What it looks like

To find a divergence you compare two swing points on price with the indicator’s readings at those same two points. If the lines joining them slope in opposite directions, price and indicator are diverging.

In plain words

Think of a ball thrown up a staircase. Each bounce lands on a higher step, but each bounce is also a little weaker than the last. The ball is still climbing, yet it is clearly running out of energy. Divergence is the chart version of noticing the weaker bounce. It does not tell you which step is the last one.

How it works

Divergence is not an indicator with its own formula. It is a way of reading an existing one, most often RSI, MACD or the Stochastic Oscillator.

Bearish divergence: price high 2 > price high 1, while indicator high 2 < indicator high 1Bullish divergence is the mirror image at lows: a lower low in price with a higher low in the indicator.

The logic comes straight from what those indicators measure. RSI compares recent gains with recent losses. If price reaches a higher high on smaller, slower gains than the previous rally, RSI peaks lower. The new high is real. It was simply reached with less force.

The four types

Type Price shows Indicator shows Commonly interpreted as
Regular bearish Higher high Lower high Upward momentum fading
Regular bullish Lower low Higher low Downward momentum fading
Hidden bearish Lower high Higher high A bounce inside a downtrend
Hidden bullish Higher low Lower low A dip inside an uptrend

Regular divergence appears at the extremes of a trend. Hidden divergence appears during pullbacks and is conventionally read as consistent with the existing trend. All four labels are interpretations, not outcomes.

Settings and what they change

Divergence inherits the settings of the indicator underneath it. A short RSI period swings widely and produces many small divergences. A long period produces few. The swing points you choose matter just as much: closer swings give more divergences, wider swings give fewer.

Since both the indicator period and the swing selection are adjustable, it is easy to tune them until every past turn shows a neat divergence. That is overfitting. It also hides the divergences that appeared and led nowhere, which never get circled on anyone’s chart.

Going deeper

What can follow

Slowing momentum is a normal part of trends. A strong advance often shows a first divergence, keeps rising, shows a second and keeps rising again. Momentum readings at extremes tend to ease, though they can stay extreme, and a market can climb for a long time on moderate momentum.

Reading one divergenceHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market rallies to a swing high of 120, where the 14-period RSI reads 78. Price pulls back, then rallies again to 124. At this second high RSI reads 66.

Price is 4 points higher (124 - 120). RSI is 12 points lower (78 - 66). That is a regular bearish divergence: a higher high in price with a lower high in RSI.

What it establishes: the second rally involved smaller gains relative to losses than the first.

What it does not establish: what comes next. Price might fall back below 120, drift sideways around 122, or continue to 130 with RSI at 64, creating a second divergence on top of the first.

Confirming the swings

A divergence compares two completed swing points, and a swing high is only confirmed after price has moved away from it. While the second high is still forming, the “divergence” can vanish with one strong candle. This makes divergence partly a hindsight tool: it is obvious on finished charts and uncertain at the right-hand edge.

How practitioners use it

Traders who watch divergence generally treat it as a reason to pay closer attention, not as a conclusion. It tends to be weighed with other evidence: a break of structure, behaviour at support or resistance, and volume. A divergence that appears with a change in market structure is a different situation from one that appears in the middle of an intact trend.

What it does NOT mean

  • Divergence does not predict a reversal. It shows that the latest push was weaker than the previous one. Trends can continue for a long time on weaker pushes.
  • Divergence is not a signal. It is a comparison of two past swings.
  • A missing divergence does not mean a trend is safe. Markets also turn sharply with no divergence at all.
  • More divergences do not add up to certainty. Seeing it on RSI, MACD and Stochastic together is largely one observation repeated, since all three are built from the same recent prices.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating divergence as a timing tool. It can appear many candles, or many swings, before anything changes.
  2. Drawing lines between arbitrary points. Without clear swing highs and lows, almost any chart can be made to show a divergence.
  3. Reading unfinished swings. A divergence at an unconfirmed high may disappear on the next candle.
  4. Remembering only the ones followed by a turn. Past charts make those divergences vivid and the many others invisible.

Risks and limitations

  • Divergence depends on a lagging indicator and on swing points that are only confirmed afterwards.
  • Identifying it is subjective. Two analysts can pick different swings and reach opposite conclusions.
  • It gives no information about how far or how long any following move might be.
  • Its apparent reliability on historical charts is inflated by hindsight.

Key takeaway

Divergence means price reached a new extreme with less momentum than before. It is a warning light worth noticing, and it leaves open whether the market reverses, pauses or simply carries on.
