Combining Indicators

Combining indicators means choosing tools that measure different things, because indicators from the same family are built from the same data and repeat the same information.

  • Intermediate
  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A chart with six indicators on it looks thorough. When all six point the same way, it feels like six independent witnesses. Usually it is closer to one witness repeating the same statement six times.

Almost every indicator is a rearrangement of the same few numbers: the open, high, low and close of past candles, plus volume. This page explains why tools from the same family echo each other, how to think about what each tool actually measures, and why adding more of them increases the risk of fooling yourself. It describes a way of thinking, not a setup to copy.

How it works

MACD is the difference between a 12-period and a 26-period EMA. If you add both of those averages to the chart as well, you have three displays of one calculation. See how indicators work for the general principle.

In plain words

Suppose you want to know whether it is raining. You look out of the front window, then the side window, then the back window. Three windows, one sky: you have checked once. A different kind of check would be to look at the weather radar. Indicators from one family are the windows. And even the radar reports the rain that has already formed, not next week’s.

One tool per question

A practical way to organise indicators is by the question each one answers. Two tools that answer the same question from the same data will agree most of the time, and their agreement is not news.

Question Example tools What a second tool from the same family adds
Direction and persistence Moving averages, MACD A slightly faster or slower version of the same line
Speed of the move RSI, Stochastic, Williams %R The same swings on a different scale
Size of the moves ATR, Bollinger Bands, Keltner Channels Another view of the same candle ranges
Participation Volume, OBV, Money Flow Index A different arrangement of the same volume bars

The table is a map of overlap, not a shopping list. It does not imply that one tool from each row makes a complete or reliable picture.

Confirmation versus redundancy

Three oscillators, one factHypothetical illustration

All prices are hypothetical. Over the last 14 candles a market has climbed steadily. The highest high is 112.5, the lowest low is 99.5 and the latest close is 112.

  • Stochastic %K = 100 × (112 - 99.5) / (112.5 - 99.5) = 100 × 12.5 / 13 = about 96.
  • Williams %R = -100 × (112.5 - 112) / 13 = -100 × 0.5 / 13 = about -4.
  • RSI is high as well, because nearly all of the 14 closes were gains.

Three panels show an extreme. They are not three findings. Each one restates a single fact that is already visible on the price chart: the market has been closing near the top of its recent range.

Agreement across families is somewhat more informative, but less than it appears. Trend, momentum and volatility tools are all computed from price, so a strong move drives all of them at once. Even volume-based tools usually take their direction from the close.

Going deeper

Structure first

Many practitioners start from what is on the chart before any formula is applied: market structure, levels and volume. Indicators are then used to describe one specific aspect more precisely. Several unrelated observations meeting in one area is called confluence, and the same caution applies there: overlapping observations are context, not certainty.

Why more indicators means more overfitting

Every indicator brings settings, and every setting is a dial that can be turned until the past looks tidy.

Number of combinations = choices for tool 1 × choices for tool 2 × ...Four indicators with five plausible settings each give 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 = 625 combinations.

With 625 versions to choose from, some will look excellent on any past chart purely by chance. Choosing the best-looking one is overfitting: it explains the old data, including its noise, and has no particular reason to hold on new data.

Adding conditions has a second cost. The more things that must line up, the fewer past occasions there are when they all did. A rule that matched five times in three years has almost no evidence behind it, however good those five occasions look.

The psychological side

Stacked indicators also feed confirmation bias. With enough panels on screen, at least one will always agree with the view you already hold, and it is tempting to notice only that one.

What it does NOT mean

  • More indicators agreeing does not mean higher probability. If they share inputs, their agreement was built in from the start.
  • One tool from each family is not a system. Covering four questions describes the past more completely. It does not predict anything.
  • Confirmation is not certainty. Independent observations can agree and price can still do the opposite.
  • A combination that fits the past is not validated by that fit. It may simply be the luckiest of many tries.
  • Fewer indicators is not automatically better. The point is knowing what each one measures, not hitting a number.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting panels as evidence. Three momentum oscillators are one opinion shown three times.
  2. Adding a tool after every surprise. Each addition makes the past look better explained and the result harder to test.
  3. Tuning several settings at once. The number of combinations multiplies, and so does the chance of a lucky fit.
  4. Ignoring disagreement. When tools from different families conflict, that conflict is information about uncertainty.

Risks and limitations

  • All price-derived indicators inherit the same blind spots: they see past candles and nothing else.
  • Independence between tools is a matter of degree and is hard to judge by eye.
  • Even a carefully chosen, non-overlapping set only describes what has already happened.

Key takeaway

Indicators from the same family repeat one another, so their agreement is redundancy, not confirmation. Knowing which question each tool answers matters more than how many tools agree, and no combination turns past data into certainty.
