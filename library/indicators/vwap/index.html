VWAP

VWAP (Volume-Weighted Average Price) is the average price traded during a session, with each price weighted by the volume traded at it.

Why it matters

A moving average treats every candle the same, whether a handful of units changed hands or a huge amount. VWAP, the Volume-Weighted Average Price, does not. It asks: across everything traded so far this session, what was the average price actually paid?

That makes VWAP a different kind of line. It began as a benchmark that institutions use to judge how well a large order was executed, and it is now a standard intraday reference on most charting platforms.

What it looks like

VWAP is an overlay: one line on the price chart. It starts at the first candle of the session and evolves from there. Early on it moves quickly. As more volume accumulates it becomes steadier.

How it is calculated

VWAP = cumulative (typical price x volume) / cumulative volumeTypical price = (high + low + close) / 3. Both sums run from the start of the session to the current candle.

In plain words

Suppose you buy 8 apples at 1.00 each in the morning and 2 apples at 1.50 each in the afternoon. The plain average of the two prices is 1.25. But you paid 11.00 for 10 apples, so your real average is 1.10 per apple. VWAP is that second kind of average, applied to every trade in the market.

Three candles, one VWAPHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical session opens with three candles.

Candle Typical price Volume Price x volume Cumulative price x volume Cumulative volume VWAP
1 100 800 80,000 80,000 800 100.00
2 102 100 10,200 90,200 900 100.22
3 104 100 10,400 100,600 1,000 100.60

The simple average of the three typical prices is (100 + 102 + 104) / 3 = 102. VWAP is 100,600 / 1,000 = 100.60.

Price has climbed to 104, but most of the session’s volume traded at 100, so the volume-weighted average has barely moved.

Settings and what they change

VWAP has no period. Its key setting is the anchor: the point where the running totals start.

Anchor What VWAP then describes
Session (the default) The average traded price since the session opened
Anchored VWAP The average traded price since a candle you choose, such as a major swing

In stock markets the session has a clear open and close. Crypto trades continuously, so the daily reset is a platform convention, commonly midnight UTC. Two platforms with different reset times show different VWAP lines for the same market. See a market that never closes.

Because any candle can be an anchor, it is easy to try several until the line happens to touch past turning points. That is overfitting: the anchor was chosen with hindsight.

How to read it

  • Price above VWAP: the current price is higher than the average price traded this session. Price below VWAP: it is lower.
  • Slope: a rising VWAP means recent volume has traded above the earlier average. A falling VWAP means it has traded below.
  • Distance: far from VWAP, price is stretched relative to the session’s average. Some platforms add standard deviation bands to show this.
  • Crossing VWAP: price has moved from one side of the session average to the other. This is a description of the session so far, not a signal.

Going deeper

A benchmark first

For an institution buying a large amount over a day, a fill price below VWAP means it paid less than the market’s average. This is VWAP’s original role: a yardstick for execution quality. Its use as a chart level followed from the fact that many participants watch the same line.

How practitioners use it

Intraday traders, including day traders, often treat VWAP as a session midpoint and note which side of it price spends its time on. It is typically read with volume, market structure and nearby levels, and sometimes compared with volume profile, which shows volume by price instead of by time.

Where it breaks down

VWAP becomes less responsive as cumulative volume grows, so late in a session it can lag far behind a fast move. On daily or longer charts, a session VWAP has little meaning, because each candle is already a whole session. Its quality also depends on the volume data, which on a crypto chart usually comes from a single exchange.

What it does NOT mean

  • Price above VWAP does not mean the market is bullish. It means the current price is above the session’s volume-weighted average. That can change within minutes.
  • VWAP is not a support or resistance level that must hold. Price crosses it many times in an ordinary session.
  • VWAP is not a fair value. It is an average of where trading happened, not a judgement of what an asset is worth.
  • VWAP on one platform is not VWAP everywhere. The anchor time and the volume source both differ.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating VWAP like a moving average. It has a fixed start and resets. Its behaviour changes through the session.
  2. Ignoring the anchor. Without knowing when the calculation starts, the line cannot be interpreted.
  3. Using a session VWAP on high timeframes. On a daily chart it has almost nothing to average.
  4. Reading early-session VWAP as stable. With little volume behind it, the line can swing widely in the first candles.

Risks and limitations

  • VWAP is cumulative and backward-looking. It lags, and the lag grows through the session.
  • It depends on volume data that may be incomplete, venue-specific or unreliable.
  • In 24-hour markets the session boundary is arbitrary, and so is the resulting line.
  • It describes the average price already traded. It contains no information about future orders.

Key takeaway

VWAP is the session’s average traded price, weighted by volume: cumulative price times volume divided by cumulative volume. It shows where the bulk of trading has taken place so far, and it resets when the session does.

Sources and further reading

  • Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners, Larry Harris (2003). Covers VWAP as a benchmark for execution quality
  • The Total Cost of Transactions on the NYSE, Stephen A. Berkowitz, Dennis E. Logue and Eugene A. Noser Jr. (1988). Journal of Finance paper that proposed volume-weighted average price as a trading benchmark
