ATR (Average True Range)

ATR is a volatility indicator that shows the average size of recent candles, including gaps, in price units. It measures how much price moves, not which way.

Why it matters

Most indicators try to say something about direction. ATR, the Average True Range, deliberately does not. It answers a different question: how far does this market typically move in one candle?

That number matters because “a 5-point move” means nothing without context. In a market that normally moves 1 point per candle it is dramatic. In a market that normally moves 10 it is a quiet day. ATR supplies the context, which is why it turns up in discussions of volatility, stop placement and position sizing.

What it looks like

ATR is a single line in its own panel. It has no upper limit and no reference levels. It rises when candles get bigger and falls when they get smaller. A sharp sell-off and a sharp rally both push it up.

How it is calculated

ATR was introduced by J. Welles Wilder Jr. It starts from the true range (TR), which improves on the simple high-minus-low range by accounting for gaps between candles.

TR = max( high - low, |high - previous close|, |low - previous close| )The vertical bars mean absolute value: the distance, ignoring the sign.

If a market closes at 100 and the next candle trades only between 103 and 106, the candle is 3 points tall, but price actually moved as much as 6 points from where it last closed. True range captures that.

ATR is then a smoothed average of the true range, 14 periods by default, using Wilder’s smoothing method.

ATR = (previous ATR x 13 + current TR) / 14For the default 14-period setting. The first value is a simple average of the first 14 true ranges.

In plain words

ATR is like the average size of the waves at a beach. It tells you whether the sea has been calm or rough lately. It does not tell you whether the tide is coming in or going out.

Updating ATR by one candleHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market has a 14-period ATR of 4.0. The newest candle has a true range of 6.

ATR = (4.0 x 13 + 6) / 14 = (52 + 6) / 14 = 58 / 14 = 4.14.

One candle 50% larger than average lifted ATR by only 0.14. It would take a run of large candles to move it substantially, and a run of small ones to bring it back down.

Settings and what they change

Period Behaviour
Shorter (for example 7) Reacts quickly to a burst of large candles and fades just as fast
Default (14) Wilder’s original setting and the most common
Longer (for example 21 or 50) A slow, stable view of background volatility

A shorter period gives each new candle more weight. A longer one spreads the weight over more history. Neither is more correct, and tuning the period to make past charts look tidy is overfitting.

How to read it

  • Rising ATR: candles are getting larger. Volatility is expanding.
  • Falling ATR: candles are getting smaller. Volatility is contracting.
  • High or low ATR only has meaning compared with the same market’s own history. There are no universal levels.

ATR is in price units

An ATR of 4 on an asset priced at 100 means candles average about 4% of price. An ATR of 4 on an asset priced at 400 means about 1%. To compare markets, traders often divide ATR by price.

ATR % = ATR / close x 1004 / 100 x 100 = 4%. 4 / 400 x 100 = 1%.

Going deeper

Volatility cycles

ATR often falls during quiet consolidations and rises when price breaks out or sells off. Low readings are commonly described as compression. Like a Bollinger squeeze, low ATR tells you the market has been quiet. It does not say when that ends or in which direction.

How practitioners use it

ATR is mostly a measuring tool for risk, not a view on the market.

  • Stop distance. Some traders express the distance between an entry and a stop in multiples of ATR, so that it adapts to current conditions. A fixed distance that sits outside normal noise in a calm market may sit well inside it in a volatile one.
  • Position size. Because ATR estimates a typical move, it is used in some sizing methods so that a volatile asset gets a smaller position than a calm one for the same amount at risk.
  • Other indicators. Keltner Channels and Supertrend are built on ATR.

These are descriptions of common practice. ATR measures typical movement, and markets regularly produce candles several times larger than typical.

What it does NOT mean

  • Rising ATR does not mean price is rising. It means candles are larger. ATR rises in crashes too.
  • Falling ATR does not mean price is falling. A slow, steady climb can have a shrinking ATR.
  • Low ATR does not mean low risk. Quiet periods end, often abruptly.
  • ATR is not a limit on how far price can move. It is an average of past ranges. A single candle can be many times the ATR.
  • A stop placed a multiple of ATR away is not safe. ATR-based distances reflect past volatility only.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading direction into ATR. It has none. The line looks the same for a surge and a collapse of equal size.
  2. Comparing raw ATR between assets. ATR is in price units. Only the percentage form is comparable.
  3. Mixing timeframes. ATR on a 1-hour chart describes hourly candles. It says little about a typical day.
  4. Treating ATR as a forecast of tomorrow’s range. It is an average of recent ranges, and volatility can change suddenly.

Risks and limitations

  • ATR lags. Wilder’s smoothing means a sudden change in volatility shows up gradually.
  • It contains no information about direction, trend or value.
  • Extreme events are, by definition, far outside the average, and ATR gives no warning of them.

Key takeaway

ATR is the smoothed average of the true range, 14 periods by default. It tells you how much a market has been moving per candle and nothing about which way.

Sources and further reading

  • New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, J. Welles Wilder Jr. (1978). The book that introduced the true range and its average
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999)
