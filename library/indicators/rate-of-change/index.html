Rate of Change (ROC)

Rate of Change is a momentum indicator that shows, as a percentage, how far the current close is above or below the close a fixed number of periods ago.

Why it matters

Rate of Change, or ROC, is about as simple as an indicator gets. It takes two prices, the latest close and an older close, and reports the percentage difference between them. Nothing is averaged or smoothed.

That simplicity makes it a useful first lesson in momentum: the speed of a price move, as opposed to its location. It also makes its weaknesses easy to see, which helps when you meet more complicated tools such as RSI or MACD.

What it looks like

ROC is a single line in a separate panel. The only reference level that comes from the formula is the zero line.

In plain words

Think of marking a child’s height on a door frame once a year. ROC is the gap between this year’s mark and last year’s, as a percentage. A big gap means fast growth over that year. It does not tell you how much the child will grow next year, and it says nothing about what happened between the two marks.

How it is calculated

  1. Take the current close.
  2. Take the close N periods ago.
  3. Subtract the old close from the current one.
  4. Divide by the old close and multiply by 100.
ROC = 100 × (close - close N periods ago) / close N periods agoWith a current close of 110 and a close of 100 ten periods ago: 100 × (110 - 100) / 100 = 10%.

A close of 95 against an older close of 100 gives 100 × (95 - 100) / 100 = -5%.

A closely related indicator, usually called Momentum, uses the plain difference (close minus close N periods ago) instead of a percentage. The two lines usually look very similar. ROC’s percentage form makes readings easier to compare across assets with very different prices.

Settings and what they change

There is no single agreed default. Platforms commonly ship with a period somewhere between 9 and 14, and much longer periods are used for slower studies.

Setting Effect Trade-off
Shorter period (for example 5) Line is jumpy and crosses zero often Many crossings that mean little
Medium period (for example 9 to 14) Describes momentum over a few weeks of daily candles A middle ground
Longer period (for example 50) Line is slow and crosses zero rarely Says little about recent behaviour

Changing the period changes which old price is used for comparison, nothing more. Searching for the period whose zero crossings best matched past turns is overfitting: the setting has been fitted to noise that will not repeat in the same way.

How to read it

  • Rising ROC above zero: price is higher than N periods ago and the gap is growing. This is commonly interpreted as upward momentum building.
  • Falling ROC above zero: price is still higher than N periods ago, but by less. An advance is slowing, not necessarily ending.
  • Crossing zero: the current close has moved from above to below the older close, or the reverse. In a sideways market this happens repeatedly.
  • Extreme readings: a very large value means an unusually fast move. Because ROC has no fixed bounds, “extreme” can only be judged against that asset’s own history, and history is not a boundary.

Some traders also compare ROC peaks with price peaks to look for divergence.

Going deeper

The drop-off effect

ROC depends on two prices, so it can move when only the old one changes.

ROC falls while price stands stillHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market closes at 110 on period 11 and again at 110 on period 12, with a 10-period ROC.

  • On period 11, the close 10 periods earlier was 100. ROC = 100 × (110 - 100) / 100 = 10%.
  • On period 12, the close 10 periods earlier was 105. ROC = 100 × (110 - 105) / 105 = about 4.8%.

ROC roughly halved although price did not change. The fall describes what happened ten periods ago, not anything new.

This is why a sharp turn in ROC is worth checking against the chart before reading meaning into it.

Using it with context

Practitioners who use ROC generally read it against market structure. In an uptrend, ROC tends to spend most of its time above zero and dips toward zero during pullbacks. In a range it oscillates around zero with no lasting bias. The structure explains the reading, not the other way round.

ROC is often described as a “leading” indicator because it can turn before price does. The page on lagging and leading indicators explains why that label does not make it predictive.

What it does NOT mean

  • A zero-line cross is not a signal. It states that the current close passed the close N periods ago. That is arithmetic about the past.
  • High ROC does not mean “too far, too fast”. Strong trends can hold large positive or negative readings for a long time.
  • Falling ROC does not mean falling price. Price can keep rising while ROC falls, as long as it rises more slowly than before.
  • ROC does not describe the path. Two very different journeys between the same two closes give the same number.

Common mistakes

  1. Inventing fixed overbought and oversold levels. ROC is unbounded, so a level that marked extremes last year may be ordinary this year.
  2. Ignoring the drop-off effect. A spike leaving the lookback window moves ROC as much as a spike entering it.
  3. Reading every zero cross as meaningful. Around flat prices the line crosses zero constantly.
  4. Stacking it with other momentum tools. ROC, RSI and Stochastic are all computed from recent closes and largely repeat each other.
  5. Optimising the period. A period tuned on past data mostly measures how well you searched.

Risks and limitations

  • ROC uses only two past closes and ignores everything between them.
  • It is unsmoothed, so it is noisy, especially with short periods.
  • It contains no information about volume, volatility or value.
  • Like every price-derived indicator, it summarises what has happened and cannot say what happens next.

Key takeaway

ROC is the percentage gap between the current close and the close N periods ago. It describes the speed of a past move, and crossing zero or reaching an extreme is a description, not an instruction.
