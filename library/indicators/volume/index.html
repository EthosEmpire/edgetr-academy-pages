Volume

Volume is the amount of an asset traded during each candle, shown as bars beneath the price chart. It measures how much activity stood behind a price move.

Why it matters

Almost every indicator on this shelf is calculated from price. Volume is different. It is a second, independent stream of data: not where price went, but how much trading took place on the way.

That independence is what makes it valuable. Two candles can look identical on the price chart while one involved ten times the activity of the other. Only volume data shows that difference. For the underlying concept, see trading volume explained.

What it looks like

Volume appears as a row of vertical bars below the chart, one per candle. Many platforms add a moving average of volume so that each bar can be compared with recent activity.

How it is calculated

Volume is not really calculated. It is counted. For each candle, the exchange adds up the size of every trade that happened in that period.

  • On a 1-hour chart, each bar is the total traded in that hour.
  • The unit depends on the market and platform: shares for stocks, contracts for futures, and for crypto either units of the asset or their value in the quote currency.

The one common calculation is relative volume, which compares a bar with the recent average.

Relative volume = volume of this candle / average volume of the last N candlesN is often 20. A value of 1 is average. A value of 2 means twice the usual activity.

In plain words

Think of volume as the size of the crowd at a vote. Price is the result of the vote. A result backed by a huge turnout and a result backed by a handful of people look the same on the scoreboard. Volume tells you how many showed up. It does not tell you how they vote next time.

Settings and what they change

Raw volume has no settings. The volume average does: a shorter period (for example 10) makes “normal” shift quickly, so fewer bars stand out. A longer period (for example 50) gives a steadier baseline. Choosing the period that makes past spikes line up neatly with past turns is overfitting.

How to read it

Volume is read together with price. These are common interpretations, not rules.

Price Volume Commonly interpreted as
Rising Above average Broad participation in the advance
Rising Below average An advance on thin participation
Falling Above average Broad participation in the decline
Falling Below average A decline on thin participation
Flat Unusually high Heavy trading without progress. Large amounts changed hands at this level
  • Rising volume means activity is increasing. Falling volume means interest is fading. Neither has a direction of its own.
  • A volume spike marks a moment when far more than usual was traded. Spikes appear at the start of moves, at the end of moves and around news.

Going deeper

Volume and breakouts

Traders often look at volume when price leaves a range. A breakout on high relative volume is commonly read as having wide participation. One on low volume is commonly read with more caution, since fewer traders took part. Both kinds can continue, and both kinds can turn into fakeouts.

Same breakout, different crowdHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset has traded between 95 and 100 for weeks. Its 20-period average volume is 1,000 units per candle.

Candle A closes at 102 on 2,500 units. Relative volume = 2,500 / 1,000 = 2.5.

Candle B, in a separate scenario, closes at 102 on 600 units. Relative volume = 600 / 1,000 = 0.6.

On the price chart the two candles are the same. Candle A happened with two and a half times the usual activity, Candle B with less than usual. That is a real difference in participation. It is not a verdict on which breakout holds.

Volume is not the same everywhere

  • Crypto volume is fragmented. Each exchange reports only its own trades, so the volume on your chart is one venue’s share, not the whole market.
  • Reported volume can be unreliable. Some venues have shown figures inflated by wash trading, where one party trades with itself.
  • Time of day matters. Activity follows daily and weekly rhythms. A bar can be “high” simply because of the hour.
  • Volume is linked to liquidity but is not identical to it. A market can trade heavily and still move sharply.

Related tools

Several indicators process volume further: OBV keeps a running total, VWAP weights the average price by volume, and volume profile shows volume by price level instead of by time.

What it does NOT mean

  • High volume does not mean price goes up. It means a lot was traded. Heavy volume accompanies sharp falls as readily as sharp rises.
  • A green volume bar is not “buying volume”. Buyers and sellers traded the same amount. The colour only copies the candle’s direction.
  • High volume does not confirm a move. It shows participation. Well-attended moves reverse too.
  • Low volume does not mean a move is fake. Markets can drift a long way on quiet trading.
  • Volume on one exchange is not the market’s volume. It is that venue’s slice.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading raw numbers without a baseline. 5,000 units is meaningless until compared with what is normal for that market and time.
  2. Treating volume colour as buyer or seller pressure. Standard volume bars cannot show who was more aggressive.
  3. Comparing volume across exchanges or assets. Units, venues and reporting differ.
  4. Assuming a spike marks a turning point. Spikes occur at beginnings, endings and in the middle of moves.

Risks and limitations

  • Volume has no direction. It must always be read with price and market structure.
  • Data quality varies by market and venue, and some markets have no central volume figure at all.
  • The bar for the current candle is incomplete until the candle closes.
  • Like every chart tool, volume records what has happened. It does not show orders that are waiting or intentions that have not been acted on.

Key takeaway

Volume counts how much was traded in each candle. It shows the level of participation behind a price move, which adds context to the move without settling what comes after it.
