Keltner Channels

Keltner Channels are a volatility envelope: an exponential moving average with an upper and a lower line placed a multiple of the Average True Range away from it.

Why it matters

A price that is 5 away from its average means little without knowing how much that market normally moves. In a quiet market, 5 may be a large move. In a wild one, it may be an ordinary candle.

Keltner Channels put that comparison on the chart. They draw a band around a moving average whose width follows the market’s recent volatility, so you can see at a glance whether price is close to its average or unusually far from it, by that market’s own recent standards.

What it looks like

The indicator is drawn on the price chart as three lines:

  • Middle line: an exponential moving average of closing prices, commonly 20 periods.
  • Upper line: the middle line plus a multiple of the Average True Range.
  • Lower line: the middle line minus the same amount.

In plain words

Imagine a road whose lanes get wider in bad weather and narrower in calm weather. The centre line follows the road’s general direction. A car near the edge of a wide lane and a car near the edge of a narrow lane are both “near the edge”, although they are very different distances from the centre. The lane markings describe the road so far. They do not steer the car.

How it is calculated

  1. Calculate the EMA of closes (the middle line).
  2. Calculate ATR, the average size of recent candles including gaps.
  3. Multiply ATR by a chosen number, commonly 2.
  4. Add the result to the EMA for the upper line and subtract it for the lower line.
Upper = EMA + multiplier × ATRLower = EMA - multiplier × ATR. The middle line is the EMA itself.

Same price, different channelHypothetical illustration

All values are hypothetical. The EMA is 100 and the multiplier is 2.

Quiet market: ATR is 2.5. Upper line = 100 + 2 × 2.5 = 105. Lower line = 100 - 2 × 2.5 = 95. The channel is 10 wide.

Volatile market: ATR is 4. Upper line = 100 + 2 × 4 = 108. Lower line = 100 - 2 × 4 = 92. The channel is 16 wide.

A close at 106 is outside the first channel and inside the second. The price is the same. What changed is how large a move 6 above the average is, compared with recent candles.

Settings and what they change

Setting Common value Effect of changing it
EMA period 20 Shorter: the centre line hugs price and turns quickly. Longer: smoother and slower
ATR period Often 10 to 20, depending on the platform Shorter: width reacts quickly to a few large candles. Longer: width changes slowly
Multiplier 2 Smaller: narrow channel that price leaves often. Larger: wide channel that price rarely touches

No combination is “correct”. A narrow channel produces many touches and a wide one produces few, and neither count says anything about what follows a touch. Choosing the settings under which past touches lined up best with past turns is overfitting.

The channel is named after Chester Keltner, who described an early version in 1960. The EMA-and-ATR form found on most platforms today is a later revision, which is one reason defaults differ between platforms.

How to read it

  • Slope: the channel tilts with the EMA. A rising channel describes a market whose average price has been rising.
  • Width: a widening channel means recent candles have been larger. A narrowing one means they have been smaller.
  • Price near the middle line: price is close to its recent average.
  • Price beyond an outer line: the move is large relative to recent candle sizes. In a strong trend, price can travel along or outside an outer line for many candles. In a range, moves beyond the lines are more often brief.

Some traders treat the lines as a form of dynamic support and resistance. That is a habit of interpretation: the lines are calculations, and no orders sit at them because of the indicator.

Going deeper

Bollinger Bands and Keltner Channels are close relatives. Because ATR is itself a smoothed average, Keltner lines tend to be steadier, while Bollinger Bands pinch and flare more dramatically. Some chartists overlay the two and describe the moments when the Bollinger Bands sit entirely inside the Keltner Channels as a volatility “squeeze”. That label describes compression that has already happened. It does not say when it ends or in which direction price moves afterwards.

Donchian Channels are a third envelope, built from recent highs and lows instead of an average. All three describe volatility, so placing them together adds little new information.

What it does NOT mean

  • A close above the upper line does not mean price is “too high”. It means the move is large compared with recent candles. Strong trends produce many such closes.
  • A close below the lower line does not mean price is “cheap”. The channel knows nothing about value.
  • The lines are not barriers. Price is not contained by them, and it does not have to return to the middle line.
  • A narrow channel does not predict a big move. Quiet periods end eventually, but the channel cannot say when or which way.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading touches as signals. A touch of an outer line is a description of distance from the average, nothing more.
  2. Fading every move outside the channel. In a trend, price can stay outside for a long time.
  3. Comparing channels across platforms without checking settings. Different ATR periods and multipliers give visibly different channels.
  4. Stacking several envelopes. Keltner, Bollinger and Donchian lines on one chart mostly repeat each other.
  5. Tuning the multiplier to past turning points. A fit to old data is not evidence about new data.

Risks and limitations

  • Both ingredients, the EMA and ATR, are averages of past candles, so the channel always lags price.
  • After a sudden shock, the channel widens only once the large candles are already in the data.
  • It contains no information about volume, liquidity or news.
  • It describes how unusual a move is relative to the recent past, and the recent past is not a limit on the future.

Key takeaway

Keltner Channels are an EMA with lines a multiple of ATR above and below it. They show how far price is from its average relative to recent volatility, and a touch or a close outside them is a description, not an instruction.

Sources and further reading

  • New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, J. Welles Wilder Jr. (1978). Introduced the Average True Range used in the modern Keltner calculation
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999). Covers moving averages, envelopes and bands
