Timeframes on a Chart

A chart's timeframe sets how much time each candle covers. Changing it regroups the same trades into different candles, which changes the picture and every indicator value.

Why it matters

The timeframe selector is the control beginners use most and think about least. One click turns a calm chart into a jagged one, or a sharp fall into a small dip. Nothing about the market changed in that click.

A timeframe is the amount of time each candle covers. Understanding what the selector does, and what it leaves untouched, explains why two people can look at the same market and describe opposite things without either being wrong.

About the platform named here

TradingView is used as the familiar example. edgeTR Academy is not affiliated with TradingView, and the same ideas apply to other charting tools.

What it looks like

On a daily chart, one candle summarises a whole day. On a 4-hour chart the same day is six candles, on a 1-hour chart it is 24, and on a 15-minute chart it is 96. These counts assume a market that trades around the clock, as crypto does. See A Market That Never Closes.

In plain words

Timeframes work like zoom levels on a map. The country view shows the motorways and hides the side streets. The street view shows every corner and hides where the motorway leads. It is one territory, and each zoom level is suited to a different question.

How it works

Every candle is built from the trades inside its period, as described in Candle Anatomy. A longer candle is the summary of the shorter candles inside it. Its open is the first one’s open, its close is the last one’s close, and its high and low are the extremes of the group.

So a higher timeframe contains no extra data. It contains less detail, arranged so that the larger movement is easier to see.

How to read it: what changes and what stays

When the timeframe is switched Result
The candles Redrawn from the same trades
Indicator values Recalculated on the new candles
Drawings Stay at the same price and time
The prices that traded Unchanged

Drawings stay anchored. A zone drawn on the daily chart remains at the same prices on the 15-minute chart, where it can look very thick.

Indicators are recalculated. An indicator setting counts candles. A 20-period average is an average of 20 candles, whatever their length.

This is why an indicator can read high on one timeframe and low on another at the same moment. Both readings are correct for their own candles. See Indicator Settings.

Timeframe is not zoom. Zooming changes how many candles fit on the screen. The timeframe changes what each candle is.

Going deeper

The last candle is unfinished. On every timeframe, the newest candle keeps changing until its period ends.

Where a candle starts is a convention. A daily candle begins and ends at a fixed clock time that depends on the exchange and on the chart’s time zone settings. Traditional markets also have opening hours, so their charts contain gaps between sessions.

Timeframes are read together. Many chart readers look at a higher timeframe for context and a lower one for detail, an approach described in Multi-Timeframe Structure. See also Choosing a Timeframe.

Four hypothetical hours, one candleHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset prints these four 1-hour candles:

Hour Open High Low Close
1 100 103 99 102
2 102 106 101 105
3 105 107 103 104
4 104 105 100 101

The 4-hour candle for the same period is: open 100, high 107, low 99, close 101.

On the hourly chart there is a clear story: two rising hours, then two falling ones. On the 4-hour chart there is one candle with a small body of 1 and a long upper wick of 6. Both views are accurate. The 4-hour candle summarises the result and the hourly candles show the path. Neither shows what the fifth hour brings.

What it does NOT mean

  • A higher timeframe is not more true. It is a summary with less detail and less noise.
  • A lower timeframe is not more information about the future. It shows the same past in finer slices.
  • A bullish hourly chart does not mean the daily chart is bullish. Each timeframe has its own structure.
  • The same indicator setting does not mean the same thing on every chart. It covers a different stretch of time on each.

Common mistakes

  1. Leaving the timeframe out of a statement. “It is in an uptrend” is incomplete without it.
  2. Reacting to an unfinished candle. Its shape is provisional until the period ends.
  3. Comparing indicator readings across timeframes as if they were one measurement. They are computed from different candles.
  4. Dropping to lower timeframes for more certainty. More candles bring more noise, which can feel like information.

Risks and limitations

  • Daily and weekly candle boundaries depend on time zone and exchange, so charts from different sources can disagree.
  • Higher timeframes describe changes late, because a candle must close before it is final.
  • Lower timeframes show more random movement, and watching several at once can produce conflicting impressions.

Key takeaway

A timeframe decides how the same trades are grouped into candles. Switching it redraws the candles and recalculates the indicators while prices and drawings stay where they were, so every statement about a chart belongs to a stated timeframe.

Sources and further reading
