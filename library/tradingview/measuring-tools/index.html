Measuring Tools

A measuring tool reports the distance between two points on a chart as a price change, a percentage change, a number of bars and an amount of time. It measures the past and nothing more.

Why it matters

The eye is a poor judge of size on a chart. Automatic scaling makes a 3 percent move fill the screen on one chart and vanish on another. A measuring tool replaces the impression with numbers.

It is also one of the most honest tools on a charting platform, because it claims so little. It reports the distance between two points that the user picked, and the result depends on which points those are.

About the platform named here

TradingView is used as the familiar example. edgeTR Academy is not affiliated with TradingView, and the same ideas apply to other charting tools.

What it looks like

The tool is dragged from one point on the chart to another. A box appears with some or all of these figures:

Figure Meaning
Price change End price minus start price
Percentage change Price change divided by the start price
Bars The number of candles between the points
Time The bars converted to hours or days

Most platforms offer a quick ruler for a temporary measurement, and drawings that stay on the chart: a price range, a date range, or both combined.

In plain words

A tape measure tells you a wall is three metres wide. It has no view on whether the sofa is worth buying. A chart ruler is the same kind of object: it answers “how far” and stays silent on everything else.

How it works

Percentage change = (end price - start price) / start price x 100The start price is the reference. Reversing the direction changes the percentage.

The price change is the same in both directions. The percentage is not, because it is divided by a different starting price. A rise from 80 to 100 is 25 percent. A fall from 100 back to 80 is 20 percent.

This asymmetry grows with the size of the move, and it is the reason losses need larger percentage gains to recover.

The same arithmetic is described on the Drawdown page.

How to read it

The anchors decide the answer. Measuring from the lowest wick gives one number and measuring from the lowest close gives another. If the wick low is 80 and the lowest close is 82, the rise to 100 is 25 percent from the wick and about 22 percent from the close. Neither is wrong. They are measurements of different things, so the anchor is worth stating.

Bars and time are not the same. On a market that trades around the clock, 30 four-hour bars are exactly five days. On a market with trading sessions and weekends, 20 daily bars cover about four calendar weeks. Bars count trading periods and time counts the calendar.

The scale does not change the numbers. A measurement reads the same on a linear or a log axis.

Going deeper: what people measure

The size of swings. Measuring several advances and pullbacks in the same trend shows what has been typical for that market so far. A fall of 8 percent means something different where earlier pullbacks were 3 percent than where they were 15 percent.

A planned position. Many platforms provide a drawing that shows an entry, a stop and a target as two coloured boxes, with the distance of each and the ratio between them.

This drawing calculates the risk/reward ratio from the three prices. With an entry at 100, a stop at 95 and a target at 112, the risk is 5, the reward is 12, and the ratio is 12 / 5 = 2.4. The tool performs the division. It knows nothing about how likely either price is to be reached.

Projections. A measured distance is sometimes copied forward, for example by adding the height of a pattern to a breakout point. This is a convention for marking an area of interest. A past distance places no obligation on future price.

Two hypothetical swings comparedHypothetical illustration

A learner measures two advances on a chart of a made-up asset.

Swing A runs from 50 to 65 over 12 bars.

  • Price change: +15
  • Percentage change: 15 / 50 = +30%
  • Pace: 30% / 12 bars = 2.5% per bar

Swing B runs from 100 to 120 over 30 bars.

  • Price change: +20
  • Percentage change: 20 / 100 = +20%
  • Pace: 20% / 30 bars = about 0.67% per bar

On a linear chart, Swing B looks bigger because 20 points is taller than 15. The measurements show that Swing A was the larger move in percentage terms and almost four times as fast. That is a statement about the past, and it corrects a visual impression. It does not indicate what a third swing would look like.

What it does NOT mean

  • A measurement is not a forecast. It describes the distance between two points that already exist.
  • A projected distance is not a target that price must reach. Copying a past move forward is a convention.
  • A good ratio on a position diagram is not a good trade. The ratio compares two distances and ignores the chance of each outcome.
  • A percentage is not absolute. It depends on the starting point and on the anchors chosen.
  • A position diagram is not an order. It exists only on the chart.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating a rise and a fall of the same percentage as equal. A 20% fall needs a 25% rise to be undone.
  2. Mixing wick and close anchors between measurements. The results are then not comparable.
  3. Stretching the target on a position diagram to improve the ratio. The ratio improves and nothing about the market changes.

Risks and limitations

  • Results depend on anchor points chosen by eye, so two people can measure the same swing differently.
  • Wick extremes vary between exchanges, which changes measurements made from them.
  • A precise number can give a false sense of rigour to an informal judgment.

Key takeaway

A measuring tool turns the distance between two chosen points into price, percentage, bars and time. It corrects what the eye gets wrong about size and says nothing about what price does next.
