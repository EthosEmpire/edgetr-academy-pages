Why it matters

A charting screen can look intimidating: rows of buttons, coloured lines, numbers changing several times a second. Underneath, almost every charting platform is built from the same small set of parts.

Learning those parts once has two benefits. It removes the feeling that the screen holds hidden knowledge, and it makes the skill portable, because the same parts appear under similar names in other tools.

About the platform named here

TradingView is used as the familiar example. edgeTR Academy is not affiliated with TradingView, and the same ideas apply to other charting tools.

What it looks like

Symbol selector. A symbol is the short code for one market, such as an asset priced in a currency on a particular exchange. The same asset usually has many symbols: one per exchange, and separate ones for spot markets and for derivative contracts. Their prices are close and not identical.

Timeframe selector. This sets how much time each candle represents. It changes how the data is grouped. The underlying trades are the same. See Timeframes on a Chart.

Price pane. The main area. It shows price as candles by default on most platforms, and other chart types can be selected.

Time axis. The horizontal axis along the bottom. The newest data is on the right.

Price axis. The vertical axis at the side. It can usually be switched between a linear and a logarithmic scale, as explained in Price Scales: Log vs Linear.

Drawing toolbar. Tools for marking the chart by hand: trend lines, horizontal levels and zones, text notes and measuring tools.

Indicator panes. Some indicators are drawn over price. Others get their own pane below, with a separate vertical scale. See Adding Indicators.

Watchlist. A saved list of symbols. See Watchlists & Layouts.

In plain words

A charting screen is like a car dashboard. The speedometer and the fuel gauge report what is happening. They do not steer. A person who understands each dial can sit in a different car and find the same dials in slightly different places.

How to read it

A few small elements do a lot of work:

Element What it shows
Crosshair The exact price and time under the pointer
Data readout The open, high, low and close of the candle under the pointer
Last-price label The most recent traded price, marked on the price axis
Volume bars How much was traded in each candle, where the data source reports it

The four values in the readout are explained in Candle Anatomy.

The last candle on the right is still forming. Its close, its high and its low can all change until its period ends, and so can every indicator value calculated from it.

Going deeper

The picture depends on the scaling. Most platforms fit the price axis automatically to whatever is visible. Zoom in on a quiet week and a 2 percent move fills the screen. Zoom out to several years and the same move is invisible. Steepness on screen is a product of the axes, so percentages are a safer basis for comparing moves than appearances.

The data has a source. A chart shows what one exchange or data provider reported, and quotes may be live or delayed.

The chart and the trading account are separate. A charting platform displays and annotates data. Orders are placed with a broker or an exchange, and a mark on a chart is not an order.

Reading a hypothetical screenHypothetical illustration

A learner opens a chart and notes what each part says.

  • Symbol: a made-up asset, ABC, priced in a dollar stablecoin on one exchange. This is a spot market.
  • Timeframe: 4 hours. Each candle covers four hours of trading.
  • Readout for one candle: open 100, high 104, low 99, close 103. The candle is bullish, because the close is above the open, with a range of 5.
  • Price axis: linear, automatically fitted to a visible range of 90 to 110.
  • Lower pane: an RSI line with its own scale from 0 to 100.
  • Last candle: still forming, with about an hour left.

Every item on this list is a fact about the display or about past trades. None of it says what the next candle will do.

What it does NOT mean

  • A professional-looking screen does not mean better analysis. Extra panels add information and also add clutter.
  • A steep line on screen is not a large move. Steepness depends on how the axes are scaled.
  • A chart is not the whole market. It shows one symbol from one data source.
  • The newest candle is not final. Its values, and indicator values based on it, change until it closes.

Common mistakes

  1. Comparing two charts without checking the symbol. Spot and derivative symbols, or two exchanges, show different prices.
  2. Judging size by eye. Automatic scaling makes small moves look large.
  3. Reading a forming candle as finished. A shape can change completely in its final minutes.
  4. Filling every pane. Each added element hides some of the price it was meant to explain.

Risks and limitations

  • Data can be delayed, incomplete or revised, and it differs between sources.
  • Interfaces and available features change, so screenshots and tutorials go out of date quickly.
  • A tidy display can create a sense of precision that the underlying market does not have.

Key takeaway

A charting screen is a price pane with two axes, selectors for the market and the candle length, tools for marking by hand, panes for calculations and a list of saved markets. Each part displays or organises information, and none of them adds knowledge of what comes next.
