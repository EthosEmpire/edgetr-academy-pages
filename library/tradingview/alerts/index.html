Alerts

A chart alert is a notification that fires when a condition set by the user becomes true, such as price crossing a level. It is not an order, and it is not a signal to act.

  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Nobody can watch a chart all day, and trying to is a reliable way to make worse decisions. An alert hands the watching to the platform. The user describes a condition, and the platform sends a message when it becomes true.

The feature is simple, and it is surrounded by two misunderstandings. One is that an alert does something in the market. The other is that an alert firing means something should be done. Neither is true.

About the platform named here

TradingView is used as the familiar example. edgeTR Academy is not affiliated with TradingView, and the same ideas apply to other charting tools.

How it works

An alert has three parts: a condition, a rule for how often it may fire, and a delivery method.

Part Common options
Condition on price Crossing a value, moving above or below it, entering or leaving a range
Condition on a drawing Price crossing a horizontal line, a trend line or the edge of a zone
Condition on an indicator A value crossing a threshold, or two lines crossing
Frequency Once only, or every time the condition occurs
Evaluation As soon as price touches, or only when a candle closes
Delivery On-screen message, sound, email or phone notification

Many platforms evaluate alerts on their own servers, so the chart does not need to stay open.

In plain words

An alert is a doorbell. It tells you someone is at the door. It does not open the door, it does not tell you who is there, and it has no opinion on whether to let them in.

How to read it: alert and order

An order is an instruction to a broker or exchange to buy or sell, as described in Order Types. A sell stop order at 95 becomes an order to sell when price reaches 95. An alert at 95 sends a message when price reaches 95, and the position, if there is one, is exactly as it was.

This matters most for risk. An alert at a level where someone intends to exit protects nothing. If the message arrives late, is missed, or arrives while the person is asleep, the position is still open.

Some platforms can forward an alert message to other software. Whatever that software then does is a separate system with its own risks. The alert itself remains a message.

Going deeper

Touch or close

A condition can become true for a moment and then stop being true. An alert set to fire on touch reports every brief spike through a level. An alert set to fire on a candle close reports only when the period ends beyond it. The first is earlier and noisier. The second is later and quieter. This mirrors the difference between a wick and a close discussed in Fakeouts.

Indicator alerts have the same issue. An indicator’s value on the forming candle changes with every trade, so a threshold can be crossed mid-candle and uncrossed by the close.

Alerts and attention

Used sparingly, alerts reduce screen time. A few alerts at prices chosen in advance mean the chart can be closed. Used heavily, they do the opposite. Dozens of alerts across many markets produce a stream of interruptions, each one feeling urgent, which feeds FOMO and overtrading.

People who keep a trading plan often decide what a level means before setting the alert.

One hypothetical level, two alertsHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset trades at 112 on a 4-hour chart. A zone of earlier highs sits at 119 to 121. Two alerts are set at 121.

  • Alert A fires as soon as price crosses 121.
  • Alert B fires only if a 4-hour candle closes above 121.

Later, a candle rises to 121.6 and then falls, closing at 118.5.

  • Alert A fired when price touched 121. The message read “price crossed 121”, and that was true at the moment it was sent.
  • Alert B did not fire, because the close of 118.5 was below 121.

Neither alert was wrong. Each reported its own condition. Neither said whether price would continue, and no order existed because of either one.

What it does NOT mean

  • An alert is not an order. It sends a message and leaves every position unchanged.
  • An alert is not a signal. It reports that a condition written earlier became true, and carries no judgment.
  • An alert is not protection. A notification at an exit level closes nothing.
  • A delivered alert is not assured. Messages can be delayed, filtered or missed, and platform outages happen.

Common mistakes

  1. Using an alert where an order was intended. The two behave differently at the moment that matters.
  2. Setting alerts without deciding what they mean. The message then arrives with no plan attached.
  3. Ignoring the touch-or-close setting. It decides how many false starts are reported.
  4. Setting too many. Constant notifications recreate the screen-watching they were meant to replace.

Risks and limitations

  • Delivery depends on the platform, the network and the device, and can fail without notice.
  • Alerts use the platform’s data feed, which can differ slightly from the price on the exchange where a position is held.
  • An alert on a drawing depends on the drawing, and inherits any error in it.

Key takeaway

An alert is a message that a condition the user wrote has become true. It saves attention, and it places no order, protects no position and gives no reason by itself to act.
