Long-Legged Doji

A Long-Legged Doji has long wicks on both sides and an open and close that are almost equal. Price swung widely and ended where it began.

Why it matters

Most indecision candles are quiet. The Long-Legged Doji is not. Price travels a long way up and a long way down inside the period, and then closes almost exactly where it opened.

That combination, a wide range with no net result, makes it one of the clearest pictures of a market that is active and undecided at the same time. It also shows why the range of a candle and the direction of a candle are two separate things.

What it looks like

A candle is usually called a Long-Legged Doji when:

  • the open and close are equal or nearly equal, so the body is a thin line;
  • both wicks are long compared with recent candles;
  • the body sits somewhere in the middle part of the range, not at either end.

No source sets an official number for any of these. A body of no more than about 5 to 10 percent of the range is a common convention for any Doji. How long the wicks must be is a matter of judgment. Some sources use the name rickshaw man when the open and close sit near the exact centre of the range.

If the body sits at the top of the range the candle is a Dragonfly Doji. If it sits at the bottom it is a Gravestone Doji.

How it forms

In plain words

Picture a football match that ends 0-0 after both teams hit the post several times. The score says nothing happened. Anyone who watched knows the match was wild. The Long-Legged Doji is that scoreline with the near misses drawn in.

Each wick is a move that was tried and reversed. The upper wick shows higher prices that did not hold. The lower wick shows lower prices that did not hold. The page on wicks and rejection covers this idea in general.

How to read it

Candle Body Wicks What it shows
Standard Doji Almost none Short on both sides Balance in a quiet period
Long-Legged Doji Almost none Long on both sides Balance after large swings both ways
Spinning top Small but visible Present on both sides A slight net move with two-sided trading

Two readings of the candle are factual. First, the period was volatile: the range was wide. Second, the period was balanced: neither side kept its gains. Anything beyond that is interpretation.

Going deeper: context decides the meaning

After a strong trend. A long run of one-sided candles followed by a Long-Legged Doji shows that the other side was able to push back hard for the first time, even though it did not win. Traders often read this as a possible loss of momentum. It can also be a pause before the trend resumes.

At a key level. At a well-watched support or resistance area, wide swings with no result show that both sides were active exactly where a decision was expected.

Around scheduled news. Wide two-way candles are common when an announcement lands and the market reprices quickly in both directions. Here the candle may say more about the event than about the trend.

Inside a range. Indecision is the normal state of a range, so the candle adds little.

Other context that practitioners commonly check:

  • Volatility. Compare the range with recent candles, or with a measure such as ATR. A “long” wick in a quiet market may be an ordinary one in a volatile market.
  • Volume. High volume means heavy two-way trading produced no net movement. Low volume with long wicks points to thin liquidity instead.
  • The next candle. The high and low of the Long-Legged Doji mark the edges of the disagreement. Many traders watch which side a later candle closes beyond, as described in context and confirmation.

A hypothetical Long-Legged Doji after a rallyHypothetical illustration

Price has risen for six daily candles from 170 to 200. The seventh candle opens at 200, rallies to 208, falls to 193 and closes at 200.5.

The body is 0.5 and the range is 15, so the body is about 3 percent of the range. The upper wick is 7.5 and the lower wick is 7. A careful reading is: “After six one-sided days, both sides moved price a long way and neither kept it.” Whether price next closes above 208, below 193 or drifts between them is still unknown.

What it does NOT mean

  • A Long-Legged Doji does not predict a reversal. It shows balance for one period. Trends often continue after it.
  • It is not a signal in either direction. The shape is neutral by construction.
  • Long wicks do not mean strong conviction. They can mean the opposite: orders were thin and price moved easily both ways.
  • It does not show which swing came first. Up then down and down then up print the same candle.
  • A wide range does not make it reliable. Size makes a candle noticeable, not informative.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating volatility as direction. A wide candle with no body carries no directional information on its own.
  2. Ignoring the cause. A Long-Legged Doji printed during a news release or an illiquid hour often reflects conditions, not a change in trend.
  3. Judging it before the close. With time left in the period, a long-wicked flat candle can still become a large body.
  4. Measuring wicks without a reference. “Long” only has meaning next to the recent candles on the same chart.

Risks and limitations

  • Wick extremes often differ between exchanges, so the candle may look less dramatic, or more, on another venue.
  • The thresholds for “nearly equal” and “long” vary between sources and charting tools.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially for neutral patterns used alone.
  • Periods that produce this candle are volatile by definition, and price can move sharply in either direction afterwards.

Key takeaway

A Long-Legged Doji says the period was wide, active and unresolved. It marks a disagreement between buyers and sellers. It does not say who wins it.
