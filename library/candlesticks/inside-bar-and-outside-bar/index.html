Inside Bar & Outside Bar

An inside bar is a candle whose whole range sits within the previous candle's range. An outside bar exceeds the previous range on both sides. One shows contraction, the other expansion.

Why it matters

Most candlestick patterns come from the Japanese tradition and compare candle bodies. Western bar-chart reading has two simpler terms that compare whole ranges, from high to low: the inside bar and the outside bar. They need no colour and no body-size rule, so they work on bar charts and candlestick charts alike.

They describe something every market does constantly: ranges contract and ranges expand. An inside bar is a period in which the market did less than in the period before. An outside bar is a period in which it did more, in both directions. Both are statements about volatility, not about direction.

What they look like

The definitions are short:

  • Inside bar: its high is below the previous candle’s high and its low is above the previous candle’s low. The larger previous candle is sometimes called the mother bar.
  • Outside bar: its high is above the previous candle’s high and its low is below the previous candle’s low.

Sources differ on ties. Some count a candle with an equal high or an equal low as an inside bar, and some require both ends to be strictly inside. Charting tools make their own choice.

The two bars have close relatives among candlestick patterns. A harami is a small body inside a larger body. An engulfing pattern is a large body that covers a smaller one. Those compare opens and closes. Inside and outside bars compare highs and lows, so a pair of candles can be one without being the other.

How they form

In plain words

Think of yesterday’s range as a room. An inside bar is a day spent entirely within that room. An outside bar is a day that went out through the front door and the back door. Neither tells you which door will be used tomorrow.

It helps to picture each bar on a lower timeframe. A daily inside bar is usually a small sideways range on the hourly chart, sometimes one that narrows into a triangle. A daily outside bar is a larger swing: price pushed beyond one end of the previous day’s range, reversed, and pushed beyond the other end.

How to read them

Inside bars are commonly interpreted as a pause. After a strong candle, an inside bar shows the market holding the new prices without extending them. Several in a row describe a tightening range, which also shows up as a falling ATR. Traders often watch the mother bar’s high and low as reference prices, because a later close beyond either one means the pause has resolved. That resolution can be a lasting breakout or a short-lived fakeout.

Outside bars show disagreement over a wide range. The close carries most of the information:

Where the outside bar closes What it shows
Near its high The move below the previous low was rejected within the period
Near its low The move above the previous high was rejected within the period
Near the middle A wide, volatile period with no clear winner

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. An inside bar just below a well-watched resistance level shows hesitation where a decision was expected. An outside bar that sweeps through a prior swing low and closes back above it is a failed push at a level that mattered. In the middle of a range, both shapes are routine.

Timeframe. On weekly and daily charts these bars summarise a lot of trading. On one-minute charts they follow one another all day and mean very little.

Quiet periods. Inside bars are a natural product of low activity. Holidays, weekends and thin overnight hours produce small candles that sit inside busier ones. That is a calendar effect, not a balance between buyers and sellers.

Volume. An outside bar on high volume shows that the wide swing involved many participants. On low volume, a few orders in a thin market may have drawn it.

The next candle. Many traders wait for a later close beyond the relevant high or low, as described in context and confirmation.

Two hypothetical pairs of daily candlesHypothetical illustration

An inside bar. Day 1 has a high of 108 and a low of 100, a range of 8. Day 2 has a high of 106 and a low of 102, a range of 4. Day 2 sits inside Day 1, and its range is half as large (4 divided by 8). A careful reading is: “The market covered half as much ground and stayed within yesterday’s prices.” Direction is unknown until a later candle closes above 108 or below 100.

An outside bar. Day 1 has a high of 55 and a low of 51, a range of 4. Day 2 has a high of 56.5, a low of 50 and a close of 55.8, a range of 6.5. It exceeded the previous high by 1.5 and the previous low by 1, and it closed 0.7 below its own high. A careful reading is: “Price traded beyond both ends of yesterday’s range and closed near the top of a wider one.” The bar does not record which end was reached first, and what follows is still unknown.

What it does NOT mean

  • An inside bar does not predict a breakout direction. It shows contraction. It contains no information about which side gives way, or when.
  • An outside bar is not a reversal by definition. Outside bars also appear in the middle of trends that simply continue.
  • Neither bar is a buy or sell signal. Each compares one period’s range with the previous one.
  • A break of the mother bar is not confirmation of a trend. Moves beyond an inside bar’s range reverse often enough to have their own name: fakeouts.

Common mistakes

  1. Mixing up bodies and ranges. Harami and engulfing use opens and closes. Inside and outside bars use highs and lows.
  2. Ignoring the size of the previous candle. An inside bar within a tiny candle, or an outside bar around one, shows very little.
  3. Reading them on very short timeframes. Minute-by-minute ranges overlap constantly.
  4. Judging before the close. An inside bar is only inside once the period ends. One late push turns it into an ordinary candle.

Risks and limitations

  • Highs and lows differ slightly between exchanges and data feeds, so a bar that is inside on one chart may not be on another.
  • Range comparisons say nothing about the size or duration of any later move.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.

Key takeaway

An inside bar shows a period contained within the previous range, and an outside bar shows a period that exceeded it on both sides. They describe contraction and expansion, and direction has to come from context and later closes.
