Spinning Top

A spinning top is a candle with a small body and wicks on both sides. Price moved in both directions and finished only slightly away from where it started.

Why it matters

The spinning top is probably the most common named candle on any chart. It has a small body, a wick above and a wick below. Buyers and sellers both moved price, and the period ended with only a slight net change.

Because it appears so often, it is rarely remarkable. Its value is in what it teaches about the body: the size of the body, compared with the whole range, shows how decisive a period was. Read that way, the spinning top is a point on a scale, not a pattern to wait for.

What it looks like

A candle is usually called a spinning top when:

  • the body is small compared with the candle’s full range;
  • there is a wick above and a wick below the body;
  • the body sits somewhere around the middle of the range.

The colour of the body is not considered important. There is no official definition of “small”. A common rule of thumb is a body of roughly a third of the range or less, but clearly more than the thin line of a Doji. Sources also disagree about the wicks. Some only require the small body. Others want each wick to be longer than the body. A spinning top with very long wicks is sometimes called a high-wave candle, which is close to a Long-Legged Doji.

How to read it

In plain words

Two evenly matched people are arm-wrestling. Their hands sway one way, then the other, and when the bell rings they are just off centre. Someone is technically ahead, but nobody would call it a win.

Candle Body compared with range What the period was like
Doji Almost none A draw
Spinning top Small Close to a draw, with two-sided trading
Ordinary candle Medium to large One side made clear progress
Marubozu Nearly all One side controlled the period from open to close

The spinning top states two facts. Price traded above and below the body, so both sides were active. The close ended near the open, so neither side kept much. The page on candle anatomy explains how the four prices produce this.

Going deeper: context decides the meaning

After a run of large bodies. A spinning top following several tall candles in one direction shows that the dominant side made much less progress for one period. Traders often read this as momentum slowing. Slowing is not reversing: trends pause all the time.

As part of a larger pattern. Small bodies are building blocks of multi-candle patterns. The middle candle of a morning star or an evening star is a small-bodied candle, and its meaning there comes from the candles on either side of it.

In a cluster. Several spinning tops in a row show a market that has stopped making progress. This is typical of a range or of falling volatility, and says nothing about which way price leaves.

In the middle of nowhere. A single spinning top with no trend before it and no level nearby is simply an ordinary, undecided period.

Other context that practitioners commonly check:

  • Timeframe. On low timeframes most candles are small-bodied because little happens in a short period. A weekly spinning top after a long trend is a rarer event.
  • Volume. High volume with a small body means heavy trading produced little net movement. Low volume with a small body usually means a quiet period.
  • The next candle. The spinning top itself has no direction, so any directional reading comes from later closes. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical spinning top after a rallyHypothetical illustration

Price has risen from 42 to 50 over five daily candles, each with a tall bullish body. The sixth candle opens at 50, trades up to 52.2 and down to 48.8, and closes at 50.8.

  • Body: 50.8 minus 50 = 0.8.
  • Upper wick: 52.2 minus 50.8 = 1.4.
  • Lower wick: 50 minus 48.8 = 1.2.
  • Range: 52.2 minus 48.8 = 3.4, so the body is about 24 percent of the range.

The candle is bullish in colour, yet a careful reading is: “After five decisive days, buyers and sellers traded a 3.4 point range and buyers kept 0.8 of it.” Whether that is a rest or the start of something else is not visible in this candle.

What it does NOT mean

  • A spinning top does not predict a reversal. It shows one period of reduced progress.
  • It is not a signal in either direction. The shape is neutral, whatever colour the body is.
  • The colour does not carry much weight. A small green body and a small red body describe almost the same period.
  • It is not rare or special. On most charts a large share of candles have small bodies.
  • Several in a row do not point to a direction. A pause can resolve either way.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading every spinning top as a warning. Most are ordinary quiet periods.
  2. Confusing it with a Doji. The difference is only a threshold, and neither label changes what happened.
  3. Ignoring the candles before it. A small body matters only against the bodies that came before.
  4. Judging it before the close. Small bodies often grow in the final part of a period.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary widely, so two tools can disagree about which candles are spinning tops.
  • The pattern is so frequent that, on its own, it separates very little from random noise.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially for neutral patterns.
  • In thin markets a small body can reflect a lack of trades, not a balance between buyers and sellers.

Key takeaway

A spinning top says the period was two-sided and ended nearly flat. Use it to notice when progress slows, and look to trend, location and later candles for everything else.
