Tweezer Top & Bottom

A tweezer top is two neighbouring candles with matching highs after an advance. A tweezer bottom is two with matching lows after a decline. Each shows a price tested twice and not passed.

Why it matters

Most candlestick patterns are defined by the size and colour of bodies. Tweezers are defined by something else: a single price. Two candles next to each other stop at the same high, or at the same low, like the two tips of a pair of tweezers.

That makes the pattern a small lesson in support and resistance. A price that is reached twice and not passed has, for the moment, acted as a boundary. Candlestick references treat tweezers as a minor pattern on their own, one that matters mainly when it lines up with other evidence.

What it looks like

The conventional criteria are loose:

  • Tweezer top: after an advance, two candles in a row have matching highs.
  • Tweezer bottom: after a decline, two candles in a row have matching lows.

The matching point can be formed by wicks, by the edges of bodies, or by one of each. The two candles can be any shape and any colour.

Sources differ on almost every detail. Some allow the two candles to be separated by one or two others. Some require opposite colours. No source fixes how close “matching” has to be. On an asset quoted to many decimal places, two exactly equal highs are rare, so in practice the match is approximate and the tolerance is a judgment call.

How it forms

In plain words

You push a heavy cabinet across a room and it stops at a certain floorboard. You push again and it stops at the same board. There may be a ridge in the floor. One more push may clear it. Two stops at the same spot tell you where the resistance is, not how strong it is.

In a tweezer top, price reached a high in the first period, returned to it in the second, and stopped again. Sell orders resting near that price, buyers losing interest, or coincidence can each produce the same two candles.

How to read it

A tweezer is rarely read by itself. It is usually noticed because the two candles also form another pattern:

Second candle Combined reading
A small body inside the first body, with matching highs A bearish harami that also failed at the same high
A shooting star whose wick matches the first high Higher prices were tested and rejected at a level already reached
A hammer whose wick matches the first low Lower prices were tested and rejected at a level already reached

The matching price adds one fact to the other pattern: the extreme was not exceeded on the second attempt.

A tweezer is also a miniature of a larger idea. Viewed on a lower timeframe, a daily tweezer top often is a small double top, and a tweezer bottom a small double bottom.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. Matching highs at a level that turned price weeks ago add a second and third touch to an existing area. Matching highs in open space mark a level that did not exist before those two candles.

Timeframe. On weekly and daily charts, two periods stopping at the same price is fairly unusual. On one-minute charts it happens constantly, because price often sits still.

Precision. Levels behave more like zones than lines. Wick highs and lows also differ slightly from one exchange to another, so a tweezer on one venue’s chart may not exist on another’s.

Volume. Heavy volume at the matching price means many trades took place there without price getting through. Light volume may mean nobody tried very hard.

The next candle. Many traders treat a tweezer top as unconfirmed until a later candle closes below the low of the two candles. A close above the matching high is commonly treated as invalidation, since the boundary has then been crossed. The reverse applies to a tweezer bottom. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical tweezer bottomHypothetical illustration

Price has fallen from 80 to 64 over nine daily candles.

  • Candle 1: open 64, close 61.5, high 64.2, low 61.
  • Candle 2: open 61.5, close 63, high 63.4, low 61.05.

The two lows are 61 and 61.05. They differ by 0.05, which is less than a tenth of one percent of the price. Most readers would call that a match. The second candle also closed higher than it opened, with a body of 63 minus 61.5 = 1.5.

A careful reading is: “Price traded down to about 61 on two days in a row and closed above it both times.” A later close above 64.2 would add some confirmation. A later close below 61 would mean the level has given way.

What it does NOT mean

  • A tweezer does not mean the trend has turned. Trends often stall at a price for two periods and then continue through it.
  • It is not a buy or sell signal. It marks a price that was tested twice within a short time.
  • A matching price is not a wall. Two touches show where a boundary was, not that it will hold a third time.
  • Confirmation is not certainty. A following candle in the expected direction improves the context and can still be reversed.

Common mistakes

  1. Finding tweezers everywhere. In quiet markets and on short timeframes, neighbouring candles share highs and lows all the time.
  2. Ignoring the prior move. Matching highs inside a sideways range are just the top of the range.
  3. Treating the matching price as a floor or ceiling. It is a reference point for invalidation, not a level price cannot cross.
  4. Reading it alone. References describe tweezers as a minor pattern that gains weight only from other evidence.

Risks and limitations

  • The definition is one of the loosest in candlestick analysis, so two people can disagree on whether a tweezer is present.
  • Highs and lows vary between exchanges and data feeds, especially in fast or thin markets.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, particularly when patterns are used alone.
  • The pattern says nothing about the size or duration of any later move.

Key takeaway

Tweezers are two neighbouring candles that stop at the same high or the same low. They point to a price worth noting, and they carry weight only when location, other candle evidence and a confirming close agree.
