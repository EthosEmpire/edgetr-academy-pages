Shooting Star

A shooting star is a candle with a small body at the bottom of its range and a long upper wick, forming after an advance. It shows that higher prices were rejected.

Why it matters

The shooting star is the mirror image of the hammer. During an advance, buyers push price to a new high for the move, and by the close sellers have taken nearly all of that gain back.

It is one of the most frequently labelled candles on any chart, and one of the most frequently over-read. Learning its definition properly, including the part about location, is a good protection against treating every long upper wick as meaningful.

What it looks like

The conventional criteria are:

  • a small body at the lower end of the candle’s range;
  • an upper wick at least about twice the height of the body;
  • little or no lower wick;
  • it forms after an advance.

The body may be bullish or bearish. Some sources regard a bearish body as slightly more notable, because the close is then below the open.

Traditional descriptions, written for markets with daily sessions, also prefer the body to gap above the previous candle’s body. Markets that trade around the clock rarely gap, so in crypto this condition is usually dropped. All of these thresholds are conventions that differ between sources and charting tools.

How it forms

In plain words

A crowd surges towards a stage door and for a moment pushes through. Then security walks everyone back to where they were standing. The surge really happened. It simply did not hold.

The long upper wick is a rejection of higher prices: they were traded, but the market did not accept them by the close.

How to read it

Feature What it shows What it does not show
Long upper wick Buyers reached higher prices and could not hold them That buyers are finished
Small body near the low Little net change, with the close near the bottom That sellers are now in control
Prior advance There is a move that could reverse That it will reverse

The same shape after a decline is the inverted hammer, which is conventionally read the opposite way. With no body at all, the candle is usually called a Gravestone Doji.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. A shooting star at a well-watched resistance area or a prior swing high forms where selling was already a possibility. A wick that pokes above a level and closes back below it is also how a fakeout often appears on a single candle. In open space, during a strong uptrend, long upper wicks are common and frequently lead nowhere.

Timeframe. A daily shooting star records a full day in which a rally was reversed. A one-minute shooting star may be one order.

Volume. Above-average volume shows that many trades took place at the higher prices before they were given up. On thin volume, the wick tells you little.

The next candle. Many traders treat a shooting star as unconfirmed until a later candle closes below its low. A close above the shooting star’s high is commonly treated as invalidation, because the rejected prices have then been accepted. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical shooting star at a prior highHypothetical illustration

Price has risen from 60 to 78 over two weeks of daily candles and is approaching 82, where it turned down last quarter. The next candle opens at 78, rises to 83, and closes at 79 with a low of 77.8.

  • Body: 79 minus 78 = 1.
  • Upper wick: 83 minus 79 = 4, four times the body.
  • Lower wick: 78 minus 77.8 = 0.2.

The shape and the prior advance fit the convention. A careful reading is: “Buyers pushed above the old high and could not close there.” If a later candle closes below 77.8, the reading has some confirmation. If one closes above 83, it has failed. Neither outcome can be known from the shooting star itself.

What it does NOT mean

  • A shooting star does not mean the advance is over. Uptrends regularly print several of them and continue.
  • It is not a sell signal. It records one failed push higher in one period.
  • It does not show who was selling. The reversal may come from new sellers or from earlier buyers closing positions. The candle cannot tell them apart.
  • A longer wick is not a stronger promise. It describes a bigger intraperiod swing, which can also reflect thin liquidity.
  • Confirmation is not certainty. A lower close afterwards improves the context. It does not settle the outcome.

Common mistakes

  1. Calling every long upper wick a shooting star. Without a prior advance, the definition is not met.
  2. Ignoring the larger trend. One candle is weighed against a structure built from hundreds.
  3. Reading it before the close. With time left in the period, the wick can be filled in by a late rally.
  4. Treating the high as a ceiling. The shooting star’s high is a reference point for invalidation, not a level that price cannot cross.
  5. Relying on a tool’s label. Pattern scanners apply fixed ratios and know nothing about location.

Risks and limitations

  • The criteria vary between sources, including whether a gap is required, so the name does not describe exactly the same candle everywhere.
  • Wick highs can differ between exchanges, especially in fast or thin markets.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, particularly when patterns are used alone.
  • The pattern says nothing about how far or how long any following move might be.

Key takeaway

A shooting star shows that higher prices were tested and rejected within one period, after an advance. It becomes more interesting at a level that mattered before and with a confirming close, and it remains a description, not a forecast.

Sources and further reading
