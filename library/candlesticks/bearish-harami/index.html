Bearish Harami

A bearish harami is two candles after an advance: a long bullish candle, then a small candle whose body sits inside the first body. It shows buying pressure pausing.

Why it matters

Advances do not always end, or pause, with a dramatic candle. Sometimes the first visible change is simply that the candles get smaller. The bearish harami is the name for the plainest version of that change: one long bullish candle, then one small candle tucked inside its body.

Harami is an old Japanese word for “pregnant”, after the shape of a large body carrying a small one. The pattern is the reverse of a bearish engulfing, and it is conventionally read as milder. It records buyers failing to extend a move for one period. It does not record sellers taking control.

What it looks like

The conventional criteria, as described in standard candlestick references, are:

  • the market is in an advance, even a short one;
  • the first candle has a long bullish body;
  • the second candle has a small body that lies inside the first body: its open and close both sit between the first candle’s open and close.

Only the bodies are compared, so the second candle’s wicks may poke beyond the first body. If the second candle’s entire range stays within the first candle’s entire range, the pair is also an inside bar.

Sources do not agree on the details. Classic descriptions allow the small body to be either colour. Many charting tools require it to be bearish. No source fixes exactly how small the second body must be. In markets that trade around the clock, each candle usually opens at the previous close, so in crypto the second body tends to begin at the top edge of the first body instead of clearly inside it.

When the second candle is a Doji, the pattern is called a harami cross, which candlestick references treat as the more notable variant.

How it forms

In plain words

A crowd has been pushing forward through a doorway. For one minute the pushing stops. People may be catching their breath, or the room ahead may be full. The pause on its own does not tell you which.

After a long bullish candle, the market had every opportunity to close higher again. It did not. That is a fact about the second period. The reason for it, and what follows, are interpretation.

How to read it

Candlestick references list several features that are conventionally thought to add weight. They are observations about context, not guarantees:

Feature Why it is noted
A very small second body, or a Doji The stall is more complete
A long or steep advance before the pattern There is more of a move to pause
The pattern forms at a known resistance area Hesitation appears where sellers were active before

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. A bearish harami below a prior swing high or at a level that turned price before has more around it than one in open space. Inside a healthy uptrend, small candles after large ones are routine. Trends advance in bursts and rests.

Timeframe. A daily harami describes two full days. On very short timeframes the shape appears many times an hour, because quiet minutes follow busy ones all the time.

Volume. Lower volume on the small candle is typical of a pause. It can also simply mean a quiet session with few participants.

The next candle. Many traders regard the pattern as unconfirmed until a later candle closes below the bottom of the first body. When the very next candle does so, the three together are named three inside down. A close above the first candle’s high is commonly treated as invalidation, because the advance has then resumed. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical bearish haramiHypothetical illustration

Price has risen from 40 to 58 over eleven daily candles.

  • Candle 1: open 58, close 62, high 62.5, low 57.7. A bullish body of 4.
  • Candle 2: open 61.5, close 60.5, high 61.9, low 60.2. A bearish body of 1.

The second body runs from 60.5 to 61.5, which lies inside the first body from 58 to 62. It is a quarter of the first body’s size (1 divided by 4). A careful reading is: “After eleven rising days, one day passed without a higher close.” A later close below 58 would add some confirmation. A later close above 62.5 would mean the advance has continued through the pattern.

What it does NOT mean

  • A bearish harami does not mean the advance is over. Strong trends rest for a period or two and then continue.
  • It is not a sell signal. It describes two finished periods, not the third.
  • A small candle does not show sellers arriving. Holders taking some profit and buyers simply waiting produce the same picture.
  • Confirmation is not proof. A lower close after the pattern improves the context and can still be reversed.

Common mistakes

  1. Labelling it without an advance. Inside a sideways market, a small body within a larger one is ordinary overlap.
  2. Comparing ranges when the definition uses bodies. Opens and closes define a harami. Highs and lows define an inside bar.
  3. Deciding before the second candle closes. A small candle can grow into a large one before the period ends.
  4. Assuming milder means safer. A quieter pattern is also a less informative one.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary between sources, particularly on the second candle’s colour and its maximum size.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.
  • The pattern says nothing about how far or how long any following move might be.

Key takeaway

A bearish harami shows a strong buying period followed by a period that went nowhere, after an advance. It is a record of a pause, and whether the pause becomes a turn depends on evidence the two candles do not contain.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999)
