Gravestone Doji

A Gravestone Doji opens and closes at or near its low and has a long upper wick. Buyers pushed price up and sellers took all of it back.

Why it matters

The Gravestone Doji is the upside-down twin of the Dragonfly Doji. During the period, buyers lifted price well above the open. By the close, sellers had pushed it all the way back, and the candle finished at its low.

It appears in most lists of bearish candles, which makes it a good place to practise a core habit: separating what the candle records (a failed rally inside one period) from what people hope it predicts.

What it looks like

The candle looks like an upside-down T. By convention it has:

  • an open and close that are equal or nearly equal, so the body is a thin line;
  • a body that sits at or very near the low, with little or no lower wick;
  • a long upper wick that makes up almost all of the candle’s range.

These are conventions, not measurements with an official value. A commonly used threshold is a body of no more than about 5 to 10 percent of the high-to-low range, with a lower wick that is similarly small. Sources and charting tools differ, so the same candle may be labelled a Gravestone Doji in one place and a shooting star in another.

How it forms

In plain words

Think of an auction where the bidding climbs quickly, then the bidders drop out one by one, and the item finally sells at the starting price. The excitement was real, but it left no mark on the result. The upper wick is the record of that excitement.

The upper wick is often described as a rejection of higher prices: they were traded, but they were not accepted by the close.

How to read it

Candle Body Wicks Usual context
Gravestone Doji Almost none, at the low Long upper, no lower Any; most discussed after an advance
Shooting star Small but visible, near the low Long upper After an advance
Inverted hammer Small but visible, near the low Long upper After a decline
Dragonfly Doji Almost none, at the high Long lower, no upper Any; most discussed after a decline

The longer the upper wick compared with recent candles, the larger the rally that failed. That describes the size of the event. It does not measure what follows.

Going deeper: context decides the meaning

After an advance. This is the conventional bearish reading. Buyers reached a new high for the move and could not hold any of it. Traders often interpret this as buying pressure that may be fading, particularly when it forms at a resistance area or near a previous high.

After a decline. The same shape is read differently. An attempt to rally appeared and failed, which mostly agrees with the existing trend. Some sources compare it with an inverted hammer in this position, and that pattern is only given a bullish reading if later candles close higher.

Inside a range. It usually carries little information, unless it forms at the upper edge of the range.

Other context that practitioners commonly check:

  • Timeframe. A daily Gravestone Doji summarises a full day in which a rally was erased. On very short timeframes the shape appears constantly.
  • Volume. Heavy volume shows that many trades took place at the higher prices before they were abandoned.
  • The next candle. A following close below the Gravestone’s low agrees with the bearish reading. A close above its high contradicts it, because the rejected prices have then been accepted. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical Gravestone Doji near a prior highHypothetical illustration

Price has climbed from 105 to 120 over a week of daily candles and is approaching 125, where it turned down two months ago. The next candle opens at 120, rises to 125, and closes at 120 with a low of 119.9.

The body is zero and the range is 5.1, almost all of it upper wick. A careful reading is: “Buyers reached the old high and gave back all 5 points, at a level that mattered before.” The candle does not say the advance has ended. A later close above 125 would mean the higher prices were accepted after all.

What it does NOT mean

  • A Gravestone Doji does not mean price will fall. It shows that one push higher failed. Advances often resume after it.
  • It is not a sell signal. It describes a single period and nothing more.
  • It is not bearish everywhere. In the middle of a range, or on a tiny timeframe, it is usually noise.
  • A long wick does not prove heavy selling. Buyers stepping away can produce the same shape as sellers stepping in.
  • Confirmation does not make it certain. A lower close on the next candle improves the context. Failures after confirmation are common.

Common mistakes

  1. Taking the name literally. “Gravestone” is a description of the shape, not a statement about the trend.
  2. Ignoring what came before. Without a prior advance, the conventional bearish reading has nothing to rest on.
  3. Judging the candle before it closes. A long upper wick with time left in the period can still be filled in by a late rally.
  4. Arguing over the label. Whether it is a Gravestone Doji or a shooting star depends on a body threshold. The wick shows the same thing.

Risks and limitations

  • The high of the wick can differ between exchanges, so the same period may not look like a Gravestone Doji on every venue.
  • Identification depends on body and wick thresholds that vary between sources and tools.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially without context.
  • The candle hides the path. A brief spike on thin liquidity and a sustained rally that slowly faded can print the same shape.

Key takeaway

A Gravestone Doji records a rally that was fully erased within one period. After an advance, at a level that mattered before, it may suggest buyers are tiring, and that remains a possibility to be confirmed or invalidated, not a result.
