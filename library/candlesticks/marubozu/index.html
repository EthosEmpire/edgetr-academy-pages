Marubozu

A Marubozu is a candle that is all body, with little or no wick at either end. One side controlled the period from the open to the close.

Why it matters

Most candles show some disagreement: a wick here, a wick there. A Marubozu shows none. Price opened at one extreme of the period and closed at the other.

It is the opposite of a Doji, and together they mark the two ends of a scale that every other candle sits on. Understanding the Marubozu makes it easier to judge how decisive any candle was.

What it looks like

The name is a Japanese word commonly translated as “bald” or “shaven head”, a reference to the missing wicks.

  • A bullish Marubozu opens at its low and closes at its high.
  • A bearish Marubozu opens at its high and closes at its low.

A perfect Marubozu is uncommon in an actively traded market, so in practice very small wicks are tolerated. How small is a convention that differs between sources and charting tools. Some references also describe looser versions with a wick at one end only: an opening Marubozu has no wick on the opening side, and a closing Marubozu has none on the closing side.

Many descriptions add that the body should be long compared with recent candles. A wickless candle with a tiny range tells you only that the period was quiet.

How to read it

In plain words

Imagine a race in which one runner leads from the starting gun to the finish line and is never overtaken. That tells you who dominated this race. It does not tell you how tired the runner is, or who wins the next one.

A bullish Marubozu states two facts. Price never traded below the open, and the period ended at its highest price. The bearish version states the reverse. In both cases there is no rejection wick: nothing that was gained was given back by the close.

The candle does not state that price moved in a straight line. On a lower timeframe there may have been pauses and small pullbacks, as long as they stayed inside the open and the close.

Going deeper: context decides the meaning

Leaving a range or a level. A large wickless candle that closes beyond a well-watched level is one way a breakout appears on a chart. It shows a decisive close through the level. It does not show that the move will hold, and fakeouts can begin with strong candles.

Early in a trend. After a quiet period, a Marubozu shows that one side has taken control. Traders often read this as strong momentum.

Late in a trend. After a long run in the same direction, an unusually large Marubozu is sometimes described as a final burst of enthusiasm or panic. This is only identifiable afterwards. At the time it looks the same as continued strength.

Against the trend. A large Marubozu in the opposite direction to the prior move shows a sharp change in who controlled the period. One candle does not establish a new trend.

Other context that practitioners commonly check:

  • Size. Compare the range with recent candles or with ATR. The same wickless shape can be an ordinary period or an extreme one.
  • Timeframe. Wickless candles are common on very short timeframes and rarer on daily and weekly charts.
  • Volume. A large body on high volume shows broad participation. A large body on thin volume may reflect an empty order book.
  • The next candle. Whether later candles hold beyond the middle of the Marubozu’s body or retrace it is commonly watched. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical bullish MarubozuHypothetical illustration

Price has moved sideways between 96 and 100 for two days. A four-hour candle then opens at 100, never trades below 100, and closes at its high of 106.

  • Body: 106 minus 100 = 6, which is also the full range.
  • Recent four-hour candles have had ranges of about 2, so this one is roughly three times the usual size.

On the one-hour chart the same four hours might read 100 to 102, 102 to 101.2, 101.2 to 104, and 104 to 106. The second hour was a small bearish candle, yet the four-hour candle shows no wick because price stayed above 100 and finished at the high.

A careful reading is: “Buyers controlled this period and closed above the range.” Whether the next candles hold above 100 is unknown.

What it does NOT mean

  • A Marubozu does not mean the move will continue. It describes a completed period. Strong candles are followed by reversals as well as by continuation.
  • It is not a buy or sell signal. By the time it has closed, the move it shows has already happened.
  • No wicks does not mean no opposition. It means opposition did not push price past the open or keep it from the close.
  • A big candle is not a safe candle. A large range means price was moving fast, and fast markets can reverse just as quickly.
  • Size alone is not strength. A thin market can print a huge wickless candle from very little trading.

Common mistakes

  1. Chasing the candle. A dramatic full body is a classic trigger for FOMO. The candle records the past, not an opportunity.
  2. Ignoring where it closed. A Marubozu that ends just below a major resistance area is in a different position from one that closes clear of every nearby level.
  3. Insisting on perfection. Rejecting a candle because of a tiny wick misses the point: the idea is a dominant body.
  4. Forgetting the lower timeframe. A weekly Marubozu contains many smaller swings that the single shape hides.

Risks and limitations

  • The tolerance for small wicks varies between sources, so tools disagree about which candles qualify.
  • Open and close prices depend on the period boundary. Shifting the boundary can add or remove wicks.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially for single candles.
  • Whether a large candle marks the start of a move or its end is usually clear only in hindsight.

Key takeaway

A Marubozu shows that one side controlled a whole period, from open to close. It measures how decisive that period was, and it leaves open what the next one will be.
