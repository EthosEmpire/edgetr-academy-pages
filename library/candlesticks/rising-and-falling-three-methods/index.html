Rising & Falling Three Methods

Rising three methods is a long bullish candle, several small candles inside its range, then another long bullish candle closing higher. Falling three methods is the mirror: a pause inside a trend.

Why it matters

Most named candlestick patterns are about reversals. The three methods are the best-known candlestick patterns about continuation: a trend that rests for a few periods and then carries on.

They are worth studying for one reason above all. They show that a handful of candles against the trend is not, by itself, evidence of a reversal. What matters is how much ground those candles take back, and what happens after them.

What they look like

Rising three methods, as described in standard candlestick references:

  • the market is in an uptrend, and a long bullish candle forms;
  • a group of small-bodied candles follows, drifting lower, and they stay within the high-to-low range of the first candle;
  • a final long bullish candle closes above the close of the first candle.

Falling three methods is the mirror: in a downtrend, a long bearish candle, small candles drifting higher within its range, and a final long bearish candle that closes below the first close.

Three small candles is the textbook number, which gives the pattern five candles in total. References accept two, or more than three. The small candles are usually the opposite colour to the trend, but they may be either colour.

Exact thresholds differ between sources. Some require only the small bodies to stay inside the first candle’s range and tolerate wicks beyond it. Some want the final candle to open above the previous close, which needs a gap and is therefore uncommon in markets that trade 24 hours a day. Tools that label the pattern each choose their own rules.

How it forms

In plain words

A cyclist climbing a long hill stops pedalling hard for three turns of the road and rolls back a few metres, never as far as the last bend. Then the climbing resumes. Seen afterwards, it was a breather. Seen in the middle of those three turns, it could just as well have been the start of turning around.

The logic of the pattern is in the depth of the pause. The first candle is a strong push. If the following candles cannot even take price back below where that push began, the opposing side has achieved little. The final candle then shows the original side returning.

How to read it

Western chart reading has its own name for almost the same picture: a flag.

A bull flag corresponds to rising three methods, and a bear flag to falling three methods. Both traditions describe a pullback that is small relative to the move before it.

Feature Why it is noted
Small candles stay well inside the first candle’s range The pause gave back only part of one candle
Lower volume on the small candles than on the long ones Fewer participants were behind the pause than behind the pushes
A final candle that closes clearly beyond the first close The trend made new ground, not just a recovery
An established trend before the pattern A continuation pattern needs something to continue

These are conventions about context, not guarantees.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

It is named in hindsight. After the first candle and three small ones, nobody can know whether the fifth candle will be a long one in the direction of the trend. The label only exists once the move has resumed.

Failure cases. If the small candles close beyond the far end of the first candle (below its low in the rising version), the pause has taken back the entire push and the pattern’s definition is not met. If the final candle is weak and fails to pass the first close, the pattern is incomplete, and the chart shows a market stalling near its high or low. A later close back through the pause is commonly treated as invalidation, as discussed in pattern failure and invalidation.

Location. A rising three methods that completes just below a major resistance area is a continuation into a level where sellers were active before. Late in a very extended trend, continuation patterns still form, and one of them is the last before the trend ends.

Timeframe. Five daily candles are a trading week. On one-minute charts, five-candle sequences of this shape appear and fail continuously.

A hypothetical rising three methodsHypothetical illustration

Price has been in an uptrend for six weeks. Five daily candles follow:

  • Candle 1: open 100, close 108, high 108.5, low 99.6. A bullish body of 8.
  • Candle 2: open 108, close 106.6, high 108.2, low 106.3.
  • Candle 3: open 106.6, close 105.4, high 106.9, low 105.1.
  • Candle 4: open 105.4, close 104.4, high 105.7, low 104.
  • Candle 5: open 104.4, close 110.5, high 110.8, low 104.2. A bullish body of 6.1.

The three small candles stay inside the first candle’s range of 99.6 to 108.5: their highest high is 108.2 and their lowest low is 104. From the first close, they gave back 108 minus 104.4 = 3.6, which is 45 percent of the first body of 8. The fifth candle closes at 110.5, above the first close of 108.

A careful reading is: “Three days of selling recovered less than half of one strong day, and the next strong day made a new high.” Had candle 5 closed at 105 instead, the same first four candles would be the start of a different story.

What it does NOT mean

  • The pattern does not mean the trend will keep going. It records that the trend resumed once. Trends end after continuation patterns too.
  • It is not a buy or sell signal. It is a five-candle description that can only be completed after the fact.
  • Small candles against the trend are not a promise of continuation. The same candles begin every reversal.
  • A shallow pause does not show strength to come. It shows that the opposing side did little during those periods.

Common mistakes

  1. Naming it after four candles. Without the final long candle and its close, the pattern does not exist yet.
  2. Ignoring the range rule. Small candles that break the far end of the first candle describe a deeper correction.
  3. Looking for it without a trend. In a sideways market there is nothing to continue.
  4. Insisting on exactly three. The count is a convention. The idea is a contained pause.
  5. Reading it alone. Structure, nearby levels and volume carry more weight than five candles.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary between sources on the number of small candles, on wicks and on gaps.
  • The pattern is identified only after the trend has already resumed.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.

Key takeaway

Rising and falling three methods show a trend pausing with small candles that stay inside one long candle’s range, then resuming. They teach that a shallow counter-move is not a reversal, and that the difference is only certain afterwards.

Sources and further reading

  • Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques, Steve Nison (2001). Second edition; describes the rising and falling three methods as continuation patterns
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999). For flags and other continuation patterns in Western chart reading
