Why it matters

Every pattern on this shelf, from the Doji to the engulfing patterns, is built from the same four numbers. If you can read one candle, you can read all of them without memorising a list of names.

A candle is a summary. It compresses every trade in a period into one shape. Knowing what that summary keeps, and what it throws away, is the first step in reading a chart honestly.

What it looks like

Each candle records four prices for one period of time. Together they are known as OHLC.

Price What it is
Open The first traded price of the period
High The highest traded price of the period
Low The lowest traded price of the period
Close The last traded price of the period

Those four prices draw two parts:

  • The body (also called the real body) is the thick part between the open and the close.
  • The wicks (also called shadows or tails) are the thin lines above and below the body. The upper wick ends at the high. The lower wick ends at the low.

Colour

Colour tells you which end of the body is the open. In a bullish candle the close is above the open, so the body is read from bottom to top. In a bearish candle the close is below the open, so the body is read from top to bottom.

Many charting tools draw bullish candles green and bearish candles red. Traditional Japanese charts use white (hollow) and black (filled) bodies. Some markets, including several in East Asia, use red for rising prices and green for falling ones. Colour is a display setting, not a fact about the market, so check it before reading an unfamiliar chart.

How one candle is built

In plain words

A candle is like a one-line weather report for a day: the temperature at midnight, the hottest moment, the coldest moment, and the temperature at the next midnight. It is a useful summary, but it cannot tell you whether the heat came before or after the cold.

The same limit applies to price. A candle does not record the order in which the high and the low were made, how long price stayed at any level, or how much was traded there. Two periods with very different stories can print the same candle.

How to read it

Read the body for the net result and the wicks for what was tried along the way.

Feature What it shows
Long body Price ended far from where it started; one side controlled most of the period
Short body Open and close are near each other; little net progress
Long upper wick Higher prices traded but did not hold until the close
Long lower wick Lower prices traded but did not hold until the close
No wicks The open and the close were the extremes of the period

Named shapes are combinations of these features. A Marubozu is all body. A Doji is almost all wick. A spinning top sits in between. The page on wicks and rejection looks at the thin lines in more detail.

Measuring a hypothetical candleHypothetical illustration

A one-hour candle has an open of 100, a high of 106, a low of 98 and a close of 104.

  • The close is above the open, so it is a bullish candle.
  • Body: 104 minus 100 = 4.
  • Upper wick: 106 minus 104 = 2.
  • Lower wick: 100 minus 98 = 2.
  • Full range: 106 minus 98 = 8, so the body is half of the range.

That is everything the candle states. It does not say whether price visited 98 or 106 first.

Going deeper: timeframe changes the candle

Each candle covers one unit of the chart’s timeframe. Change the timeframe and the same trading is cut into different candles.

In a market that trades around the clock, one daily candle contains 24 one-hour candles. The daily open is the first hourly open, the daily close is the last hourly close, and the daily high and low are the most extreme hourly prices. A long lower wick on the daily chart may be a full sell-off and recovery, many candles long, on the hourly chart.

Three practical points follow:

  • Period boundaries are a convention. Crypto markets never close, so a “daily” candle starts and ends at a clock time chosen by the platform, often midnight UTC. Markets with trading sessions have a real open and close, which is one reason they show more gaps.
  • The newest candle is unfinished. Its close, colour and wicks keep changing until the period ends.
  • Volume is separate. The candle shows price only. Volume is usually drawn as bars underneath.

What it does NOT mean

  • A bullish candle does not mean the next one will be bullish. It records that the close was above the open. It says nothing about the following period.
  • A candle does not show the path. The order of the high and the low, and the time spent at each price, are lost.
  • A large candle is not automatically important. Size only has meaning compared with recent candles and with the market’s normal volatility.
  • The same shape does not carry the same weight on every timeframe. A one-minute candle summarises far less trading than a daily one.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading a candle before it closes. A candle with a long wick halfway through the period can end as a plain full body.
  2. Assuming the colour scheme. Green and red are defaults, not rules, and some charts invert them.
  3. Judging size without a reference. A body of 4 is large in a quiet market and small in a volatile one.
  4. Learning names before the four prices. Pattern names are shortcuts for OHLC relationships, and they are easier to remember once those relationships are clear.

Risks and limitations

  • Candles for the same asset can differ between exchanges and data providers, especially in the wicks.
  • In thinly traded markets a candle may be built from a handful of trades and describe very little.
  • In round-the-clock markets the open and close are cut points on a clock. Shift the boundary and the shapes change.
  • A candle describes a period that has ended. Interpretation of what it implies is opinion, not data.

Key takeaway

A candle is four prices drawn as a body and two wicks. Read the body for the net result, the wicks for what was tried and given back, and treat everything beyond that as context, not as a forecast.
