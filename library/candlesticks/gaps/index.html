Gaps

A gap is an empty space on a chart where no trades took place, because price jumped from one level to another between two candles. Gaps are common in stocks and rare in 24-hour crypto markets.

Why it matters

A gap is the chart’s record of a jump. Price was at one level, and the next trade happened at a clearly different level, with nothing in between.

Gaps matter for two reasons. Several classic candlestick patterns, such as the morning star and the piercing line, are defined with gaps between candles, which explains why they are rare on crypto charts. And gaps are a practical risk: an order cannot be filled at a price that never traded.

What it looks like

  • Gap up: a candle’s low is above the previous candle’s high.
  • Gap down: a candle’s high is below the previous candle’s low.

Candlestick references call a gap a window: a rising window or a falling window.

Usage varies. Some people call any open that differs from the previous close a gap, even if the two candles’ ranges later overlap. The stricter definition above uses highs and lows, so the empty space remains visible after both candles have closed.

How it forms

In plain words

A bakery closes at six with a loaf priced at 100. Overnight the price of flour jumps. In the morning the loaf is 110. Nobody ever paid 104 or 107. Those prices were skipped, and on a chart they would be an empty space.

In a market with fixed sessions, news and orders build up while trading is closed. The opening price is wherever buyers and sellers balance at the open, and that can be far from the previous close.

The four textbook types

Type Where it appears Conventional reading
Common gap Inside a range or in a thinly traded market Little significance. Often closed within a few periods
Breakaway gap At a breakout from a range or pattern, often on heavy volume The possible start of a move
Runaway gap In the middle of an established move The move continuing with urgency. Also called a continuation or measuring gap
Exhaustion gap Near the end of a long move A final jump that is reversed soon after

The classification has a serious limit. A runaway gap and an exhaustion gap look identical on the day they form. Which one it was depends entirely on what happens next, so the labels are mostly applied in hindsight.

Why gaps are rare in crypto

A spot crypto market never closes. Each candle normally opens at the price where the previous one closed. When news hits, the market reprices while it is open, and the chart shows a very long candle or wick where a stock chart would show a gap. This is one of the structural differences between crypto markets and stock markets.

Gaps still occur in crypto in a few situations: very illiquid pairs where minutes pass between trades, exchange outages, and crypto futures listed on traditional exchanges that close for the weekend.

“Gaps always fill” is a myth

A gap is said to be filled when price later trades back through the empty space. A popular saying holds that every gap is filled eventually.

Standard references reject it. Many common gaps are filled quickly, which is unsurprising, since price inside a range revisits nearby levels all the time. Breakaway gaps can stay open for months or years, and some are never revisited. The saying also sets no time limit, so it cannot be tested: an open gap is always described as not filled yet.

Traders often watch the edges of a gap as possible support or resistance. That is a convention about where attention gathers, not a rule about where price must go.

Going deeper: gaps and orders

A gap means the market skipped prices, and orders skip with it. A stop order typically becomes a market order once its trigger price is reached or passed. If price gaps beyond the trigger, the order is executed at the next available price, not at the chosen one. The difference is a form of slippage.

A hypothetical case: a market closes at 97 with a sell stop resting at 95. It reopens at 90. The stop triggers at the open and fills near 90, which is 5 below the level that was set. Exact order behaviour differs between exchanges and order types.

Measuring a hypothetical gapHypothetical illustration

A stock trades on Friday with a high of 50.4, a low of 49.2 and a close of 50. On Monday it opens at 53, with a low of 52.6, a high of 54.1 and a close of 53.5.

  • The open is 53 minus 50 = 3 above Friday’s close, or 6 percent of 50.
  • The gap itself runs from Friday’s high of 50.4 to Monday’s low of 52.6. It is 2.2 wide.

Two weeks later price falls to 51.5. It has entered the gap by 52.6 minus 51.5 = 1.1, which is half of 2.2. The gap is partly filled. It would be fully filled only if price traded down to 50.4. Nothing in the gap says whether that happens.

What it does NOT mean

  • A gap does not have to fill. Some do quickly, some do after years, and some never do.
  • A gap is not a signal of direction. A gap up shows a repricing that has already happened. It does not show that the move continues.
  • An open gap is not a magnet. Price is not pulled toward empty space on a chart.
  • A chart without gaps is not a market without jumps. In crypto, sudden moves appear as long candles instead.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating “gaps always fill” as a rule. It has no time limit and plenty of counterexamples.
  2. Naming the gap type too early. The same gap can be read as the start, the middle or the end of a move until later candles decide.
  3. Assuming a stop order fixes the exit price. It fixes the trigger. A gap can move the fill well beyond it.
  4. Mistaking data problems for market gaps. Missing candles from an exchange outage or a faulty feed are not a repricing.

Risks and limitations

  • Gap classification is subjective and depends on hindsight.
  • Gaps expose leveraged positions to losses larger than planned, because prices between the two candles could not be traded.
  • Gaps visible on one venue or timeframe may be absent on another, and no gap type has a dependable outcome.

Key takeaway

A gap is a range of prices the market skipped. It shows that a repricing happened, not what follows, and the belief that gaps always fill is a saying, not a rule.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999). Describes breakaway, runaway and exhaustion gaps, and rejects the idea that gaps are always filled
  • Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques, Steve Nison (2001). Second edition; covers gaps under their candlestick name, windows
