Three Inside Up & Down

Three inside up is a bullish harami followed by a third candle that closes higher. Three inside down is a bearish harami followed by a lower close. Each is a harami plus one candle of follow-through.

Why it matters

Almost every candlestick page says the same thing near the end: a pattern is unconfirmed until a later candle closes in the expected direction. Three inside up and three inside down are what happens when that sentence is turned into a pattern of its own. Each is a harami plus the confirming candle.

That makes them less a new shape to memorise and more a case study in confirmation: what it adds, and what it costs.

What they look like

Three inside up, after a decline:

  1. a long bearish candle;
  2. a small body that lies inside the first body, which makes the first two candles a bullish harami;
  3. a bullish candle that closes higher than the second.

Three inside down, after an advance, is the mirror: a long bullish candle, a small body inside it (a bearish harami), then a bearish candle that closes lower.

Exact thresholds differ between sources. Some only ask the third candle to close beyond the second candle’s close. Many ask for more: a close beyond the first candle’s body, or beyond its entire range. Sources also differ on whether the middle candle must be the opposite colour to the first. Charting tools each apply their own version, so the same three candles may be labelled by one tool and not by another.

Unlike the morning star, this pattern needs no gaps between candles. The middle candle sits inside the first body, not beyond it. That makes it one of the three-candle patterns that forms naturally in markets that trade 24 hours a day.

How it forms

In plain words

You hear one cough from the next room and wonder if someone is unwell. Then you hear a second. Two coughs are more convincing than one. They still are not a diagnosis, and by the time you heard the second, more time had passed.

A harami on its own shows a stall: a strong period followed by a period that went nowhere. The third candle shows that the stall resolved against the previous trend, at least for one period.

How to read it

The useful question is what the third candle changes.

After candle 2 (harami only) After candle 3 (three inside up)
What has been shown Selling paused Selling paused, then buyers closed above the first body
How much of the move is already visible Little Much more
Distance from the close to the pattern’s low Small Larger

Confirmation is a trade-off. Waiting for the third candle filters out the haramis that failed immediately. It also means the pattern is recognised later and further from the price that would invalidate it.

There is a second, subtler point. Requiring a third candle in the same direction guarantees that every example looks convincing in hindsight, because the examples that did not follow through are simply not called three inside up. That is a property of the definition. It is not evidence about the fourth candle.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. Three inside up at a well-watched support area or a prior swing low has more around it than one in the middle of a decline. Three inside down below a prior high works the same way in reverse.

The larger trend. On a pullback inside a larger uptrend, three inside up agrees with the bigger structure. Against a strong trend, a three-candle shift is a small event.

Timeframe and volume. Three daily candles summarise three days. On very short timeframes, the sequence large-small-medium occurs constantly. Rising volume on the third candle shows more participation behind the close.

Failure. A close back through the pattern’s extreme (below the low of three inside up, above the high of three inside down) is commonly treated as invalidation. Confirmed patterns fail too, as covered in pattern failure and invalidation.

A hypothetical three inside upHypothetical illustration

Price has fallen from 64 to 50 over eight daily candles.

  • Candle 1: open 50, close 46, high 50.2, low 45.7. A bearish body of 4.
  • Candle 2: open 46.6, close 48, high 48.3, low 46.4. A bullish body of 1.4, inside the first body from 46 to 50.
  • Candle 3: open 48.1, close 50.9, high 51.1, low 47.9. A bullish body of 2.8.

The third candle closes at 50.9, above the second close of 48, above the first open of 50 and above the first high of 50.2. It meets even the stricter versions of the definition.

Now compare the two moments. After candle 2, the close of 48 was 2.3 above the pattern’s low of 45.7, about 5 percent of 48. After candle 3, the close of 50.9 is 5.2 above the same low, about 10 percent of 50.9. More of the move is now visible, and the distance to the invalidation point more than doubled.

What it does NOT mean

  • Three inside up does not mean a decline is over, and three inside down does not mean an advance is over. Three-period counter-moves are common inside trends that continue.
  • They are not buy or sell signals. They describe a harami and one candle after it.
  • Confirmation is not certainty. The third candle is one more period of evidence, not a verdict.
  • A built-in confirmation does not make the pattern more accurate. It makes the pattern later, and it removes the failed cases from the name, not from the market.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating the third candle as proof. It is the minimum follow-through, not a guarantee of more.
  2. Skipping the harami check. If the second body is not inside the first, it is a different pattern.
  3. Ignoring how far price has moved. By the third close, a large share of the short-term move is already on the chart.
  4. Reading it alone. Structure, the prior trend, nearby levels and volume carry more weight than three candles.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary between sources, especially on how far the third candle must close.
  • The pattern is recognised late by design.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.

Key takeaway

Three inside up and three inside down are a harami followed by one candle of follow-through. They show what confirmation looks like and what it costs: more evidence, later, and further from the point of invalidation.
