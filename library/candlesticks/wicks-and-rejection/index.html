Wicks & Rejection

A wick shows prices that traded during a period but did not hold until the close. A long wick is often described as the market rejecting those prices.

Why it matters

The body of a candle tells you where price started and finished. The wicks tell you where it went in between and failed to stay. Several named patterns, including the hammer, the shooting star and two members of the Doji family, are simply candles with one long wick.

Reading wicks directly is more useful than memorising those names. It is also where over-interpretation starts, because a long wick looks like a clear message and often is not.

What it looks like

A wick (also called a shadow or tail) is the thin line between the body and the high or the low. The anatomy page covers the basics. The idea of rejection adds an interpretation: prices inside the wick were available, trades happened there, and by the close the market had moved away from them.

The opposite of rejection is acceptance: price closes beyond a level and later candles continue to trade there.

There is no fixed rule for when a wick counts as “long”. Common conventions compare it with the body (for example, at least twice the body’s height) or with the ranges of recent candles. These conventions differ between sources.

How to read it

In plain words

A shop marks an item down sharply at noon. By closing time the price tag is back where it was in the morning. Somebody clearly bought at the low price, and the low price did not last. From the tag alone you cannot tell whether one customer cleared the shelf or a hundred did.

What you see What it states What it leaves open
Long lower wick Lower prices traded and were not held to the close Whether they will be tested again
Long upper wick Higher prices traded and were not held to the close Whether they will be tested again
Long wicks on both sides Two attempts, both reversed Which side prevails
No wick The open or close was the extreme Nothing was given back this period

A wick is also a matter of timeframe. A long lower wick on a daily candle is, on the hourly chart, a decline followed by a rally. The “rejection” is a full two-way move that the daily candle compresses into a line.

Going deeper: why wicks form

A wick shows that price reversed. It cannot show the cause. Buyers with resting limit orders may have absorbed the selling. A cluster of stop orders may have triggered and then been exhausted. In leveraged markets, a wave of liquidations can drive price sharply and stop just as sharply. Or the order book was simply thin.

Context that practitioners commonly check:

  • Location. A wick that pierces a well-watched level and closes back inside it is how a fakeout often appears on a single candle. Wicks are also one reason levels are better drawn as zones than as lines.
  • Prior trend. A lower wick after a decline and a lower wick after an advance are the same mark with different conventional readings, as the hammer and the hanging man show.
  • Volume. A wick on high volume means many trades took place at the rejected prices. A wick on low volume may be a handful of orders.
  • Repetition. Several wicks into the same area are read in two opposite ways: as a level holding, or as a level being worn down by repeated tests. The chart alone does not settle which.
  • The next candle. A later close beyond the tip of the wick means the rejected prices have been accepted after all. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical wick through a support zoneHypothetical illustration

Price has pulled back to a support zone between 99 and 100. A four-hour candle opens at 101, falls to 97.5, and closes at 100.8 with a high of 101.3.

  • Body: 101 minus 100.8 = 0.2.
  • Lower wick: 100.8 minus 97.5 = 3.3.
  • The low was 1.5 below the bottom of the zone, and the close was back above the zone.

A careful reading is: “Prices under 99 traded and were not held to the close.” On another exchange the low of the same candle might be 98.4, which would make the wick look less dramatic. If a later candle closes below 97.5, the lower prices have been accepted and the rejection reading no longer applies.

What it does NOT mean

  • A long wick does not mean price will move the other way. It shows one reversed attempt. A second attempt often follows.
  • Rejection is not a signal. It is a description of where price did not close.
  • A wick does not reveal who traded or why. Absorption, stop runs, liquidations and thin books all look alike.
  • The tip of a wick is not a barrier. It is a price that traded once. Markets revisit and pass such prices regularly.
  • A wick is not final until the candle closes. During the period, what looks like a wick is only the current distance from the extreme.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading every wick as a message. On low timeframes and in quiet markets, wicks are mostly noise.
  2. Ignoring the close. The rejection idea depends on where the candle finished, not on how far the wick reached.
  3. Trusting one venue’s wick. Extreme highs and lows often differ between exchanges.
  4. Forgetting the lower timeframe. The wick hides a full move with its own structure.
  5. Treating wick tips as exact levels. A few ticks beyond a previous wick is rarely meaningful by itself.

Risks and limitations

  • Wicks are the least consistent part of candle data across exchanges and data providers.
  • In thin markets or during fast moves, a wick can be created by very little trading.
  • The same wick supports opposite interpretations, and neither can be verified from the candle.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns, including long-wick patterns, predict price is limited and mixed.

Key takeaway

A wick marks prices that were tried and not kept by the close. It is evidence of a reversed attempt inside one period, and it needs location, volume and later candles before it means more than that.
