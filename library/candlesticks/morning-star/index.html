Morning Star

A morning star is three candles after a decline: a long bearish candle, a small-bodied candle, then a long bullish candle that closes well into the first body.

Why it matters

The morning star is one of the best-known three-candle patterns. Two-candle patterns compare one period with the next. A three-candle pattern can show a sequence, and the morning star’s sequence is easy to say in one breath: sellers were winning, then nobody was winning, then buyers were winning.

It is named after the morning star in the sky, the bright point visible before sunrise. The name carries an expectation that three candles cannot deliver. They show a shift over three periods, not what the fourth period brings.

What it looks like

The conventional criteria, as described in standard candlestick references, are:

  • the market is in a decline;
  • the first candle has a long bearish body;
  • the second candle has a small body, of either colour, that sits below the first body. This middle candle is the star;
  • the third candle has a long bullish body that closes well into the first body. Many sources ask for a close above its midpoint.

Classic descriptions want the star’s body to gap below the first body, and ideally a second gap between the star and the third body. Gaps between candles are common on daily stock charts and rare in markets that trade 24 hours a day, where each candle normally opens at the previous close. In crypto, the pattern is usually accepted when the star is simply a small body at or near the low of the first candle.

Exact thresholds differ between sources: how long “long” is, how small the star must be, and how far the third candle must close into the first body.

How it forms

In plain words

Picture a swing. It travels down fast, hangs almost still at the lowest point for a moment, then travels up the other side. A chart is not a swing, and nothing forces price back up. The picture only helps you remember the three stages.

The middle candle is what separates a morning star from an ordinary bounce. A spinning top or Doji at the low shows that the selling of the first candle did not carry on. The third candle then shows a recovery of most of the first candle’s loss.

How to read it

Variant Middle candle Conventional view
Morning star Small body The standard form
Morning Doji star A Doji The stall is more complete
Abandoned baby A Doji whose wicks do not overlap its neighbours The rarest form. It needs two full gaps, so it is almost absent from 24-hour markets

Candlestick references also list features that are conventionally thought to add weight, such as a third candle that closes deeper into the first body and higher volume on the third candle than on the first. These are observations about context, not guarantees.

The two-candle relative is the piercing line: a lower open followed by a close more than halfway back up the previous body.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. A morning star at a well-watched support area or a prior swing low has more around it than one in the middle of a decline. On a pullback inside a larger uptrend, it agrees with the bigger structure.

Timeframe. Three daily candles summarise three days. On a one-minute chart, large-small-large sequences form continuously and carry little information.

Cost of waiting. By the time the third candle closes, price has already recovered a large part of the first candle. The pattern is clearer than a single candle, and it is also later.

The next candle. Many traders still look for a later close above the first candle’s open before treating the shift as confirmed. A close below the star’s low is commonly treated as invalidation, because the lowest price of the pattern has then been broken. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical morning starHypothetical illustration

Price has fallen from 150 to 118 over three weeks of daily candles.

  • Candle 1: open 118, close 110, high 118.5, low 109.5. A bearish body of 8.
  • Candle 2: open 109, close 109.8, high 110.4, low 107.6. A small bullish body of 0.8.
  • Candle 3: open 110.2, close 116, high 116.4, low 110. A bullish body of 5.8.

The midpoint of the first body is (118 + 110) divided by 2 = 114. The star’s body, from 109 to 109.8, sits below the first body, which ends at 110. The third candle closes at 116, above the midpoint. Measured from the first close, it recovered 116 minus 110 = 6 of the 8 points, or 75 percent.

A careful reading is: “A strong down day, a day of balance at lower prices, then a strong up day that took back most of the first day’s loss.” A later close above 118 would add some confirmation. A later close below 107.6 would mean the decline has continued through the pattern.

What it does NOT mean

  • A morning star does not mean the decline is over. Downtrends contain three-day recoveries that fade.
  • It is not a buy signal. It describes three finished periods and nothing certain about the fourth.
  • Three candles are not three times the evidence. They are still a very small sample of a market’s behaviour.
  • Confirmation is not proof. Morning stars that confirm and then fail are common.

Common mistakes

  1. Ignoring the prior trend. Without a decline before it, the definition is not met.
  2. Accepting a weak third candle. A small bullish candle that barely enters the first body does not show the recovery the pattern describes.
  3. Expecting gaps in a 24-hour market. Strict versions, and the abandoned baby in particular, are rare in crypto.
  4. Reading it alone. Structure, nearby levels and volume carry more weight than three candles.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary between sources on gaps, body sizes and the depth of the third close.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.
  • The pattern gives no information about the size or duration of any later move.

Key takeaway

A morning star shows a strong decline, a stall and a strong recovery over three periods, after a decline. It is a clear description of a short-term shift, and it still depends on location, confirmation and a known invalidation point.

Sources and further reading

  • Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques, Steve Nison (2001). Second edition; gives the conventional criteria for the morning star and its variants
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999)
