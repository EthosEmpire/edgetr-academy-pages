Hanging Man

A hanging man is a hammer-shaped candle, a small body on top of a long lower wick, that forms after an advance. It is read as a caution, not a conclusion.

Why it matters

The hanging man is the hammer in a different place. The shape is identical: a small body at the top of the range and a long lower wick. A hammer forms after a decline. A hanging man forms after an advance.

That makes it the best single example of the main rule on this shelf: a candle’s name and conventional reading come from its context, not from its outline. It is also a pattern that standard references treat with extra care, because the shape and the label seem to disagree.

What it looks like

The conventional criteria match the hammer’s, with the trend reversed:

  • a small body at the upper end of the candle’s range;
  • a lower wick at least about twice the height of the body;
  • little or no upper wick;
  • it forms after an advance.

The body may be bullish or bearish. Some sources regard a bearish body as slightly more notable, because the close is then below the open. The ratios are conventions. Sources and charting tools set them differently, and a tool that scans for shapes may not check the prior trend at all.

How it forms

Price opens near the high, sells off sharply during the period, then recovers to close near where it opened. The lower wick shows lower prices that were rejected by the close.

In plain words

A climber slips, drops several metres and is caught by the rope. By the end of the day they are back at the same height, so the logbook shows no change. The slip is still information: the footing was less secure than it looked.

How to read it

On a first look the candle seems positive, since buyers recovered the whole drop. The conventional reading focuses on something else: during an advance, sellers were able to push price down hard inside one period. That may be the first evidence of supply appearing after a stretch in which buyers met little resistance.

Feature The cautious reading The other reading
Long lower wick Sellers showed up with force during an advance The dip was bought, as dips in an uptrend often are
Small body near the high Little net progress for the period Price held its gains
Prior advance There is a move that could reverse The trend is intact until structure changes

Both columns are reasonable. This is why candlestick references, Nison’s among them, stress that a hanging man needs bearish confirmation before it is given weight, more so than most patterns.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. A hanging man at a well-watched resistance area or a prior swing high forms where selling was already a possibility. In the middle of a healthy uptrend, with no level nearby, long lower wicks are common and usually mark ordinary dips that were bought.

Timeframe. A daily hanging man records a real intraday sell-off. On short timeframes the shape forms constantly.

Volume. Above-average volume shows that the dip and recovery involved heavy trading. Light volume weakens any reading.

The next candle. Confirmation is commonly defined as a later close below the hanging man’s body, and more strictly as a close below its low. A close above its high contradicts the cautious reading. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical hanging man near a prior highHypothetical illustration

Price has risen from 80 to 99 over two weeks of daily candles and is near 100, where it turned down earlier in the year. The next candle opens at 99.5, drops to 96.5, and closes at 100.5 with a high of 100.7.

  • Body: 100.5 minus 99.5 = 1.
  • Lower wick: 99.5 minus 96.5 = 3, three times the body.
  • Upper wick: 100.7 minus 100.5 = 0.2.

The shape and the prior advance fit the convention. A careful reading is: “Sellers pushed price down 3 points at a level that mattered before, and buyers recovered it.” If a later candle closes below 99.5, and especially below 96.5, the cautious reading gains support. If one closes above 100.7, the candle was a dip that got bought.

What it does NOT mean

  • A hanging man does not mean the advance is over. Uptrends print this shape repeatedly and continue.
  • It is not a sell signal. It records one sharp dip that was recovered.
  • The name is not a forecast. It describes how the candle looks, nothing else.
  • It is not a hammer. The shapes match, but the hammer is defined after a decline and read the opposite way.
  • Confirmation does not make it certain. A lower close afterwards improves the context. Failed reversals are common.

Common mistakes

  1. Labelling by shape alone. Without a prior advance, the candle is not a hanging man.
  2. Skipping confirmation. The candle closes near its high, so on its own it says little that is bearish.
  3. Ignoring the larger trend. One candle against an established uptrend is weak evidence.
  4. Reading it before the close. A deep dip with time left in the period can end as a hanging man, a full bearish body or something in between.

Risks and limitations

  • Criteria vary between sources, and automated pattern labels often ignore the trend requirement.
  • Wick lows differ between exchanges, especially when a dip is brief.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, particularly when patterns are used alone.
  • The candle cannot show why price dipped. A single large order and broad selling look the same.

Key takeaway

A hanging man is a hammer shape after an advance. It notes that sellers were able to push price down sharply for one period, and it stays an open question until later candles close lower or higher.

Sources and further reading

  • Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques, Steve Nison (2001). Second edition; gives the conventional criteria for the hammer and hanging man and stresses bearish confirmation
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999)
