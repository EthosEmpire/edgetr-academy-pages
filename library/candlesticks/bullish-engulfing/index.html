Bullish Engulfing

A bullish engulfing pattern is two candles after a decline: a bearish candle followed by a bullish candle whose body covers the whole of the first body.

Why it matters

Single candles describe one period. The bullish engulfing pattern is usually the first two-candle pattern people meet, and it introduces a new idea: comparing one period with the one before it.

The comparison is simple. In the first period sellers produced a net loss. In the second, buyers produced a larger net gain that covers it completely. That is a clear change over two periods. Whether it is a change of trend is a separate question that two candles cannot answer.

What it looks like

The conventional criteria, as given in standard candlestick references, are:

  • the market is in a decline, even a short one;
  • the first candle has a bearish body;
  • the second candle has a bullish body that engulfs the first body: it opens at or below the first close and closes above the first open.

Only the bodies are compared. The second body does not have to cover the first candle’s wicks. When the second candle’s whole range covers the first candle’s whole range, Western chart reading calls it an outside bar.

Sources differ on the details. Some want the second candle to open clearly below the first close, which needs a gap. Markets that trade around the clock usually open each candle at the previous close, so in crypto a second candle that opens level with the first close and closes above the first open is widely accepted. Some sources set a minimum size for the second body. Charting tools each apply their own rules.

How it forms

In plain words

One wave draws back a little way down the beach. The next comes in and covers all the sand the first one exposed, and a bit more. You know the second wave was bigger. You do not know anything yet about the third.

A mechanical consequence is worth noticing. Everyone who sold during the first candle’s body did so at a price below the second candle’s close. The pattern does not say what those participants do next.

How to read it

Candlestick references list several features that are conventionally thought to add weight. They are observations about context, not guarantees:

Feature Why it is noted
A small first body and a large second body The shift between the two periods is more pronounced
A long or steep decline before the pattern There is more of a move to reverse
Higher volume on the second candle More trading stood behind the recovery
The second body covers several earlier bodies More than one period of selling was erased

A piercing line is a weaker relative in which the second candle closes above the middle of the first body without covering it. When a bullish engulfing is followed by a third candle that closes higher, the three together are called three outside up.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. The pattern has more around it at a well-watched support area or a prior swing low than in the middle of a decline. On a pullback inside a larger uptrend it agrees with the bigger structure.

Inside a range. Engulfing shapes appear often in sideways markets, where candles regularly overlap. Without a decline before it, the pattern’s definition is not met.

Timeframe. Two daily candles summarise two days. Two one-minute candles summarise two minutes and overlap all the time.

The next candle. Many traders treat the pattern as unconfirmed until a later candle closes above the second candle’s high. A close below the pattern’s low is commonly treated as invalidation. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical bullish engulfingHypothetical illustration

Price has fallen from 70 to 53 over nine daily candles.

  • Candle 1: open 53, close 51.5, high 53.3, low 51.2. A bearish body of 1.5.
  • Candle 2: open 51.4, close 54, high 54.2, low 50.8. A bullish body of 2.6.

The second body runs from 51.4 to 54, which covers the first body from 51.5 to 53. It is about 1.7 times as large. A careful reading is: “After nine falling days, buyers recovered all of the previous day’s loss and more.” A later close above 54.2 would add some confirmation. A later close below 50.8 would mean the decline has continued through the pattern.

What it does NOT mean

  • A bullish engulfing does not mean the decline is over. Downtrends often contain sharp two-day recoveries that fade.
  • It is not a buy signal. It compares two finished periods and says nothing certain about the third.
  • A bigger second candle is not a stronger promise. It also means price has already moved further from the low by the time the pattern is visible.
  • It does not show new buyers arriving. Short sellers closing positions can produce the same candles.
  • Confirmation is not proof. Engulfing patterns that confirm and then fail are common.

Common mistakes

  1. Ignoring the prior trend. The same two candles inside a range or after an advance are not this pattern.
  2. Comparing wicks, not bodies. The definition rests on opens and closes.
  3. Judging before the second close. A candle that engulfs with an hour left can close back inside the first body.
  4. Overlooking tiny candles. A small body engulfing an even smaller one meets the rule and shows very little.
  5. Reading it alone. Structure, nearby levels and volume carry more weight than two candles.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary between sources, particularly on gaps and minimum body size.
  • Candle opens and closes depend on the period boundary and the exchange, so the pattern may not appear on every chart of the same market.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.
  • The pattern gives no information about the size or duration of any later move.

Key takeaway

A bullish engulfing shows that one period’s gain more than erased the previous period’s loss, after a decline. It is a clear two-candle shift that still needs location, confirmation and a known invalidation point to be read responsibly.
