Evening Star

An evening star is three candles after an advance: a long bullish candle, a small-bodied candle, then a long bearish candle that closes well into the first body.

Why it matters

The evening star is the mirror image of the morning star. It appears after an advance and tells a three-part story: buyers were winning, then nobody was winning, then sellers were winning.

It is one of the most frequently quoted candlestick patterns, which is a reason to learn it carefully. A pattern with a famous name is easy to over-trust. Three candles describe three periods. They show that a short-term shift took place. They do not show whether a rally has ended or merely stumbled.

What it looks like

The conventional criteria, as described in standard candlestick references, are:

  • the market is in an advance;
  • the first candle has a long bullish body;
  • the second candle has a small body, of either colour, that sits above the first body. This middle candle is the star;
  • the third candle has a long bearish body that closes well into the first body. Many sources ask for a close below its midpoint.

Classic descriptions want the star’s body to gap above the first body, and ideally a second gap between the star and the third body. That is realistic on daily stock charts, where each session opens fresh. It is rare in markets that trade 24 hours a day, where a candle normally opens at the previous close. In crypto the pattern is usually accepted when the star is a small body at or near the high of the first candle.

Exact thresholds differ between sources: how long the outer candles must be, how small the star must be, and how deep the third close must reach.

How it forms

In plain words

Think of a three-sentence report from a football match. “The home side attacked all through the first half. For ten minutes after the break, nothing happened. Then the visitors took over.” You know how the last three stretches of play went. The final score is still open.

The star is the hinge. A small body at the top of an advance shows that the buying of the first candle did not carry on. When that small candle has a long upper wick it may also be a shooting star. The third candle then gives back most of the first candle’s gain.

How to read it

Variant Middle candle Conventional view
Evening star Small body The standard form
Evening Doji star A Doji The stall is more complete
Abandoned baby (bearish) A Doji whose wicks do not overlap its neighbours The rarest form. It needs two full gaps, so it is almost absent from 24-hour markets

Candlestick references also list features that are conventionally thought to add weight, such as a third candle that closes deeper into the first body and higher volume on the third candle than on the first. These are observations about context, not guarantees.

The two-candle relative is dark cloud cover.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. An evening star at a well-watched resistance area or a prior swing high shows a failure where sellers were active before. In open space, it is three candles with nothing around them.

Timeframe. Three weekly or daily candles summarise a lot of trading. Large-small-large sequences on a one-minute chart are continuous and mostly noise.

Cost of waiting. When the third candle closes, price is already well below the high of the pattern. The evidence is clearer than with one candle, and it arrives later.

The next candle. Many traders still look for a later close below the first candle’s open before treating the shift as confirmed. A close above the star’s high is commonly treated as invalidation, because the highest price of the pattern has then been exceeded. See context and confirmation.

A hypothetical evening starHypothetical illustration

Price has risen from 40 to 66 over four weeks of daily candles.

  • Candle 1: open 66, close 72, high 72.4, low 65.7. A bullish body of 6.
  • Candle 2: open 72.6, close 73, high 73.8, low 72.3. A small bullish body of 0.4.
  • Candle 3: open 72.4, close 67.5, high 72.7, low 67.2. A bearish body of 4.9.

The midpoint of the first body is (66 + 72) divided by 2 = 69. The star’s body, from 72.6 to 73, sits above the first body, which ends at 72. The third candle closes at 67.5, below the midpoint. Measured from the first close, it gave back 72 minus 67.5 = 4.5 of the 6 points, or 75 percent.

A careful reading is: “A strong up day, a day of balance at higher prices, then a strong down day that erased most of the first day’s gain.” A later close below 66 would add some confirmation. A later close above 73.8 would mean the advance has continued through the pattern.

What it does NOT mean

  • An evening star does not mean the advance is over. Uptrends regularly absorb a three-day setback and continue.
  • It is not a sell signal. It describes three finished periods and nothing certain about the fourth.
  • A famous name is not extra evidence. The pattern carries only as much weight as its context gives it, however often it is quoted.
  • Confirmation is not proof. Evening stars that confirm and then fail are common.

Common mistakes

  1. Labelling it without an advance. The pattern is defined by what came before it.
  2. Accepting a weak third candle. A small bearish candle that barely enters the first body does not show the giveback the pattern describes.
  3. Judging before the third close. A candle that is deep in the first body with an hour left can still recover.
  4. Reading it alone. Structure, nearby levels and volume carry more weight than three candles.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary between sources on gaps, body sizes and the depth of the third close.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.
  • The pattern says nothing about how far or how long any following move might be.

Key takeaway

An evening star shows a strong advance, a stall and a strong giveback over three periods, after an advance. It describes a short-term shift, and whether that shift matters depends on location, confirmation and a known invalidation point.

Sources and further reading

  • Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques, Steve Nison (2001). Second edition; gives the conventional criteria for the evening star and its variants
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999)
