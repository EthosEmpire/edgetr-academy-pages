Three Outside Up & Down

Three outside up is a bullish engulfing pattern followed by a third candle that closes higher. Three outside down is a bearish engulfing followed by a lower close.

Why it matters

A bullish engulfing or bearish engulfing pattern is two candles: a small body, then a larger body of the opposite colour that covers it. Descriptions of both end with the same caution, that the pattern is unconfirmed until a later candle closes in the same direction.

Three outside up and three outside down build that later candle into the definition. They are an engulfing pattern plus one period of follow-through. Like their siblings, three inside up and down, they are best studied as a lesson in what confirmation adds and what it costs.

What they look like

Three outside up, after a decline:

  1. a bearish candle;
  2. a bullish candle whose body engulfs the first body, opening at or below the first close and closing above the first open;
  3. a third candle that closes higher than the second.

Three outside down, after an advance, is the mirror: a bullish candle, a bearish candle whose body engulfs it, then a third candle that closes lower.

Exact thresholds differ between sources. Some require the third candle to have a body in the direction of the pattern, and others only compare closes. Engulfing definitions themselves vary on whether the second candle must open beyond the first close, which needs a gap, or may open level with it. In markets that trade 24 hours a day, each candle usually opens at the previous close, so the level open is the normal case and is widely accepted.

Here “outside” describes the second body wrapping around the first. That is related to, but not the same as, an outside bar, which compares whole ranges from high to low.

How it forms

In plain words

A ball rolling one way is hit hard and starts rolling back. That is the engulfing candle. A moment later it is still rolling in the new direction. That is the third candle. You know the hit was real. You do not know how far the ball goes, or whether something is about to stop it.

How to read it

In a three inside pattern, the second candle is quiet and the third does the moving. In a three outside pattern, the second candle has already reversed the whole of the first, and the third extends the move. Two consequences follow.

First, the pattern is usually recognised further from its starting point. Two candles in a row have moved against the prior trend before the name applies.

Second, the price that would commonly be treated as invalidation, the extreme of the pattern, is further away from the third close. How that distance relates to risk is covered in stop placement.

As with any pattern that includes its own confirmation, the definition excludes the engulfing patterns that failed at once. That makes every textbook example look clean. It says nothing about the candle after the third.

Going deeper: context decides the weight

Location. Three outside down at a well-watched resistance area or a prior swing high shows a failure where sellers were active before. Three outside up at a known support area works the same way in reverse. In open space, the same candles have little around them.

The larger trend. A three-candle shift against a strong, long-running trend is a small event. The same shift at the end of a pullback, in the direction of the larger trend, agrees with the bigger structure.

Timeframe and volume. Three daily candles summarise three days. On very short timeframes, engulfing bodies and follow-through candles occur constantly. Higher volume on the second and third candles shows more participation behind the shift.

Failure. A close back beyond the pattern’s extreme is commonly treated as invalidation. Confirmed patterns fail as well, which is the subject of pattern failure and invalidation and of context and confirmation.

A hypothetical three outside downHypothetical illustration

Price has risen from 30 to 44 over ten daily candles.

  • Candle 1: open 44, close 45.2, high 45.4, low 43.8. A bullish body of 1.2.
  • Candle 2: open 45.3, close 43.2, high 45.6, low 43. A bearish body of 2.1 that covers the first body from 44 to 45.2.
  • Candle 3: open 43.1, close 41.8, high 43.4, low 41.6. A bearish body of 1.3.

The third candle closes at 41.8, below the second close of 43.2. The pattern’s high is 45.6.

Compare the two moments. After candle 2, the close of 43.2 was 2.4 below the pattern’s high, about 5.6 percent of 43.2. After candle 3, the close of 41.8 is 3.8 below it, about 9 percent of 41.8. A careful reading is: “After a ten-day advance, sellers reversed one day’s gain and then added a second lower close.” Whether that is the start of a decline or a three-day setback inside an uptrend is not on the chart yet.

What it does NOT mean

  • Three outside up does not mean a decline is over, and three outside down does not mean an advance is over. Sharp three-period counter-moves are common inside trends that continue.
  • They are not buy or sell signals. They describe an engulfing pattern and one candle after it.
  • Confirmation is not certainty. The third candle is one more period of evidence.
  • A stronger-looking pattern is not a safer one. Much of what makes it look strong is price that has already moved.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating the third candle as proof. It shows the engulfing candle was not reversed at once. It shows nothing further.
  2. Comparing wicks, not bodies. The engulfing step rests on opens and closes.
  3. Ignoring the distance already covered. Two full candles against the prior trend have printed before the pattern has its name.
  4. Reading it alone. Structure, the prior trend, nearby levels and volume carry more weight than three candles.

Risks and limitations

  • Definitions vary between sources on the engulfing step and on the third candle.
  • The pattern is recognised late by design, and usually later than a three inside pattern.
  • Evidence that candlestick patterns predict price is limited and mixed, especially when patterns are used alone.

Key takeaway

Three outside up and three outside down are an engulfing pattern followed by one more close in the same direction. The extra candle adds evidence and takes away distance, and the result still has to be weighed against location and the larger trend.

  • Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques, Steve Nison (2001). Second edition; for the engulfing patterns that form the first two candles
