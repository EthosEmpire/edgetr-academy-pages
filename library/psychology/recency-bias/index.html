Recency Bias

Recency bias is the tendency to give the most recent events too much weight and to expect that what has just happened is what happens next.

Why it matters

Ask someone how their trading is going and the answer usually describes the last week. Ask what a market is like and they describe the last month. Recency bias is this habit of letting the newest information stand for the whole picture.

It matters in trading because recent results are a small sample, and small samples are noisy. Decisions about size, risk and whether a method “works” get made on evidence too thin to support them.

What it is

Recency bias is the tendency to overweight what happened most recently and to project it forward. It rests on findings that are well established in psychology:

  • Recent events are easier to recall. Memory favours the latest items.
  • Ease of recall is mistaken for frequency. Tversky and Kahneman called this the availability heuristic: if examples come to mind easily, the mind concludes that they are common.
  • Small samples are trusted too much. People expect a short run to look like the long-run average, and treat a short run as proof when it does not.

In plain words

After a week of sunshine, people leave their umbrellas at home and are surprised by rain. After a week of rain, they carry umbrellas on a clear day. The climate is the same in both weeks. Only the last few days have changed, and the last few days are doing all the forecasting.

How it shows up at a chart

In reading markets. A trend that has run for months starts to feel permanent. In a long bull market, falls come to be seen as brief and harmless. In a long bear market, every rally is assumed to fail. Both beliefs are drawn from one phase of a market cycle.

In reading volatility. After a quiet stretch, wide moves seem unlikely and stops and sizes get set for calm conditions. Volatility tends to change without notice.

In judging one’s own trading. This is the most direct effect.

Why streaks mislead

Streaks are a normal product of chance. In a series of 100 trades where each has an even chance of winning, a run of five or more wins in a row somewhere in the series is more likely than not. To the person living through it, that run feels like evidence of skill or of a method that has “clicked”.

The mind then errs in one of two opposite directions:

  • The hot hand. “I am on a roll, so the next one is likely to win too.”
  • The gambler’s fallacy. “I have had five wins, so a loss is due.”

Both treat the recent sequence as if it had a memory. If trades are independent, the chance on the next one is whatever it always was. See win rate and expectancy.

Going deeper

Recency and the crowd

Recency bias operates on whole markets as well as on individuals. After a long rise, recent returns are what everyone has experienced, so expectations drift upward together. This feeds herd behaviour and the swings described in fear and greed.

Recency and the dramatic

Vivid events get extra weight on top of recent ones. A single spectacular crash or rally is remembered for years and can dominate a person’s sense of what is “normal” for an asset, long after it has stopped being recent.

Five winsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical journal holds 100 trades. Over the whole record there are 48 winners, a win rate of 48%. The last twenty include 11 winners, and the last five are all winners.

After the fifth win, the trader’s notes read: “The method is really working now.” The next trade is opened at three times the usual size.

  • Over the last 5 trades the win rate is 100%.
  • Over the last 20 it is 55%.
  • Over all 100 it is 48%.

The best available estimate of the win rate is the one from the largest sample. Five straight wins in a record like this is an ordinary event. If each trade has a 48% chance of winning, any particular five in a row all win about 2.5% of the time, and across a hundred trades there are many places for such a run to start.

The enlarged trade may win or lose. Either way, its size was chosen on the evidence of five trades.

What it does NOT mean

  • Recent information is not worthless. Markets do change, and new data matters. The bias is in letting it outweigh everything else.
  • A streak is not proof of skill, and not proof of its absence. Runs of wins and losses appear in purely random sequences.
  • A loss is never “due” for independent events. They do not balance out on a schedule.
  • A long trend is not permanent because it is long. Its length says nothing certain about its future.
  • Using a longer history is not a guarantee of accuracy. Old data can describe conditions that no longer exist.

Common mistakes

  1. Changing size after a few results. Raising it after wins and cutting it after losses ties risk to noise.
  2. Rewriting a plan after a short losing run. A few trades cannot tell a flawed rule from an unlucky week.
  3. Setting stops for last week’s volatility. Conditions can widen quickly.
  4. Judging an asset by its latest move. One strong month is not a character reference.
  5. Trusting memory for the long-run record. Memory serves up the recent and the dramatic.

Habits people use

  • Keeping a trading journal and reviewing statistics over the full record, not the last few entries.
  • Fixing risk per trade in a trading plan so that it does not move with recent results.
  • Looking at a longer timeframe before forming a view of what is normal for a market.
  • Setting a minimum number of trades before any rule is judged.

Risks and limitations

  • The opposite error also exists: clinging to old data when conditions really have changed. No formula says how much weight the recent deserves.
  • Trades are not perfectly independent. Market conditions can make wins or losses cluster for real reasons.
  • A long record of one’s own trading takes time to build, and beginners do not have one yet.

Key takeaway

Recency bias makes the last few events stand in for the whole record. Short runs of wins, losses or price moves are small, noisy samples, and treating them as the new normal ties decisions to chance.

Sources and further reading

  • Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1974). Science. Describes the availability heuristic and misjudgment of small samples
  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (2011)
  • Trading in the Zone, Mark Douglas (2000). On treating each trade as one event in a series
