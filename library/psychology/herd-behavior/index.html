Herd Behavior

Herd behavior is the tendency of people to copy what a large group is doing, buying because others buy and selling because others sell, instead of judging independently.

Why it matters

Markets are crowds, and crowds do not behave like the sum of independent individuals. People watch each other, copy each other and take comfort in numbers. Herd behavior is the result: large groups moving in the same direction for the simple reason that everyone else is.

It helps explain why prices can travel much further, up or down, than any calm assessment would suggest. For an individual, it explains the strange pull to do what the chat room is doing.

What it is

Herding means setting aside one’s own information or judgment and following the observed actions of others. The key feature is that the reason for acting is the crowd itself, not something the person has independently assessed.

In a market, herding creates feedback. Buying pushes price up, a rising price attracts attention, attention brings more buyers. The same loop runs in reverse during a sell-off.

In plain words

In a theater, one person stands to see better. The person behind has to stand too, then the row behind them. Soon the whole audience is standing, nobody sees any better than before, and nobody can sit down without losing their view. Each person acted sensibly given what the others did. The crowd as a whole went nowhere.

Why the mind does it

Other people as information. When someone is unsure, what others do is a clue. If many people are buying, perhaps they know something. Economists call the result an information cascade: after a few people act the same way, later arrivals follow them and ignore their own information, so the crowd’s apparent agreement rests on very little.

Social pressure. Classic experiments on conformity in the 1950s, by the psychologist Solomon Asch, found that many people would go along with a group’s obviously wrong answer to a simple question instead of standing apart. Disagreeing with a group is uncomfortable.

Shared emotion. Excitement and panic spread between people. See fear and greed.

How it shows up at a chart

Signs of herding in markets include prices rising fastest when attention is highest, the same phrases and charts repeated across feeds, and assets moving on popularity with no change in anything measurable. In crypto, meme coins are the clearest case: their price depends almost entirely on how many people are paying attention.

In an individual, herding feels like FOMO on the way up and like panic on the way down, often triggered by FUD. The inner sentence is always some version of “everyone else is doing it”.

Going deeper

Bubbles and crashes

Histories of speculative manias describe the same sequence many times: a rise, a story that explains why this time is different, a rush of new participants, and a reversal once the supply of new buyers runs out. Robert Shiller’s work describes these as feedback loops in which rising prices and rising enthusiasm amplify each other. The pattern is one part of what market cycles describe.

Mechanical herds

Some crowd moves are forced, not chosen. When price falls far enough to liquidate leveraged positions, the forced selling pushes price lower and triggers more liquidations. See liquidation cascades. A short squeeze is the same mechanism in the other direction.

Herds can be manufactured

Because people follow visible crowds, the appearance of a crowd is valuable to anyone selling something. Paid promotion, fake accounts and coordinated buying are used to simulate enthusiasm and draw real buyers in, which is the method of a pump and dump. A crowd seen through a screen may be much smaller than it looks.

Following price and following people

Some trading styles, such as trend following, deliberately trade in the direction price is already moving, using written rules with defined exits. That is different from herding as described here. The distinction is between acting on a rule decided in advance and acting because of social pressure in the moment.

A group chat and a hypothetical tokenHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical token rises from 100 to 190 in four days. A group chat of a few hundred people fills with screenshots of gains. One member, who knows nothing about the token, reads for two evenings and buys at 200, reasoning that “all these people cannot be wrong”.

What the member could not see: most of the chat had bought below 120. A few dozen accounts produced most of the messages. Some of the earliest buyers were already selling into the new demand.

A week later the token trades at 120. The late buyer is down 40%. Many of the early buyers are still in profit.

The example does not show that the crowd was “wrong”. Those who bought early did well. It shows that “many people are buying” described their position, not the position of someone arriving at 200, and that the visible agreement came from people who had already acted and had every reason to hope others would follow.

What it does NOT mean

  • The crowd is not always wrong. Majority views are often correct, and trends can run for a long time.
  • Doing the opposite of the crowd is not a method. Automatic contrarianism is just another way of letting the crowd decide.
  • Herding is not stupidity. Using others’ behavior as a clue is reasonable in everyday life. It misfires when everyone is doing the same thing.
  • Popularity is not evidence of quality or safety. The number of people holding something says nothing about what it is worth.
  • A visible crowd is not necessarily a real one. Online enthusiasm can be purchased or automated.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating many voices as many independent opinions. They are often echoes of a few.
  2. Assuming others have done the research. Each person in a herd may be assuming the same about the rest.
  3. Joining because of the size of the move. A large move shows what has happened to those already in.
  4. Confusing agreement with confirmation. Seeking out groups that share a position adds comfort, not evidence. See confirmation bias.
  5. Forgetting the exit. A crowd that entered gradually can try to leave all at once, when liquidity is thinnest.

Habits people use

  • Writing down their own reason for a trade before reading what others think.
  • Asking who is speaking, what position they already hold and what they gain if others follow.
  • Checking whether anything measurable has changed, apart from price and attention.
  • Keeping entry rules in a trading plan that do not depend on anyone else’s enthusiasm.

Risks and limitations

  • It is rarely possible to tell in real time whether a crowd is reacting to real information or only to itself.
  • “Herd behavior” is easy to diagnose in hindsight, after a reversal has made it obvious.

Key takeaway

Herd behavior is following a crowd because it is a crowd. It creates feedback loops that carry prices far in both directions, and it means that visible agreement often contains much less independent information than it appears to.
