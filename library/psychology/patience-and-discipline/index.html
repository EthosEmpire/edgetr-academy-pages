Patience & Discipline

Patience is waiting until the conditions of a plan are met. Discipline is doing what the plan says once they are, including the parts that are uncomfortable.

  • 5 min read

Why it matters

Every other page in this shelf describes a way that decisions go wrong: jumping in, hitting back, holding on, following the crowd. Patience and discipline are the two qualities that stand against all of them. They are less about the market than about the distance between what a person planned and what they do.

They are also widely misunderstood. They are often presented as traits of character, a kind of toughness that some have and others lack. Most of what is known about self-control points somewhere else: toward arranging things in advance so that less toughness is needed.

What they are

  • Patience is the ability to wait: for conditions to be met before entering, and for a trade to play out before interfering.
  • Discipline is consistency: doing what the plan specifies, at the size it specifies, including taking the planned loss.

Both presuppose a trading plan. Without written rules there is nothing to be patient for and nothing to be disciplined about.

In plain words

A gardener cannot make a seed grow faster by digging it up to check on it. Most of the job is preparing the ground, watering on schedule and leaving things alone. The gardener who keeps interfering is working harder and harming the plant.

Why they are hard

  • The present outweighs the future. People often prefer a smaller reward now to a larger one later. A trade available this minute beats a better one that may appear next week.
  • Doing nothing feels like failing. An open screen with no position feels like wasted time. See overtrading.
  • Rules bind hardest when they hurt. Taking a planned loss, or sitting out a move that others are enjoying, is the moment the rule matters and the moment it is least welcome. See loss aversion and FOMO.
  • Randomness punishes good behaviour. A disciplined trade can lose and an impulsive one can win. In the short run, the feedback is unreliable.
  • Self-control takes effort. The slow, deliberate mode of thinking is easily pushed aside by stress, tiredness and strong emotion.

How they show up at a chart

For a selective plan, most sessions contain no valid trade. Patience looks like nothing happening: a closed platform, an alert set, a note in the journal that conditions were not met. Treating no position as an acceptable result is the core of it.

Discipline shows at the points of decision:

  • entering when the plan’s conditions are met, even after recent losses;
  • using the planned position size, not one adjusted for mood;
  • leaving the stop loss where it was placed;
  • not closing a winning trade early out of nervousness;
  • ending the session when a pre-set limit is reached.

Going deeper

Structure over willpower

A long-standing idea in behavioural economics is precommitment: making a choice in advance that limits one’s own later options, because the later self is expected to be tempted. People who describe themselves as disciplined traders often turn out to be describing arrangements of this kind, not feats of restraint. Orders are placed at entry. Limits are written down. The platform is closed between setups.

Process as the measure

Mark Douglas and other authors argue for judging a trading day by whether the rules were followed, not by whether it made money. The reasoning is that process is within a person’s control and the outcome of a handful of trades is not. A day of well-executed losing trades counts as a good day by this standard, and a day of impulsive winners does not.

The same plan, followed and not followedHypothetical illustration

Two hypothetical traders use the same selective plan for a month of 20 sessions. The plan produces a valid setup in 6 of them.

Trader A takes the 6 trades the plan allows, at the planned size, and does nothing in the other 14 sessions.

Trader B takes the same 6 trades, plus 15 more on quiet days because “something might happen”. In two of the planned trades, B moves the stop further away as price approaches it.

Costs alone separate them. If each completed trade costs 0.2% of a position equal to the account, A pays 6 × 0.2% = 1.2% for the month. B pays 21 × 0.2% = 4.2%.

More importantly, A’s month is a clean test of the plan. Whatever the result, A can learn from it. B’s result is a mixture of the plan and 15 unplanned trades with altered stops, and nothing can be concluded about either. The example does not say which of them ended the month ahead. Over a single month, either is possible.

What it does NOT mean

  • Patience is not passivity. It includes acting promptly when the conditions are met.
  • Discipline does not make a plan profitable. It makes results consistent with the plan, whatever the plan’s quality.
  • Neither means feeling nothing. Emotions still arrive. The aim is to keep them from placing orders.
  • Discipline is not rigidity. Plans are revised, at a calm moment and on the evidence of many trades. What discipline rules out is changing the plan mid-trade because the trade has become uncomfortable.
  • Waiting is not always right. Endless hesitation, where no setup is ever good enough, is its own failure to follow a plan.

Common mistakes

  1. Relying on resolve in the moment. Promises made while calm are weak against a moving price.
  2. Trading at a size that makes calm impossible. The larger the stake relative to what a person can bear, the harder every rule becomes to keep.
  3. Judging discipline by results. A broken rule that led to a win is still a broken rule.
  4. Staring at the screen while waiting. Constant watching turns patience into endurance.
  5. Abandoning the plan after a short losing run. A few trades cannot show whether the rules or luck were responsible. See recency bias.

Habits people use

  • Writing the session’s conditions and limits before the market is opened.
  • Setting alerts at prices of interest and stepping away until one triggers.
  • Placing exits as orders at entry.
  • Marking each trade in a trading journal as “followed the plan” or “did not”, and reviewing that column separately from the money column.
  • Taking a fixed break after any loss. See revenge trading.

Risks and limitations

  • Disciplined execution of a poor plan produces reliably poor results. The two qualities say nothing about whether the rules are any good.
  • Preset orders and limits reduce impulsive decisions. They do not remove gaps, slippage or exchange failures.
  • What counts as “patient” depends on the style. A wait of minutes is long for one approach and nothing for another.

Key takeaway

Patience is waiting for a plan’s conditions, and discipline is carrying the plan out when they arrive. Both depend less on willpower in the moment than on decisions made in advance: written rules, preset exits and limits that do not need to be argued with.

Sources and further reading

  • Trading in the Zone, Mark Douglas (2000). On consistency, rules and accepting the uncertainty of each trade
  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (2011). On effortful self-control and fast, impulsive judgment
  • Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom, Van K. Tharp
