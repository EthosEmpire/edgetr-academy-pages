Overtrading

Overtrading is trading more often, in larger size or in more markets than a person's own plan and capital support, usually driven by emotion and not opportunity.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

More trading feels like more effort, and in most activities more effort brings better results. In markets the link is weaker. Each trade carries a cost and a risk, and trades taken without a good reason add both while adding nothing else.

Overtrading is a common way for small accounts to shrink. It rarely feels like a mistake while it is happening. It feels like being busy.

What it looks like

Overtrading is measured against a person’s own rules, not against a universal number. A scalper may place dozens of trades a day within a plan, while someone who trades daily charts is overtrading with three trades in an afternoon. The question is whether the trades come from the plan or from the urge to act.

Common signs include:

  • trades that cannot be matched to any written condition;
  • a rising number of trades after a loss or after a win;
  • switching to shorter timeframes to find “something to do”;
  • several positions in closely related assets at once;
  • end-of-day totals where costs are a large part of the result.

In plain words

Someone fishing who casts and reels in every ten seconds looks very active. The line is hardly ever in the water long enough for a fish to bite, and every cast is a chance for a tangle. The quiet person who waits with the line out is doing less and is no worse placed to catch something.

Why the mind does it

  • Action feels better than waiting. People tend to prefer doing something to doing nothing, even when nothing is the sounder choice. Sitting out feels like falling behind.
  • Boredom. Markets spend long stretches doing little. A screen that is always on invites activity to fill the gap.
  • Unpredictable rewards are habit-forming. Occasional wins arriving at irregular intervals are a powerful reinforcer of repeated behaviour.
  • The pull of a loss or a win. After a loss, the urge is to win it back, which is revenge trading. After a win, overconfidence says the next one is just as easy.
  • Design. Trading apps are built to be engaging. Alerts, price flashes and one-tap orders lower the effort needed to act.

How it shows up in the numbers

Every completed trade pays trading fees twice, once in and once out, and usually loses a little to the spread and to slippage. Each amount is small. Together and repeated, they become a steady drain that the trades have to overcome before any gain is left.

Size is the other channel. Positions that are too large for the account turn ordinary price movement into painful swings, which in turn provokes more reactive trading.

Going deeper

Costs scale with leverage

Fees are charged on the full position value. With leverage, the position is a multiple of the account, so the same percentage fee is a larger share of the trader’s own capital. Frequent leveraged trading compounds two effects at once.

Doing nothing is a position

Most trading plans are idle most of the time, because their conditions are not met. Holding no position is a decision with its own merits: no cost, no market risk and a clear head. Treating it as a failure is one of the roots of overtrading. See patience and discipline.

When to step away

Trading that feels compulsive, that continues despite a wish to stop, or that causes ongoing distress is more than a bad habit. Persistent distress or loss of control is a reason to step away and seek support from a qualified professional.

A busy month, in numbersHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical trader has an account of 10,000 and opens positions worth 10,000 each time. The fee is 0.1% on the way in and 0.1% on the way out, so each completed trade costs 0.2% of the position: 20.

  • A month with 10 trades: 10 × 20 = 200 in costs, 2% of the account.
  • A month with 60 trades: 60 × 20 = 1,200 in costs, 12% of the account.

In the busy month, the trades as a group must gain more than 12% of the account before costs just to break even. Spread and slippage would add to that.

Now suppose the same 60 trades use 5x leverage, so each position is 50,000. Each completed trade costs 100, and the month costs 6,000, which is 60% of the account.

A review of the journal shows that 14 of the 60 trades matched a written condition in the plan. The other 46 were taken, in the trader’s own notes, because “the market was moving”.

What it does NOT mean

  • A high number of trades is not overtrading by definition. Some styles are built on frequent trading within clear rules. The test is the plan, not the count.
  • Being active is not the same as being productive. In trading, effort and results are only loosely linked.
  • Sitting out is not laziness or fear. It is what following a selective plan looks like most of the time.
  • Overtrading is not cured by a better strategy. It is a behaviour pattern that can attach itself to any method.
  • Low fees do not make frequent trading harmless. Spread, slippage and the risk of each position remain.

Common mistakes

  1. Leaving the platform open all day with nothing to do. Constant exposure produces constant temptation.
  2. Dropping to a faster timeframe to find trades. Lower timeframes offer more activity and more noise.
  3. Ignoring costs because each one is small. Their sum is what matters.
  4. Raising size to make quiet markets “worth it”. This adds risk without adding opportunity.
  5. Counting only money results. A day with many unplanned trades that happened to end positive is still a day of overtrading.

Habits people use

  • Defining in a trading plan what a valid setup is, and sometimes a maximum number of trades per session.
  • Using price alerts so that the screen does not need to be watched.
  • Recording the reason for every trade in a trading journal, then counting how many had one.
  • Adding up fees for the month as a separate line.
  • Scheduling time away from the market.

Risks and limitations

  • There is no universal number of trades that marks the line. It depends on the plan and the style.
  • Rules that cap activity can also cause missed trades that the plan would have allowed.
  • A cost figure such as 0.2% is an illustration. Real fees and spreads differ between venues and change over time.

Key takeaway

Overtrading is trading beyond what a plan and an account support, in frequency, size or number of positions. Its costs are quiet and cumulative, and its usual cause is the discomfort of doing nothing.
