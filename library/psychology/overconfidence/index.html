Overconfidence

Overconfidence is the tendency to overrate one's own skill, knowledge or control over outcomes, which in trading leads to larger positions and thinner safety margins.

Why it matters

Confidence is useful. Without some of it, nobody would act at all. Overconfidence is confidence that has drifted away from the evidence: believing one knows more, predicts better or controls more than is true.

It is one of the most consistently documented biases, and in trading it has a direct channel to damage. Overconfident people take larger positions, trade more often and keep less margin for error, so that when a normal run of losses arrives it lands on an account that was not sized for it.

What it is

Researchers describe several related forms: overestimating one’s own ability, placing oneself above others, and being too certain about one’s beliefs. Daniel Kahneman adds two ideas that apply closely to markets:

  • The illusion of validity. A judgment feels right because the story behind it is coherent, not because the method has been shown to work.
  • The illusion of skill. In fields where outcomes depend heavily on chance, people still experience their results as the product of ability.

Three supporting habits keep overconfidence alive:

  • Self-attribution. Wins are credited to skill and losses to bad luck or outside interference.
  • Hindsight. After the fact, outcomes feel as if they had been predictable, so past foresight is remembered as better than it was.
  • The illusion of control. Being active, choosing the moment and clicking the button create a feeling of influence over a result that the person does not actually control.

In plain words

Someone who has driven for a year without an accident starts to steer with one hand and follow the car in front more closely. The clean record feels like proof of skill. It may only show that nothing unexpected has happened yet, and the closer following distance means that when something does, there is less room to deal with it.

How it shows up at a chart

  • Size grows after wins. A good run leads to larger positions or more leverage, in the belief that the method has been proven.
  • Rules feel optional. Stops are widened or dropped because “I can see where this is going”.
  • More trades. Confidence in one’s reading of the market makes every wiggle look like an opportunity. See overtrading. Research on individual brokerage accounts has found that the most active traders tended to earn lower returns after costs, a pattern its authors linked to overconfidence.
  • Narrow forecasts. Views are stated as certainties, with no thought given to the alternative.
  • Bull-market skill. In a broadly rising market, most long positions gain. It is easy to mistake the market’s direction for personal ability. See bull market.

Going deeper

Small samples feel like proof

A record of ten trades with eight winners feels conclusive. Statistically it is weak evidence. Runs like this turn up by chance in methods with no advantage at all. See recency bias and win rate and expectancy.

Confidence and accuracy are different things

How sure a person feels is a fact about the person. Studies of expert forecasting have repeatedly found that confidence is a poor guide to accuracy. A calm, certain voice, including one’s own, is not evidence.

Ten good trades and three bad onesHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical account starts at 10,000 in a rising market. The plan risks 1% per trade with gains and losses of equal size.

  • First ten trades: eight winners and two losers. The balance is about 10,613, a gain of 6.1%.

Convinced the method is proven, the trader raises the risk to 5% per trade.

  • Next three trades: all losers, which is an unremarkable streak. The balance falls to about 9,099.

The account is now about 9% below where it started. Thirteen trades with eight winners have produced a loss, because the losses came at five times the size of the wins.

Had the risk stayed at 1%, the same three losses would have left about 10,298, still above the starting balance. The market and the sequence of outcomes were identical. The difference was a decision about size made on the evidence of ten trades.

What it does NOT mean

  • Overconfidence is not the same as confidence. Acting on a tested plan requires confidence. The bias is certainty beyond the evidence.
  • Past wins do not prove skill. They may reflect a favourable market, a small sample or chance.
  • Feeling certain is not information about the market. Price is unaffected by anyone’s conviction.
  • Experience does not automatically cure it. Experts in many fields show strong overconfidence, sometimes more than beginners.
  • The opposite is not the answer. Paralysis and constant self-doubt lead to their own errors.

Common mistakes

  1. Raising risk after a winning streak. The largest positions end up being opened at the moment of greatest, and least tested, confidence.
  2. Taking credit for a rising market. When nearly everything goes up, gains say little about selection or timing.
  3. Explaining away every loss. If no loss ever counts against the method, the method is never being tested.
  4. Dropping safeguards that “have not been needed”. A stop that was never reached has not been shown to be unnecessary.
  5. Looking only for agreement. A confident person searches less for contrary evidence and finds less of it. See confirmation bias.

Habits people use

  • Keeping risk per trade fixed by rule, so that it does not rise with mood.
  • Writing predictions down with a stated level of confidence and checking them later in a trading journal.
  • Asking before each trade: “what would have to be true for this to fail?”
  • Reviewing results over the largest available sample and comparing them with what the market as a whole did in that period.
  • Treating a strong feeling of certainty as a cue to slow down.

Risks and limitations

  • Overconfidence is hard to see from the inside, because it feels identical to being right.
  • Research findings describe groups on average. Not every active or confident trader fits the pattern.
  • Measuring one’s own skill takes a long record, and market conditions change while it is being built.

Key takeaway

Overconfidence is certainty that outruns the evidence. After a run of wins it pushes size and frequency up and safeguards down, so that an ordinary losing streak does outsized damage.

Sources and further reading
