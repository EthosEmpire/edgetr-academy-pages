Anchoring

Anchoring is the tendency to rely too heavily on one reference number, such as a purchase price or a past high, when judging what a price is worth now.

Why it matters

“It was 200 last year, so 80 is a bargain.” Sentences like this are heard in every market, and they feel like analysis. They are usually anchoring: judging a price by its distance from one remembered number instead of by anything about the present.

Anchors are unavoidable. The mind needs a starting point for any estimate. The problem is that the starting point is often arbitrary, and people adjust away from it far less than they should.

What it is

Anchoring was described by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in 1974. In their experiments, people were exposed to a number, sometimes an obviously random one, before estimating something unrelated. Their estimates were pulled toward the number they had just seen.

Two things make the effect notable. It works even when the anchor is known to be meaningless. And it works on people who are told about it.

In plain words

A shop marks a jacket “was 200, now 80”. The 200 may never have been a real selling price, yet 80 now feels like a saving. Without the crossed-out number you would have asked a different question: is this jacket worth 80 to me?

Why the mind does it

Estimating a value from nothing is hard. Starting from an available number and adjusting is easy, so that is what the mind does. Adjustment takes effort and tends to stop too early, as soon as the answer feels plausible. An anchor also directs attention: once 200 is in mind, reasons why the asset could be worth 200 come to mind more easily than reasons why it could not.

How it shows up at a chart

Charts are full of candidate anchors.

  • The purchase price. The most powerful anchor for anyone holding a position. Gains and losses are measured from it, and getting back to it can become a goal in itself. See loss aversion.
  • Past highs and lows. A previous peak makes current prices look cheap. A previous low makes them look expensive or “safe”.
  • Round numbers. Prices such as 100 or 1,000 attract attention simply because they are round. See round numbers and psychological levels.
  • The first price seen. Whatever an asset cost when a person first looked at it often becomes their sense of its “normal” price.
  • Someone else’s number. A target mentioned in a headline or a post lodges in the mind whether or not it had any basis.
  • Price per unit. A token priced at a fraction of a cent feels cheap, and one priced in the thousands feels expensive, regardless of how many units exist.

Price per unit says nothing by itself. Value comparisons need market capitalization, which multiplies price by the number of units, and an understanding of token supply.

Going deeper

Shared anchors can move markets

Anchors are not purely an illusion in markets, because many participants share the same ones. If thousands of people have orders near a prior high or a round number, price sometimes reacts there. This is part of the reasoning behind support and resistance. The distinction worth keeping is between “many people are watching this level” and “price belongs at this level”.

Percentages and the anchor to the high

A fall is usually quoted from the high, which hides how much room remains below. An asset that has fallen from 200 to 80 is down 60%. That feels as if most of the fall must be over. Arithmetically, it can halve again.

Cheap compared with what?Hypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset traded at 200 a year ago and is now at 80.

  • From 200 to 80 is a fall of 60%.
  • A buyer at 80, anchored on 200, thinks: “It only has to get back to where it was.” Returning to 200 from 80 would take a gain of 120 / 80 = 150%.
  • If the price later falls to 40, the buyer at 80 has lost 50%, even though the asset was already “60% off”. Measured from the old high, it is then down 80%.

A second holder bought at 150 and is waiting to sell at break-even. From 80, that requires a gain of 70 / 80 = 87.5%. The figure 150 is important to this holder and to nobody else in the market.

Neither number, 200 or 150, describes anything about the asset today. Whether 80 turns out to be high or low is unknown. The example shows only that both judgments were measured from a memory.

What it does NOT mean

  • A price far below its high is not thereby cheap. A large fall does not limit a further fall.
  • A price far above its past is not thereby expensive. Past prices do not cap future ones either.
  • A low price per unit does not mean a low valuation. Unit price depends on how many units exist.
  • Anchoring does not mean past prices are useless. Prior highs and lows are widely watched reference points. The error is treating them as where price ought to be.
  • Knowing about anchoring does not prevent it. The effect has been shown in people who were warned in advance.

Common mistakes

  1. Making the entry price the centre of every decision. The market has no record of where any individual bought.
  2. Calling a fallen asset a bargain because of its old high. The comparison says how far it has fallen, not what it is worth.
  3. Comparing tokens by unit price. A price of 0.50 and a price of 500 cannot be compared without supply.
  4. Adopting targets heard elsewhere. A number repeated often enough starts to feel like a fact.
  5. Reading only a linear chart after a large move. The choice of price scale changes how big past moves look, and with it which levels stand out.

Habits people use

  • Asking of a held position whether they would open it today at the current price, which sets the entry price aside.
  • Stating moves in percentages from the current price in both directions, not only from the high.
  • Writing the reasons for a view without mentioning any past price, then seeing what is left.
  • Looking at market capitalization before unit price.
  • Noting where a target number came from before giving it any weight.

Risks and limitations

  • There is no anchor-free judgment. Replacing one reference point with another only changes the anchor.
  • Because anchors are shared, ignoring them completely would mean ignoring levels that other participants act on.
  • Laboratory effects are measured in simple tasks. How strongly they operate in a given real decision is hard to know.

Key takeaway

Anchoring is judging a price by its distance from one reference number, such as an old high, a purchase price or a round figure. Those numbers shape how a price feels. They carry no obligation for the market to return to them.

Sources and further reading

  • Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman (1974). Science. The paper that described anchoring and adjustment
  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (2011). Includes a chapter on anchors
  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999). On round numbers and prior highs and lows as reference levels
