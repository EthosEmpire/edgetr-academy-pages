Sunk Cost Fallacy

The sunk cost fallacy is continuing with a decision because of the money, time or effort already spent on it, even though those costs cannot be recovered.

Why it matters

“I have already lost so much on this, I cannot sell now.” The sentence sounds like determination. It is usually the sunk cost fallacy: allowing what has already been spent to decide what is done next.

In trading it keeps people in positions they would never open today, and it often persuades them to add more. A small problem becomes a large one because of the wish not to “waste” what has gone in.

What it is

A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered whatever is decided now. Economic reasoning says that such costs should have no bearing on a decision, because they are the same in every possible future. Only what can still change, the costs and benefits from here on, is relevant.

People do not behave that way. Experiments by Hal Arkes and Catherine Blumer, published in 1985, showed that having invested money, time or effort in something made people more likely to continue with it, even when continuing was the worse choice.

In plain words

You are halfway through a film at the cinema and it is dreadful. The ticket money is gone whether you stay or leave. The only question left is how to spend the next hour. Staying “to get your money’s worth” does not bring the money back. It adds a wasted hour to a wasted ticket.

Why the mind does it

  • Closing makes the loss final. While a position is open, the loss can still be imagined away. This is loss aversion at work.
  • Waste feels wrong. People are brought up not to waste things, and abandoning something paid for feels like waste.
  • Consistency. Having made a decision, and especially having told others, people feel pressure to stand by it.
  • Effort creates attachment. The more hours spent researching an asset, the harder it is to accept that the conclusion was mistaken.

How it shows up at a chart

The classic form is averaging down for the wrong reason: buying more of a falling asset because the average entry price drops and break-even looks closer. The arithmetic of the average is correct. What it leaves out is that the position is now larger, so each further fall costs more, and that the purchase was prompted by the earlier cost and not by a fresh assessment.

Other forms include:

  • holding a position long after the original reason for it has gone, because “it has cost too much to give up on”;
  • refusing to close because of fees, spreads or funding already paid;
  • staying with a project or token because of time spent in its community;
  • keeping a failing approach because of money spent on a course or tool.

The purchase price in these cases also acts as an anchor: the position is judged against what was paid, not against the present.

Going deeper

The test people apply

A common way to separate the two ledgers is to imagine holding cash instead of the position and to ask whether one would buy it now, at this price and in this size. If the honest answer is no, then the only thing keeping the position open is its history.

Adding is not always the fallacy

Buying more of something that has fallen is not automatically an error of this kind. Some plans specify building a position in stages at prices set in advance, with a total size and an exit fixed beforehand. The fallacy lies in the reason. A planned addition follows a rule written when neutral. A sunk-cost addition is made to repair a loss.

Concentration follows

Each addition increases the share of the account tied to one outcome. Repeated averaging down is a common route to concentration risk, and with leverage it can end in liquidation. For an asset that can fall to zero, averaging down can compound a total loss.

Averaging down, in numbersHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical trader buys 10 units of an asset at 100, spending 1,000. The price falls to 60. The position is worth 600, a loss of 400.

Thinking “I am too deep to sell, and if I buy more I only need 80 to break even”, the trader buys another 10 units at 60, spending 600.

  • Total: 20 units for 1,600. Average cost: 1,600 / 20 = 80.
  • Break-even has moved from 100 to 80. From a price of 60, that is a rise of 33.3% instead of 66.7%.

The price then falls to 45.

  • With the addition: 20 × 45 = 900. Loss: 1,600 - 900 = 700.
  • Without the addition: 10 × 45 = 450. Loss: 1,000 - 450 = 550.

The lower average made break-even look closer and made the loss larger. If the price had risen instead, the addition would have helped. Neither outcome was knowable. What can be said is that the second purchase was decided by the 400 already lost, which nothing could change.

What it does NOT mean

  • It does not mean losing positions must always be closed. Holding can be consistent with a plan. The fallacy is holding because of what has been spent.
  • It does not mean the past is irrelevant. Past events can carry useful information. It is past cost that has no bearing on the choice.
  • Persistence is not always a fallacy. Continuing is reasonable when the outlook from here justifies it, judged without reference to what has gone in.
  • A lower average cost is not a smaller risk. It comes with a larger position.
  • Accepting a loss is not an admission of incompetence. It is a decision about the future made with present information.

Common mistakes

  1. Asking “how do I get my money back?” The question assumes the money is still there to be retrieved from that particular trade.
  2. Adding to a loser without a pre-set plan. Size grows as the loss grows, with no rule limiting either.
  3. Letting effort stand in for evidence. Weeks of research do not make a conclusion correct.
  4. Counting fees already paid as a reason to stay. They are gone on every path.
  5. Defending a position because it was announced publicly. The market is indifferent to anyone’s reputation.

Habits people use

  • Defining the exit and the maximum position size before entry, in a trading plan.
  • Asking of an open position whether they would enter it today.
  • Writing the original reason for a trade in a trading journal, then checking whether it still holds.
  • Deciding in advance whether additions are allowed at all, and at what prices.

Risks and limitations

  • The “would I buy it today?” test is simple to state and hard to answer honestly while holding the position.
  • Closing a position has real costs, including fees and possibly tax. Tax rules differ by jurisdiction.
  • Hindsight makes the fallacy look obvious. At the time, persistence and stubbornness feel the same.

Key takeaway

A sunk cost is gone whatever is decided next, so it has no logical place in the decision. The fallacy is holding or adding to a position because of what has already been lost, which increases exposure for a reason that has nothing to do with the outlook from here.

Sources and further reading

  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (2011)
  • Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979). Econometrica
