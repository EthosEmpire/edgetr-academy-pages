Loss Aversion

Loss aversion is the tendency for a loss to hurt more than a gain of the same size pleases, which leads people to treat losing and winning positions differently.

Why it matters

Ask most people whether they would accept a coin flip that wins 100 or loses 100, and they decline. The possible loss looms larger than the equal possible gain. This is loss aversion, one of the best-documented findings in the psychology of decision making.

In trading it has a specific and costly result. People tend to close winning positions quickly and hold losing ones, which is the reverse of what their own plans usually say.

What it is

Loss aversion comes from prospect theory, published by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979. The theory made three observations about how people actually judge risky outcomes:

  • Outcomes are judged relative to a reference point. People think in gains and losses from some starting position, not in total wealth.
  • Losses weigh more than gains. A loss is felt more strongly than a gain of the same size.
  • Sensitivity diminishes. The difference between losing 0 and 100 feels larger than the difference between losing 900 and 1,000.

A consequence is that attitudes to risk flip. With gains, people tend to be cautious and prefer a certain outcome to a gamble. With losses, they tend to gamble in the hope of avoiding the loss altogether.

In plain words

Finding a banknote on the pavement is a nice moment. Discovering that the same note has fallen out of your pocket spoils the afternoon. The amount is identical. The feeling is not, and most people would go to more trouble to avoid the second than to get the first.

How it shows up at a chart

For a trader, the reference point is usually the entry price. Everything is then seen as “up” or “down” from there.

  • In profit, the cautious side appears. The gain could vanish, so the urge is to close and secure it, often well before the planned take profit.
  • At a loss, the gambling side appears. Closing would turn a paper loss into a final one, so the urge is to wait, to move the stop loss, or to add to the position. See sunk cost fallacy.

Researchers Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman named this pair of habits the disposition effect in 1985: a disposition to sell winners too early and ride losers too long.

Going deeper

What it does to the arithmetic

A plan might define a risk/reward ratio of 1:2. If winners are closed early and losers are allowed to run past the stop, the realised ratio can be the reverse. The win rate needed to break even then rises sharply, without the trader having changed anything on paper.

The break-even magnet

Because the entry price is the reference point, getting “back to even” takes on an importance the market does not share. A position is held not because of anything on the chart but because closing below the entry would make the loss official. This overlaps with anchoring.

Unrealised is still real

The distinction between a paper loss and a booked loss feels large and is mostly an accounting one. The value has already fallen. See realized and unrealized P&L.

Two positions and one planHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical trader buys two assets at 100 each. The plan for both is a stop at 95 and a target at 110, a ratio of 1:2 with a break-even win rate of 33.3%.

  • Asset A rises to 104. Worried about losing the gain, the trader closes it: +4.
  • Asset B falls to 95. The trader cancels the stop, intending to sell “when it gets back to 100”. It falls to 92, where the trader finally closes it: -8.

The realised numbers are a loss of 8 and a gain of 4. If that pattern repeated, the ratio would be 2:1 the wrong way round, and the break-even win rate would be 8 / (8 + 4) = 66.7%, double what the plan assumed.

Nothing here depended on a forecast. The plan’s exits were replaced by the wish to avoid the feeling of a loss.

What it does NOT mean

  • Loss aversion is not the same as caution. It can make people take more risk, by holding and adding to losing positions.
  • It is not a sign of poor character or low intelligence. It has been observed across many groups, including experienced professionals.
  • It does not mean every held loser is an error. Holding can be consistent with a plan. The bias lies in holding because closing would hurt.
  • Taking a loss is not the same as being wrong about everything. A closed loss is one outcome in a series.
  • Knowing the theory does not remove the feeling. It only makes the pattern easier to recognise.

Common mistakes

  1. Moving a stop further away as price approaches it. The loss was acceptable when planned and becomes unacceptable when near.
  2. Closing winners at the first pullback. This caps gains while leaving losses uncapped.
  3. Waiting for break-even. The entry price is meaningful to the holder and to nobody else.
  4. Treating unrealised losses as not counting. They count in every way except the feeling.
  5. Judging trades by how they felt. A well-made trade that lost can feel worse than a careless one that won.

Habits people use

  • Placing the stop as an actual order when the trade is opened, so that closing does not require a fresh decision.
  • Remembering that a stop order limits the planned loss only if it is filled near its price. Gaps, slippage and outages can make the real loss larger.
  • Deciding the size from the planned loss, so that the loss is an amount already accepted. See position sizing.
  • Recording planned and actual exits side by side in a trading journal.
  • Asking of an open position: “would I enter this now, at this price?”, which shifts the reference point from the entry to the present.

Risks and limitations

  • Laboratory findings describe averages. Individuals differ in how strongly they show the effect.
  • Later research has refined and debated parts of prospect theory. The core observation, that losses tend to weigh more than gains, remains widely accepted.
  • Recognising the bias in a past trade is far easier than noticing it in an open one.

Key takeaway

Loss aversion, described by Kahneman and Tversky in 1979, means losses are felt more strongly than equal gains. At a chart it shows up as closing winners early and holding losers, which quietly reverses the risk/reward a plan was built on.

Sources and further reading

  • Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979). Econometrica. The original paper describing how people value gains and losses relative to a reference point
  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (2011). An accessible account of prospect theory and loss aversion
  • The Disposition to Sell Winners Too Early and Ride Losers Too Long: Theory and Evidence, Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman (1985). Journal of Finance. Named the disposition effect
