Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias is the tendency to look for, notice and remember information that supports what one already believes, and to discount what contradicts it.

Why it matters

Once a person has a view, the mind stops acting like a judge weighing evidence and starts acting like a lawyer building a case. Confirmation bias is the name for this shift. It is among the most thoroughly studied biases in psychology, and it affects experts as well as beginners.

At a chart it is especially powerful, because a chart contains enough information to support almost any story. Someone looking for reasons to stay in a trade can always find some.

What it is

Confirmation bias works at three stages:

  • Search. People look for information in places likely to agree with them.
  • Interpretation. Ambiguous evidence is read as support. Contrary evidence is examined far more critically than friendly evidence.
  • Memory. Hits are remembered and misses fade.

Experiments by the psychologist Peter Wason in the 1960s showed how deep the habit runs: asked to discover a rule, people mostly tried examples that would confirm their first guess and rarely tried ones that could disprove it.

In plain words

After deciding to buy a particular model of car, you suddenly see it on every street. The number of those cars has not changed. Your attention has. In the same way, once you believe a price is heading somewhere, the evidence for it seems to be everywhere, and the evidence against goes unnoticed.

Why the mind does it

  • It saves effort. Checking a belief against every contrary fact is slow. Accepting what fits is quick.
  • Being wrong is unpleasant. Evidence against a view is, in a small way, evidence against oneself.
  • Commitment raises the stakes. A view backed with money is defended harder than one held idly. After a position is opened, objectivity drops.
  • Feeds are built to agree. Social platforms show more of what a person already engages with, so the supply of agreeing voices is effectively unlimited.

How it shows up at a chart

Common forms include:

  • Indicator shopping. Adding or adjusting indicators until one supports the trade. Since many are built from the same price data, a determined search usually succeeds. See combining indicators.
  • Timeframe shopping. If the daily chart looks bad, the hourly chart is consulted, then the weekly, until one looks right.
  • Selective pattern reading. Seeing the bullish pattern and missing the bearish one on the same chart.
  • Dismissing failure. A broken level is described as a “shakeout” or as FUD, not as information. See pattern failure and invalidation.
  • Curated company. Following only accounts that share the same position, a small-scale version of herd behaviour.

Going deeper

Invalidation as an antidote

The standard counter in trading literature is to state, before entering, what would show the idea to be wrong. A stop loss placed at that price turns the statement into something that cannot later be reinterpreted. Evidence is then judged against a line drawn when the person was still neutral.

It grows with confidence

Each round of selective evidence makes the view feel better tested, which makes contrary evidence seem even less credible. This is one of the routes to overconfidence, and it is why strongly held views tend to be the least examined.

One trade, two notebooksHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical trader buys an asset at 100, believing it is about to rise. Support is seen at 95.

Notebook as kept. Over the next week the trader saves six posts predicting higher prices, notes that one momentum indicator is rising, and records that “volume looks healthy”. Price closes at 94, below the support. The entry for that day reads: “Shakeout. Weak hands are being cleared. Still bullish.” A different indicator, added that evening, is not yet negative.

Notebook as it could have been. The same week also contained: a lower high on the daily chart, two analysts with the opposite view, falling volume on the rallies, and the close below 95, which was the level the trader had originally named as important.

Both notebooks describe the same market. The first was written by someone looking for support. Nothing in the example says where price went next. The point is that the first notebook could not have changed its owner’s mind, whatever happened.

What it does NOT mean

  • Confirmation bias does not mean the belief is wrong. A view defended with biased evidence can still turn out to be correct. The process is flawed, not necessarily the conclusion.
  • It is not dishonesty. People are usually unaware they are doing it.
  • Seeking a second opinion is not the cure if the second opinion is chosen for agreeing. The bias extends to the choice of advisers.
  • More research does not automatically reduce it. More searching through a biased filter produces more one-sided material.
  • Agreement between several indicators is not independent confirmation. Tools built from the same prices tend to agree with each other.

Common mistakes

  1. Researching after entering. By then the search is for reassurance.
  2. Changing the reason for a trade when the first reason fails. A position opened on one idea and held on another has no test left.
  3. Unfollowing people who disagree. The feed becomes an echo.
  4. Counting only the calls that worked. Memory keeps a flattering record.
  5. Treating every contrary signal as noise. If nothing could count as evidence against a view, it is no longer a view about the market.

Habits people use

  • Writing the invalidation level and the reason for a trade before entry, in a trading plan.
  • Deliberately reading the best argument for the other side.
  • Keeping a trading journal that includes failed ideas, so that memory cannot edit them out.
  • Asking what evidence would be expected if the view were wrong, then looking for it.

Risks and limitations

  • Awareness helps only a little. Studies suggest people spot this bias in others much more easily than in themselves.
  • Looking for contrary evidence can be overdone, leading to an inability to decide anything.
  • Charts are ambiguous by nature. Two careful, unbiased readers can still disagree.

Key takeaway

Confirmation bias is the habit of gathering and reading evidence so that it supports an existing view. At a chart, where some support for any story can be found, the usual counter is to define in advance what would prove the idea wrong.
