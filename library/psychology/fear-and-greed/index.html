Fear & Greed

Fear and greed are the two emotions most often used to describe market behaviour: greed pushes people toward risk as prices rise, fear pushes them away as prices fall.

Why it matters

“Markets are driven by fear and greed” is one of the oldest sayings in finance. It is a simplification, but a useful one. Prices are set by people making decisions under uncertainty, and those two emotions colour a great many of the decisions.

For a learner, the point is not to diagnose the market’s mood. It is to recognise the same two forces in their own behaviour, because that is where they do their damage.

What it looks like

Over a full market cycle, crowd emotion tends to follow price, not lead it. Optimism grows as prices rise and is usually greatest near highs. Pessimism grows as prices fall and is usually greatest near lows. The labels on the curve are a teaching model. Real markets do not announce which stage they are in.

  • Greed, in this context, is the wish for more gain combined with a shrinking sense of risk.
  • Fear is the wish to avoid loss or regret, combined with a growing sense of danger.

In plain words

Think of driving on a motorway. After an hour with no trouble, speed creeps up and the gap to the car in front shrinks. After a near miss, the same driver crawls along, braking at shadows. The road has not changed. The driver’s sense of danger has, and it is tracking recent experience more than actual risk.

How it shows up at a chart

Greed and fear do not usually feel like greed and fear from the inside. Greed feels like confidence and good analysis. Fear feels like prudence. That disguise is what makes them hard to catch.

In a rising market such as a bull market, greed shows up as growing size, growing leverage and thinner safety margins. Its sharpest form is FOMO. In a falling market such as a bear market, fear shows up as rushed selling, or as a refusal to act at all. It is often fed by FUD.

Why the mind does it

Kahneman and Tversky’s work on decisions under risk showed that people do not treat gains and losses evenly. Losses hurt more than equal gains please, and attitudes to risk change depending on whether a person feels ahead or behind. See loss aversion.

Recent experience also dominates. After a run of rising prices, risk feels low. After a run of falling prices, it feels high. Neither feeling is a measurement. See recency bias.

Finally, emotion is contagious. Seeing others excited or frightened shifts a person’s own state, which is part of herd behaviour.

Going deeper

Fear has several forms

Trading literature commonly separates fear into several kinds. They matter because they pull in different directions: one fear makes a person close a good trade too early, another makes them hold a bad one too long.

Sentiment indexes

Some data providers publish “fear and greed” indexes that blend inputs such as volatility, momentum, trading volume and survey or social media data into a single score. They are attempts to describe the crowd’s mood at a moment in time. Methods differ between providers, the inputs lag, and an extreme reading can persist for a long time. They are descriptions of sentiment, not timing tools.

The two emotions swap places

A person can pass from greed to fear within one trade. A position bought eagerly near a high becomes a source of dread when it falls. The reverse also happens: a relieved seller watches price recover and buys back higher. The emotions change faster than any analysis could justify.

One hypothetical asset, two moodsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset rises from 100 to 180 over several months.

Near 180. A holder who began with a modest position has added to it three times. The most recent addition used leverage. Their notes say: “It keeps going up. Risk seems low.” The plan they wrote at 100 mentioned a maximum position size. It has been exceeded.

Later, near 110. The asset has fallen from 180 to 110, a drop of about 39%. The same person sells everything in one evening after reading alarming posts. Their notes say: “It could go to zero. I cannot watch this.”

Nothing in this example says whether buying near 180 or selling near 110 turned out well. The observation is narrower: both decisions were made at emotional extremes, both departed from the written plan, and in both the feeling of risk moved opposite to the price.

What it does NOT mean

  • Fear and greed do not explain every price move. News, liquidity, forced selling and ordinary trading all move markets too.
  • Extreme sentiment is not a timing tool. Markets can stay euphoric or fearful far longer than seems reasonable.
  • Fear is not weakness and greed is not ambition. Both are ordinary emotional responses that every participant has.
  • Doing the opposite of the crowd is not a method. The crowd is sometimes right for long stretches.
  • Feeling calm does not mean the decision is sound. Greed in particular often feels like calm confidence.

Common mistakes

  1. Assuming it only affects others. The crowd is made of individuals who each believe they are the exception.
  2. Letting size grow with confidence. Rising prices raise both confidence and exposure, so the largest positions tend to exist near the highest prices.
  3. Making large decisions at emotional peaks. The moments of strongest feeling are the moments of weakest judgment.
  4. Reading a sentiment score as an instruction. A number describing mood does not say what follows.
  5. Mistaking relief for analysis. Selling to stop the discomfort is a different act from selling because the plan says so.

Habits people use

  • Fixing position size and exits in a trading plan before the emotion arrives.
  • Recording state of mind next to each trade in a journal, then checking whether size and mood rise together.
  • Delaying large decisions made during a surge of feeling.
  • Reviewing what they wrote at the previous extreme.

Risks and limitations

  • The “emotional cycle” is a model drawn with hindsight. In real time, the stages cannot be identified reliably.
  • Explaining a move by “fear” or “greed” after the fact is easy and usually untestable.
  • Individual temperaments differ widely. General descriptions will not fit everyone.

Key takeaway

Greed shrinks the sense of risk as prices rise, and fear inflates it as prices fall. Both are normal, both disguise themselves as good judgment, and both do the most harm when they, not a plan, decide the size and timing of a trade.

Sources and further reading

  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (2011)
  • Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979). Econometrica. Shows that attitudes to risk differ between gains and losses
  • Trading in the Zone, Mark Douglas (2000). On fear as the main source of trading errors
