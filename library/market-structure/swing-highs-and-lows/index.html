Swing Highs & Swing Lows

A swing high is a peak with lower highs on both sides, and a swing low is a trough with higher lows on both sides. They are the turning points used to read market structure.

Why it matters

Trends, ranges, support, resistance and most chart patterns are all built from the same raw material: the points where price turned. Those turning points are called swing highs and swing lows.

If you can mark them consistently, you can describe almost any chart. If you mark them carelessly, every later conclusion inherits the error. That makes this small topic one of the most practical on the shelf.

What it looks like

  • A swing high is a peak: a candle whose high is above the highs of the candles on both sides of it.
  • A swing low is a trough: a candle whose low is below the lows of the candles on both sides of it.

In plain words, a swing high is a hilltop and a swing low is a valley floor. Other names for the same thing include peaks and troughs, pivot highs and pivot lows, and fractals.

In plain words

Think of a hiking trail seen from the side. You only know you stood on a summit once the path has gone downhill for a while behind you. While you are standing on it, it could just be a ledge on the way to something higher.

How a swing point is defined

The definition above leaves one thing open: how many candles on each side need to be lower (or higher). There is no official answer. It is a convention that each trader or charting tool chooses.

A widely used setting is two candles on each side, which makes a five-candle pattern. Other people require three, five or more. Charting tools that mark these points automatically usually let you set the number.

Neither column is better. A small number shows fine detail and reacts quickly. A large number filters out noise and reacts slowly. What matters is using one rule consistently on one chart.

How to read it

Once swing points are marked, the reading is a comparison:

Latest swing high is... Latest swing low is... Commonly described as
Higher than the previous one Higher than the previous one Uptrend
Lower than the previous one Lower than the previous one Downtrend
About level About level Range
Higher Lower Expanding swings, no clear direction
Lower Higher Contracting swings, no clear direction

Traders usually label the points with four abbreviations: HH (higher high), HL (higher low), LH (lower high) and LL (lower low).

Swing points also become reference prices. An old swing low is a place where buyers stepped in before, so it is often watched later as possible support. An old swing high is often watched as possible resistance.

Going deeper

Wicks or closes. Most definitions use the candle’s high and low, which includes the wicks. Some traders prefer closing prices, because a close shows where the market accepted price and not only where it briefly traded. The two methods can mark different points.

Major and minor swings. A chart contains swings of many sizes nested inside each other. Small swings within a single leg of a larger move are often called minor or internal. The large ones that define the overall trend are called major. Confusing the two is a frequent source of disagreement about what “the trend” is.

Equal highs and lows. Two peaks at almost the same price do not fit neatly into “higher” or “lower”. Most readers treat them as level, which is one of the building blocks of a range and of patterns such as the double top.

Confirming a swing high, candle by candleHypothetical illustration

Seven hypothetical candles have these highs, in order: 104, 106, 109, 112, 110, 108, 107.

Using the rule “two lower highs on each side”:

  • The fourth candle has the highest high, 112.
  • To its left are 106 and 109. Both are lower.
  • To its right are 110 and 108. Both are lower.

So the fourth candle is a swing high, but this only became known when the sixth candle closed. When the fourth candle itself closed, it was simply the newest high in a rising move.

With a stricter rule of three candles on each side, the left side (104, 106, 109) and the right side (110, 108, 107) both still qualify, and confirmation arrives one candle later, at the close of the seventh.

What it does NOT mean

  • A swing high is not a top. It marks where one advance paused. Price can exceed it a few candles later.
  • A swing low is not a floor. It shows where buyers appeared once, not where they must appear again.
  • A confirmed swing point is not a forecast. Confirmation tells you a turn happened, with a delay. It says nothing about how far the move goes.
  • There is no “true” set of swing points. A different candle count or a different timeframe gives a different, equally defensible set.

Common mistakes

  1. Marking a peak before it is confirmed. Until the candles on the right side have closed, the high may still be exceeded.
  2. Changing the rule mid-chart. Using two candles each side in one place and five in another produces a structure that follows no rule at all.
  3. Counting every wiggle as a swing. Very small swings mostly reflect noise, especially on short timeframes.
  4. Trusting automatic markers blindly. A tool applies its setting mechanically and has no sense of which swings matter.

Risks and limitations

  • Swing points are always identified in hindsight, even if the delay is only a couple of candles.
  • Highs and lows of wicks can differ between exchanges, so the exact swing price may vary by data source.
  • Rules based on a fixed candle count ignore the size of the move, so a tiny blip and a large reversal can both qualify.

Key takeaway

Swing highs and swing lows are the turning points of price, defined by lower highs or higher lows on both sides. How many candles count is a convention, and every swing point is only known after the fact.
