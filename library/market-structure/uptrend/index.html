Uptrend

An uptrend is a sequence of higher swing highs and higher swing lows. Price climbs in steps, and each pullback ends above the low of the one before it.

Why it matters

“The trend is up” is one of the most common phrases in markets and one of the least precisely used. People say it about a single green candle, a good week or a feeling.

Market structure gives the phrase a definition that can be checked on a chart. Once you know that definition, you can also say exactly what would have to happen for the description to stop being true.

What it looks like

An uptrend is a series of:

  • higher highs (HH): each swing high is above the previous swing high;
  • higher lows (HL): each swing low is above the previous swing low.

Both conditions matter. Higher highs show that buyers keep pushing into new territory. Higher lows show that when price falls back, buyers return sooner than they did last time.

In plain words

Picture someone climbing a staircase who steps back down one stair after every two stairs up. They are often moving downwards, yet they keep ending up higher. The steps back down are part of the climb, not the opposite of it.

The two kinds of move inside an uptrend

An uptrend alternates between two phases:

Phase Direction Typical character What it creates
Advance (often called an impulse leg) With the trend Larger candles, more progress A new higher high
Pullback Against the trend Smaller, slower, less progress A new higher low

The pullbacks are what make the structure readable. Without them there would be no swing lows to compare.

How to read it

Three things are commonly checked on an uptrend:

  1. Is each high above the last? If price stalls below the previous high, the first condition has failed, at least for now.
  2. Is each low above the last? The most recent higher low is the price that currently holds the structure together.
  3. How do the legs compare? Advances that shrink while pullbacks deepen are often read as a trend losing strength, though trends can also recover from this.

A trend line drawn under the higher lows is a common visual aid. It summarises the slope of the trend. It is a drawing on the chart, not a barrier in the market.

Going deeper

Why uptrends can persist. A common interpretation is that at each successive level, demand has been strong enough to absorb what sellers offered. That is a description of what the chart shows, not a law. Trends continue until they do not.

Trends come in sizes. Dow Theory, the classical source of these ideas, describes three sizes of trend operating at once: a primary trend lasting many months or longer, secondary reactions within it, and minor day-to-day moves. A market can be in a primary uptrend and a short-term downtrend simultaneously. See multi-timeframe structure.

Uptrend and bull market are related, not identical. A bull market is a long, broad period of generally rising prices. An uptrend is a structural description that can apply to any timeframe, including one afternoon.

How uptrends end. The usual sequence is a failure to make a new high, then a close below the most recent higher low, which is commonly called a break of structure. Even then, the result may be a sideways range and not a reversal.

Checking an uptrend against its own definitionHypothetical illustration

Using the hypothetical staircase above, the swing points are 100, 113, 106, 124, 116, 136 and 128.

  • Advances: 100 to 113 is 13 points, 106 to 124 is 18 points, 116 to 136 is 20 points.
  • Pullbacks: 113 to 106 is 7 points, 124 to 116 is 8 points, 136 to 128 is 8 points.
  • Highs: 113, 124, 136. Lows: 106, 116, 128. Both series rise.

The structure fits the definition of an uptrend, and the most recent higher low is 128.

Now suppose price rallies only to 133, short of the 136 high, and then closes at 126. The high was lower and the close is below 128. The description “uptrend” no longer fits cleanly. Whether a downtrend, a range or a renewed advance follows cannot be read from this alone.

What it does NOT mean

  • An uptrend does not mean price keeps rising. It means price has risen in a particular pattern so far.
  • An uptrend is not a buy signal. Structure describes direction. It says nothing about whether any price is a sensible one to act on.
  • A pullback is not always a pullback. A dip is only known to have been a pullback once the trend resumes. Some dips are the start of a reversal.

Common mistakes

  1. Calling a single rally an uptrend. One advance has no higher low yet. The definition needs at least two highs and two lows to compare.
  2. Watching highs and forgetting lows. A market can print a marginal new high while its lows are already breaking down.
  3. Ignoring the timeframe. “Uptrend” on a five-minute chart and on a weekly chart are very different claims.
  4. Assuming the trend line is the trend. Price can cross a hand-drawn line while the higher lows are still intact.

Risks and limitations

  • An uptrend is recognised only after several swings, so part of the move has always passed by the time it can be named.
  • The end of an uptrend is also recognised late, after price has already fallen from the high.
  • Readers using different swing rules or timeframes may disagree about whether an uptrend exists.

Key takeaway

An uptrend is higher highs and higher lows, nothing more and nothing less. The most recent higher low is the price that keeps the description valid, and no uptrend comes with a promise to continue.
