Downtrend

A downtrend is a sequence of lower swing highs and lower swing lows. Price falls in steps, and each bounce ends below the high of the one before it.

Why it matters

Falling markets are where newcomers most often misread a chart. A sharp bounce inside a decline can look like a recovery. A price far below last month’s can look “cheap”. Neither observation says anything about the structure.

Defining a downtrend precisely gives you a way to tell a bounce inside a decline from a change in direction, or at least to know which evidence would separate the two.

What it looks like

A downtrend is a series of:

  • lower highs (LH): each swing high is below the previous swing high;
  • lower lows (LL): each swing low is below the previous swing low.

It is the mirror image of an uptrend. Lower lows show that sellers keep pushing price into new territory. Lower highs show that when price recovers, sellers return earlier than they did last time.

In plain words

A ball bouncing down a staircase goes up after every landing. Each bounce is real, and each one peaks lower than the one before. Watching a single bounce, you might think the ball is rising. Watching the whole staircase, the direction is obvious.

How to read it

A downtrend alternates between declines, which create new lower lows, and bounces, which create new lower highs. A bounce inside a downtrend is often called a relief rally. It is the counterpart of a pullback in an uptrend.

Three things are commonly checked:

  1. Is each low below the last? If price holds above the previous low, the decline has at least paused.
  2. Is each high below the last? The most recent lower high is the price that currently holds the structure together.
  3. How do the legs compare? Declines that shrink while bounces grow are often read as selling pressure fading. That reading can be wrong for a long time.

A trend line drawn across the lower highs is a common way to visualise the slope. When a downtrend fits between two parallel lines it is called a descending channel.

Going deeper

Bounces can be violent. Some of the sharpest single-day rallies occur inside downtrends. One commonly cited reason is that traders holding short positions buy to close them, which adds buying pressure for a while. A large bounce is still a lower high if it ends below the previous swing high.

Downtrend and bear market. A bear market is a long, broad period of generally falling prices. A downtrend is a structural description that applies on any timeframe. A market in a long-term uptrend spends a lot of time in short-term downtrends.

Old lows as reference points. When a swing low breaks, the event is called a breakdown. The broken area is often watched afterwards as possible resistance, an idea covered in role reversal.

How downtrends end. The usual sequence is the mirror of an uptrend ending: a low that holds above the previous low, then a close above the most recent lower high. Many traders call that close a break of structure. What follows may be a new uptrend or simply a sideways range.

A large bounce that changes nothingHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market prints these swing points: a high at 148, a low at 135, a high at 142, a low at 124, a high at 132, and a low at 112.

  • Highs: 148, 142, 132. Each is lower.
  • Lows: 135, 124, 112. Each is lower.

This fits the definition of a downtrend, and the most recent lower high is 132.

Price then rallies from 112 to 123 in three candles. That is a rise of 11 points, or about 9.8 percent (11 divided by 112), and it looks dramatic on the chart. Structurally it is still below 132, so the sequence of lower highs is intact. If the rally stops at 123 and price turns down, 123 becomes the next lower high. Only a close above 132 would interrupt the pattern under the common definition.

What it does NOT mean

  • A downtrend does not mean price keeps falling. It means price has fallen in a particular pattern so far.
  • A downtrend is not a sell signal. It is a description of direction, not an instruction.
  • A big bounce is not a reversal. Until the most recent lower high is exceeded, a rally fits inside the existing structure.
  • A large fall does not make a price “cheap”. Structure compares swing points with each other. It has no opinion on value, and no percentage decline makes a turn due.

Common mistakes

  1. Calling the bottom on every bounce. Downtrends are full of rallies that fail below the previous high.
  2. Anchoring on the old high. Comparing today’s price with a peak from months ago says how far price fell, not whether the fall is over. See anchoring.
  3. Watching lows and forgetting highs. A market can stop making lower lows and still be capped by lower highs.
  4. Reading the wrong timeframe. A downtrend on an hourly chart may be a routine pullback on the daily chart.

Risks and limitations

  • A downtrend is identified only after several swings, and its end is also recognised late.
  • Falling markets are often more volatile and less liquid, which can exaggerate wicks and make swing prices differ between exchanges.
  • Readers using different swing rules or timeframes may disagree about whether the downtrend is still intact.

Key takeaway

A downtrend is lower highs and lower lows. Bounces are a normal part of it, and the most recent lower high is the price that keeps the description valid. The structure describes what has happened, not how much further price may go.
