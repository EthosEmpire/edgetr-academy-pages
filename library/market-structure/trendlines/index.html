Trend Lines

A trend line is a straight line drawn through successive swing lows in an uptrend or swing highs in a downtrend. It summarises the slope of a trend and marks a moving area that traders watch.

Why it matters

A trend is a staircase of swing points. A trend line turns that staircase into a single straight line, which makes the slope of the trend easy to see and easy to compare from one chart to the next.

It is one of the oldest and simplest tools in chart analysis, and also one of the most abused. Because any two points can be joined by a line, it is easy to draw one that confirms whatever the person drawing it already believes.

What it looks like

  • An up trend line is drawn under price, through successive higher swing lows in an uptrend.
  • A down trend line is drawn above price, through successive lower swing highs in a downtrend.

In plain words

A trend line is like a ruler laid along the edge of a staircase. It does not hold the staircase up. It shows you how steep the stairs are, and it makes it obvious when a new step no longer lines up with the old ones.

How a trend line is drawn and validated

The conventional description in classical technical analysis has three stages:

Stage What is needed Status of the line
Draw Two swing points: two rising lows, or two falling highs Tentative. Any two points make a line.
Validate A third touch where price turns at the line Commonly treated as valid
Watch Further touches over time Often regarded as more significant the more times and the longer it has held

Two practical conventions vary between traders:

  • Wicks or bodies. Some draw through the extreme lows and highs of the wicks. Others use candle closes. Either is acceptable if applied consistently.
  • Exact touch or near miss. Price seldom turns exactly on the line. Many people treat a trend line as a narrow band, in the same spirit as zones, not lines.

How to read it

Slope. The angle of the line shows the pace of the trend. Steeper lines describe faster moves and tend to break sooner, because a rapid pace is hard to sustain. A break of a very steep line often means only that the trend has slowed to a more moderate slope, at which point a new, flatter line can be drawn.

A moving level. A horizontal level stays at one price. A trend line is at a different price on every candle, which is why it is described as dynamic support and resistance.

Breaks. A close through the line is commonly read as a sign that the trend may be changing pace or direction. A brief intraday spike through the line that closes back on the original side is usually given less weight. Neither is conclusive. A trend line can break while the sequence of higher lows that defines the uptrend is still intact, so it is worth separating the two ideas. See break of structure.

Role after a break. A broken up trend line is sometimes watched as resistance if price returns to it from below, and the reverse for a down trend line. This is the diagonal version of role reversal.

Going deeper

Channels. If a second line can be drawn parallel to the trend line on the other side of price, the result is a channel.

Scale matters. On a chart with a logarithmic price scale, a straight line represents a constant percentage rate of change. On a linear scale it represents a constant change in price. The same two points give lines that pass through different prices later on. See price scales.

Checking whether three lows line upHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical uptrend has swing lows at 104 on day 12, at 113 on day 30 and at 122 on day 48.

  • From the first low to the second, price rose 9 points in 18 days: 0.5 points per day.
  • From the second low to the third, price rose 9 points in 18 days: again 0.5 points per day.

The slope between each pair is the same, so all three lows sit on one straight line. The first two defined the line and the third is the touch that commonly counts as validation.

Extending the line forward at 0.5 points per day puts it at 131 on day 66 (18 more days, 9 more points). That is where the line is on that day. It is not a forecast that price turns there. If a candle closed at 127 on day 66, price would be 4 points below the line, and the line would be broken.

What it does NOT mean

  • A trend line is not a physical barrier. It is a drawing. Price turns near it only if enough buying or selling appears there.
  • A touch of the line is not a buy or sell signal. It is a place where the trend’s description is being tested.
  • A break of the line does not mean the trend has reversed. Price may continue at a slower pace, move sideways or resume.
  • A line through two points proves nothing. Two points always fit a line exactly.

Common mistakes

  1. Forcing the line. Cutting through several candle bodies to make the line fit is a sign that the line is not really there.
  2. Treating two points as confirmation. A line needs a third touch before the usual convention regards it as tested.
  3. Trusting very steep lines. They describe a pace that rarely lasts and are broken by ordinary pullbacks.
  4. Covering the chart in lines. With enough lines, price is always “reacting” to one of them, which explains nothing.

Risks and limitations

  • Drawing a trend line is subjective. Two people using the same chart often choose different anchor points.
  • A line’s exact price on a given day depends on the anchor points, the scale and whether wicks or closes were used.
  • Brief moves through a line that then reverse are common, so a single candle beyond it is weak evidence.

Key takeaway

A trend line needs two swing points to draw and is commonly treated as valid after a third touch. It is a useful summary of a trend’s slope, a moving area to watch, and a drawing that the market is under no obligation to respect.
