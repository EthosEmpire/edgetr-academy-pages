Pullbacks & Corrections

A pullback is a temporary move against the prevailing trend, and a correction is a larger or longer one. Both are a normal part of trends and are only confirmed as temporary in hindsight.

Why it matters

No trend moves in a straight line. Even the strongest advance spends a large part of its time falling, and even the steepest decline is interrupted by rallies. These counter-moves are called pullbacks and corrections.

Understanding them matters for a simple reason: from the inside, a harmless pullback and the first leg of a reversal look the same. Knowing what separates the two, and how late that separation becomes visible, prevents a lot of false confidence.

What it looks like

  • A pullback is a relatively brief, shallow move against the prevailing trend.
  • A correction is a larger or longer move against the trend.

There is no agreed boundary between the two. They sit on one scale and differ in size and duration. Related words include retracement and dip. In a downtrend the same thing happens upside down, where the counter-move is called a bounce or relief rally.

In plain words

A long-distance runner slows down to catch their breath and then carries on. That is a pullback. A runner who slows down, stops and turns back for home has reversed. For the first few seconds, the two look identical to someone watching from the roadside.

How to read it

In structural terms, a pullback in an uptrend is the move that creates the next higher low. The test is whether it ends above the previous swing low.

A common way to describe its size is the retracement: the pullback as a percentage of the advance before it.

Retracement % = (swing high - pullback low) / (swing high - prior swing low) x 100For an uptrend. Mirror the terms for a downtrend.

Three features are commonly compared:

Feature Often seen in a pullback Often seen when a trend is turning
Depth Gives back part of the prior leg Gives back all of it and breaks the prior swing low
Character Smaller candles, slower pace than the advance Candles as large as, or larger than, the advance
Volume Lighter than during the advance Heavier than during the advance

These are tendencies described in the classical literature, not tests that give a verdict. Plenty of reversals begin quietly and plenty of sharp, heavy dips are followed by new highs.

Going deeper

How deep is typical. Classical technical analysis, following Dow Theory, describes secondary reactions as commonly retracing between one third and two thirds of the prior move, with around one half often mentioned. Many traders also watch the Fibonacci-derived levels of 38.2 and 61.8 percent. None of these figures is a rule. Markets retrace by every amount in between.

“Correction” in market commentary. In stock market reporting, a fall of 10 percent or more from a recent high is conventionally called a correction, and 20 percent or more a bear market. These thresholds are conventions from equity indexes. In more volatile markets such as crypto, moves of that size occur far more often, and the labels carry less meaning.

Where pullbacks tend to end. Traders often watch areas that mattered before: a former resistance area that price broke through, a rising trend line, or a rising moving average. A return to a broken level is called a retest. These are places where a turn would fit the existing structure, not places where a turn is assured.

When a pullback stops being one. If price closes below the most recent higher low, the structure that defined the uptrend has been interrupted. That is commonly called a break of structure.

Measuring two pullbacksHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market rises from 100 to 120, dips to 111, rises to 131 and dips to 122.

First pullback:

  • Advance: 120 minus 100 = 20 points.
  • Dip: 120 minus 111 = 9 points.
  • Retracement: 9 divided by 20 = 45 percent.

Second pullback:

  • Advance: 131 minus 111 = 20 points.
  • Dip: 131 minus 122 = 9 points.
  • Retracement: 9 divided by 20 = 45 percent.

Both dips ended above the previous low (111 is above 100, and 122 is above 111), so the sequence of higher lows is intact. With hindsight both were pullbacks. At the moment price reached 122, however, nothing on the chart could confirm that. Had it continued to 109, the same dip would have broken the 111 low.

What it does NOT mean

  • A pullback is not a discount. A lower price inside an uptrend is a lower price. Whether it is the end of the dip is unknown at the time.
  • A retracement level is not a turning point. 50 percent, 38.2 percent and the rest are reference marks that price often passes straight through.
  • A shallow pullback does not prove a strong trend. It describes one counter-move. The next one may be deep.
  • A dip is not a pullback until afterwards. The label is only earned when the trend resumes.

Common mistakes

  1. Naming it too early. Calling a fall a pullback while it is still falling assumes the outcome.
  2. Measuring from the wrong swing. Retracement only makes sense against the specific advance that came immediately before.
  3. Treating retracement percentages as targets. They describe depth after the fact. They do not pull price toward themselves.
  4. Forgetting the larger timeframe. A correction on a daily chart is a complete downtrend on an hourly chart. “Just a pullback” depends on which trend it is measured against.

Risks and limitations

  • A pullback and the start of a reversal cannot be told apart reliably while they are happening.
  • Depth, pace and volume are tendencies with many exceptions, and volume data differs between exchanges.
  • In fast markets a correction can travel a long way in very few candles, leaving no orderly structure to read.

Key takeaway

Pullbacks and corrections are the counter-moves that give a trend its shape. In an uptrend they form the higher lows. They can be measured precisely after the fact and cannot be identified with certainty while they are still unfolding.
