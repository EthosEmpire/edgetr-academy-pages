What Is Market Structure?

Market structure is the pattern of swing highs and swing lows that price leaves behind. It describes whether a market is trending up, trending down or moving sideways.

Why it matters

Before a chart reader looks at a single candle, a pattern or an indicator, there is one question to settle: what is this market doing right now? It may be rising, falling or going nowhere. Market structure is the tool for answering that question.

Everything else on a chart is read against this backdrop. The same candle or the same indicator reading is interpreted differently in a rising market, a falling market and a sideways one. That is why this shelf starts here.

What it looks like

Price does not travel in a straight line. It advances, pauses, gives some of the move back and then moves again. Each turn leaves a mark on the chart:

  • a swing high is a peak, a point where price stopped rising and turned down;
  • a swing low is a trough, a point where price stopped falling and turned up.

Market structure is the description of how those points sit relative to one another. It asks whether the latest peak is higher or lower than the one before it, and the same for the latest trough.

In plain words

Stand on a beach and watch the waves. One wave tells you nothing about the tide. To know whether the tide is coming in, you watch whether each wave reaches a little further up the sand than the last. Swing highs and lows are the waves. Structure is the tide.

The three states

Comparing swing points produces one of three descriptions.

State Swing highs Swing lows In plain words
Uptrend Each higher than the last Each higher than the last A staircase going up
Downtrend Each lower than the last Each lower than the last A staircase going down
Range Roughly level Roughly level Sideways between two areas

These are definitions, not opinions. A market that is making higher highs and higher lows is in an uptrend by definition, whatever anyone expects to happen next.

How to read it

Reading structure is a mechanical habit more than a talent. A common sequence is:

  1. Mark the obvious turning points. Start with the largest, clearest swing highs and swing lows and ignore the small wiggles.
  2. Compare like with like. Compare each high with the previous high, and each low with the previous low.
  3. Name the state. Higher highs and higher lows, lower highs and lower lows, or neither.
  4. Note the most recent swing points. These are the prices at which the current description would stop being true.

Step four is what makes structure useful. An uptrend is not a feeling. It has a specific recent low, and if price closes below it the staircase is interrupted. That event is commonly called a break of structure.

Going deeper

Where the idea comes from. Defining a trend by its successive peaks and troughs goes back to the writings of Charles Dow around the start of the twentieth century, later organised by others into what is known as Dow Theory. Modern “market structure” vocabulary is newer, but the underlying idea is the same.

Structure depends on the timeframe. A daily chart and a fifteen-minute chart of the same market can show opposite structures at the same moment. Neither is wrong. They are describing moves of different sizes, a subject covered in multi-timeframe structure.

Structure creates levels. Old swing highs and lows are prices where the market turned before. Many traders watch them again later, which is one way support and resistance areas come to exist.

Structure changes. Trends pause, turn into ranges and reverse. The page on trend transitions looks at how that usually unfolds.

Not every chart is clean. Real markets often sit between categories: a trend with one messy swing, or a range that drifts. “Unclear” is a legitimate reading.

Naming the structure from five pricesHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical market prints these turning points in order: a low at 100, a high at 112, a low at 106, a high at 121 and a low at 114.

  • Highs: 112, then 121. The second is higher.
  • Lows: 100, then 106, then 114. Each is higher.

By definition this is an uptrend. The most recent swing low is 114. If price later closed at 110, the run of higher lows would be interrupted. That would not prove a downtrend had begun. It would mean the description “uptrend” no longer fits without doubt, and the market might be turning, or simply moving sideways for a while.

What it does NOT mean

  • Structure does not predict the next move. It describes what price has already done. An uptrend can end on the very next swing.
  • An uptrend is not a reason to buy, and a downtrend is not a reason to sell. The label describes direction so far, nothing more.
  • There is no single correct structure. Different timeframes and different rules for marking swings give different, equally valid answers.

Common mistakes

  1. Marking every tiny wiggle. Counting each small bump as a swing point turns any chart into noise. Start with the turns that are obvious at a glance.
  2. Mixing timeframes without noticing. Comparing a daily swing high with a five-minute swing low produces a description that fits neither chart.
  3. Forcing a label. Some periods are neither trend nor clean range. Calling them one anyway hides the uncertainty.
  4. Treating the trend as permanent. Every trend in history has ended. Structure is a current reading that has to be updated.

Risks and limitations

  • Identifying swing points involves judgement, so two careful readers can describe the same chart differently.
  • Structure is confirmed late. A swing high is only known to be a swing high after price has already fallen away from it.
  • Sudden news can break a tidy structure in a single candle.

Key takeaway

Market structure is the map of swing highs and swing lows that shows whether a market is rising, falling or moving sideways. It is the first layer of context for everything else on a chart, and it describes the past and present, not the future.
