Momentum Trading

Momentum trading is an approach built on the idea that a strong, fast price move may continue for a while. Its characteristic risk is a sharp reversal once the move is exhausted.

Why it matters

Momentum is the approach behind much of the excitement in markets: an asset is moving fast, attention gathers, and the move seems to feed on itself. It is also the setting in which people most often buy late, near the end of a move.

The term has a precise meaning that is worth separating from the excitement. Momentum is the speed of price change, and the approach concerns what tends to happen while that speed lasts and when it fades.

How it works

Momentum trading is built on the idea that an asset moving strongly in one direction may continue in that direction for a while. Practitioners of this approach typically look for unusual strength or weakness, measured by how far price has moved over a recent period, and give less weight to whether price looks “high” or “low”.

Holding periods tend to be short relative to the timeframe used, because momentum is a temporary condition.

In plain words

A ball thrown upward keeps rising after it leaves the hand, but it rises more slowly every moment. Its height is still increasing while its speed is already falling. Momentum is about the speed, and speed changes before direction does.

What momentum traders typically watch

Tool What it describes
Rate of Change The percentage change in price over a set number of periods
RSI The balance of recent gains and losses on a 0 to 100 scale
Volume Whether participation is rising with the move
Candle size Large bodies with small wicks, compared with recent candles
Relative strength Which assets in a group have moved most over the same period
ROC = (close today - close n periods ago) / close n periods ago x 100A plain measure of speed: the percentage change over the last n periods.

Going deeper

Momentum fades before price turns. Because momentum is a rate, it can decline while price continues to make new highs. A slowing advance is still an advance. This is the basis of divergence, and it is a description of slowing, not a forecast of reversal.

Reversals are often abrupt. A fast move attracts participants who entered late and hold with little room for error. When the move stalls, many leave at once. Momentum moves frequently end with a decline that is faster than the rise.

Late entry is the natural error. The strongest-looking moment of a move can be close to its end, when the candles are largest and attention is highest. This is where momentum meets FOMO.

Not every fast move is organic. In thinly traded assets, coordinated buying can manufacture the appearance of momentum in order to sell to latecomers. This is the mechanism of a pump and dump.

The academic term is narrower. In finance research, momentum refers to a measured tendency, in historical data, for recent relative performance among many assets to persist over some horizons. Those studies concern diversified portfolios over long periods and include episodes of sudden, severe loss. They do not describe what a person trading single assets after costs can expect.

Higher price, lower momentumHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset is measured with a 10-day Rate of Change.

  • Day 20: price is 112. Ten days earlier it was 100. ROC = (112 - 100) / 100 = 12%.
  • Day 25: price is 113. Ten days earlier it was 104. ROC = (113 - 104) / 104 = about 8.7%.

Price is higher on day 25 than on day 20, yet momentum has fallen from 12% to about 8.7%. The advance is continuing at a slower pace.

This reading describes the past ten days. From here the move may speed up again, drift sideways or reverse, and the number cannot say which.

What it does NOT mean

  • Strong momentum does not mean the move will continue. The approach assumes persistence for a while. How long “a while” lasts is unknown in every case.
  • A high reading is not a reason by itself. An RSI or ROC value describes what price has done, not what it will do.
  • Momentum does not mean the asset has value. Speed is a property of the price. It says nothing about what is being traded.
  • Fading momentum is not a reversal. Moves can slow and then resume.

Common mistakes

  1. Chasing the largest candle. The most dramatic moment of a move is often close to its exhaustion.
  2. Confusing momentum with trend. A slow, steady trend can have little momentum, and a violent spike can have no trend behind it.
  3. Treating a pump as momentum. Manufactured moves are designed to end suddenly.
  4. Holding after the condition has gone. The reason for the position was speed, and speed is temporary.

Risks and limitations

  • Sharp reversals are the characteristic failure of the approach, and they can exceed any planned loss through gaps and slippage.
  • Momentum measures lag, since they are calculated from past prices.
  • High activity during fast moves often means wider spreads and worse fills.
  • The approach is especially exposed to emotional decisions, because it operates where excitement is highest.

Key takeaway

Momentum trading concerns the speed of a price move and the idea that speed may persist for a while. Speed fades before direction changes, and the approach’s typical cost is a sudden reversal that arrives when the move looks strongest.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999). Momentum and rate of change as measures of the speed of price movement
  • Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers: Implications for Stock Market Efficiency, Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman (1993). Journal of Finance; the standard academic reference on momentum in stock portfolios
  • What Is Momentum Investing? (opens in a new tab), FINRA (2025). Describes trading stocks that are moving rapidly higher or lower, and warns it can be highly risky with false signals in volatile markets
