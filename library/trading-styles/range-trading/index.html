Range Trading

Range trading is an approach built on price moving back and forth between a support area and a resistance area. It relies on the boundaries holding, and every range eventually ends.

Why it matters

Markets spend a large share of their time moving sideways. Range trading is the approach designed for those periods, when approaches built on trends produce one false start after another.

Its logic is easy to see on a chart, and so is its weakness. A range is defined by the past. The approach relies on the boundaries holding one more time, and the one time they do not is the time that matters most.

How it works

A range forms when price repeatedly turns down from roughly the same upper area and up from roughly the same lower area. The upper area is resistance and the lower area is support.

Range trading is built on the idea that, while the range lasts, price near one edge may travel back toward the other. Practitioners of this approach typically pay attention near the boundaries and are inactive in the middle, where there is no nearby reference.

In plain words

A ball bouncing between the floor and the ceiling of a room is predictable in a limited way: near the ceiling, the next direction is probably down. That holds only while the ceiling is there. Nothing about the earlier bounces says when it will give way.

What range traders typically watch

Feature Why it is observed
Number of touches Boundaries tested several times are visible to more participants
Width The distance between the edges, compared with trading costs
Behaviour at the edges Rejection candles, or price pressing against an edge without retreating
Oscillators RSI and the Stochastic Oscillator are commonly consulted in sideways markets
Trend strength A low ADX reading is one description of a market without a trend

Going deeper

Width and costs

Only part of a range is usable. A turn at an edge cannot be recognised until price has already moved away from it, and the opposite edge is rarely reached exactly. Costs are then subtracted from what remains. In a narrow range, little or nothing is left.

Every range ends

A range ends with a breakout or a breakdown. On that final visit to the edge, a range trader is positioned against the move that begins. The earlier visits, which held, give no warning about the one that does not.

The loss in that case is not limited to the width of the range. A break can travel a long way quickly, and a planned exit can be filled at a worse price.

Fakeouts cut both ways

A fakeout, where price pokes beyond an edge and returns, is the event that hurts breakout approaches and suits range approaches. It also makes boundaries untidy. An exit placed just outside the range may be triggered by a brief excursion, after which price returns inside. Placing it further away increases the loss when the break is real.

The fifth visit to supportHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset has moved between 100 and 110 for eight weeks. It has turned up from the 100 to 101 area four times. Price now falls to 100.50 for the fifth time. A trader opens a long at 100.50, with a planned exit at 98.50 if the range fails. The planned loss is 2.

Ending A: the range holds. Price turns up again and trades at 108 a week later.

Ending B: the range ends. Price falls through 100 and reaches 96 within the hour. The exit order triggers at 98.50 and fills at 98.10 in the fast market.

  • Planned loss: 100.50 minus 98.50 = 2.00.
  • Actual loss: 100.50 minus 98.10 = 2.40, before fees.

At 100.50, the fifth visit looked the same as the four before it. The history of the range could not distinguish the two endings.

What it does NOT mean

  • A level that held before does not have to hold again. Some practitioners consider a boundary weaker after many tests, not stronger. Neither view is a rule.
  • The top of a range is not “expensive” and the bottom “cheap”. Those words describe position inside a temporary structure, not value.
  • An oscillator at an extreme does not mean a turn. In a market that starts trending, such readings can persist for a long time.
  • Sideways does not mean low risk. The break that ends a range is often fast and large.

Common mistakes

  1. Trading the middle. Half-way between the edges there is no reference in either direction.
  2. Ignoring width against costs. A narrow range can be visible on a chart and unusable in practice.
  3. Refusing to accept the break. Holding a position against a range that has ended turns a planned small loss into an open-ended one.
  4. Applying range thinking to a trend. Every new high looks like “resistance” to someone still expecting the old range.

Risks and limitations

  • The defining risk is the end of the range, which cannot be timed in advance.
  • Typical gains are bounded by the width of the range, while the loss on a break is not bounded in the same way.
  • Boundaries are zones that two people may draw differently, so anything measured from them is imprecise.
  • Quiet, range-bound markets often have thinner liquidity, which worsens fills when the break comes.

Key takeaway

Range trading is built on price moving between two areas for as long as the range lasts. The approach is bounded by the range’s width on the favourable side and exposed to the break on the other, and no range announces its last bounce.
