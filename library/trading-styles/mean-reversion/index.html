Mean Reversion

Mean reversion is an approach built on the idea that a price stretched far from its average may move back toward it. Its characteristic risk is a trend that does not revert.

  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

After a sharp move, the feeling that price has “gone too far” is almost automatic. Mean reversion is the approach that formalises that feeling: it measures how far price is from an average and builds on the idea that the distance may shrink.

The feeling is unreliable, which is the reason the approach deserves careful study. Prices sometimes return to their averages. They also sometimes keep going, and the approach is positioned against exactly those moves.

How it works

The mean is an average of recent prices, most often a moving average. Reversion is a return toward it. Practitioners of this approach typically look for prices that are unusually far above or below the average and act against the recent direction, on the idea that the stretch is temporary.

This makes it a counter-trend approach in the short term. It is the logical opposite of momentum trading, which builds on a strong move continuing.

In plain words

A dog on a long elastic lead runs ahead of its owner and is drawn back. That works as long as the owner walks slowly. If the owner starts running in the same direction, the dog is never pulled back. The distance closes because the owner caught up.

What mean-reversion traders typically watch

Tool How it describes “stretched”
Distance from a moving average The gap between price and its average, as a percentage
Bollinger Bands Price relative to bands set two standard deviations from a 20-period average, by default
RSI Readings above 70 or below 30, conventionally called overbought and oversold
Stochastic Oscillator Where the close sits within the recent high-low range

Going deeper: the trend that does not revert

“Overbought” and “oversold” are names for readings. They are not conditions that the market is obliged to correct. In a strong trend, price can remain beyond a band, and an oscillator can remain at an extreme, for a long time.

There is also a subtlety in the word “reversion”. An average is calculated from recent prices, so if price stays high, the average climbs toward it. The gap closes without price falling at all. A trader positioned for a decline has then been right about the gap and wrong about the price.

This shapes the approach’s typical profile. Its favourable case is limited: the distance back to the average. Its unfavourable case is a trend, which has no built-in limit. A long series of small favourable outcomes can therefore be outweighed by a few large unfavourable ones.

The approach is most closely associated with range conditions, and range trading can be seen as a version of it that uses horizontal levels instead of an average. Nobody knows in advance when a range will give way to a trend.

Stretched, with two continuationsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset has a 20-period average of 100 and a standard deviation of 2. Price rises quickly to 106.

  • Distance from the average: 106 minus 100 = 6.
  • In standard deviations: 6 / 2 = 3.

By this measure the price is unusually stretched. A trader opens a short at 106.

Continuation A: price returns. Over the next ten periods price falls to 101, while the average edges up to 100.50. The short is ahead by 5.

Continuation B: the average catches up. Price does not fall. It drifts between 106 and 109 for twenty periods and stands at 108. The average, now calculated entirely from these higher prices, has risen to 107.

In B, price is only 1 above its average, so the “stretch” has disappeared. The short is behind by 2, before costs, and the market is in an uptrend. Both continuations began from the same reading.

What it does NOT mean

  • “Overbought” does not mean price is about to fall. It means recent gains have been large relative to recent losses. Strong trends produce this reading continuously.
  • Far from the average does not mean wrong. A new trend always begins as a stretch away from the old average.
  • Reversion to the mean does not mean returning to an earlier price. The mean moves. Price can meet it at a much higher or lower level than before.
  • Frequent small gains do not show that an approach is sound. The losing case is rare, large and easy to leave out of a short sample.

Common mistakes

  1. Acting against a trend because it “must” turn. Markets are under no obligation to return anywhere on a schedule.
  2. Adding to a losing position as the stretch grows. This enlarges the position fastest in the scenario where the idea is failing.
  3. Having no point of invalidation. Without one, the approach has an unlimited losing case.
  4. Anchoring on an old price. Treating a past level as the “right” price is a form of anchoring.

Risks and limitations

  • A sustained trend is the characteristic failure and can produce losses far larger than the typical gain.
  • Short positions against a rising price carry an uncapped loss and the risk of a short squeeze.
  • Measures of “stretched” depend on settings and look-back periods, which are choices and not facts.

Key takeaway

Mean reversion builds on the idea that a price far from its average may come back toward it. The gap can also close because the average follows the price, and that case, a trend that does not revert, is the approach’s defining risk.
