Scalping

Scalping is a style built on very short holding periods, from seconds to a few minutes, and very small price moves. It is highly sensitive to fees, spread and execution.

Why it matters

Scalping is the shortest of the commonly named holding periods. It attracts attention because it looks busy and fast, and because a screen recording of rapid small gains is easy to share.

It is also the style where the mechanics of the market matter most. On a holding period of a minute, the spread, the fee and the quality of the fill are not footnotes. They are a large part of the result. Understanding scalping is mostly a matter of understanding those costs.

How it works

Practitioners of this style typically hold a position for seconds to a few minutes and look for price moves that are a small fraction of one percent. A session may contain dozens or hundreds of trades. No position is normally held for long, and none overnight.

Because each move is tiny, the style depends on doing the same small thing many times. That makes it the opposite of approaches that wait for a few large moves.

In plain words

A currency exchange booth earns a sliver on every banknote that passes through. One transaction is almost nothing. The booth only works if the sliver is larger than its running costs, thousands of times in a row. If rent rises slightly, the whole business can stop making sense.

What scalpers typically watch

Area What is observed
Timeframes Tick charts and 1-minute to 5-minute candles
Order book The size resting at the best bid and ask, and how it changes
Liquidity Whether the market is deep enough to enter and leave without moving price
Spread How wide the gap between bid and ask is at that moment
Fees The maker and taker rates on the venue, which vary by exchange and account tier

Scalping is usually confined to the most liquid markets, because a wide spread or thin book makes the arithmetic impossible.

Going deeper: the cost arithmetic

A fixed cost matters more the smaller the move it is measured against. In the hypothetical figure above, a round trip costing 0.12 is barely visible against a move of 40 and takes 40% of a move of 0.30.

Three further points follow from this.

Execution. A fill that is slightly worse than expected, known as slippage, is a minor issue for a long holding period and can remove the entire gain of a scalp. Delays of a fraction of a second matter.

Competition. At these timescales a person is trading alongside automated systems and professional market makers with faster connections and lower fees.

Leverage. Small moves are sometimes magnified with leverage. This magnifies losses and costs in the same proportion and introduces the possibility of liquidation.

One hypothetical scalp, both waysHypothetical illustration

An asset has a best bid of 100.00 and a best ask of 100.02. The taker fee is 0.05% per side, which is about 0.05 per unit at this price.

If price moves up by 0.30:

  • Buy at the ask: 100.02.
  • The bid rises to 100.30. Sell at the bid: 100.30.
  • Gross result: 100.30 minus 100.02 = 0.28.
  • Fees: about 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.10.
  • Net result: about +0.18.

If price moves down by 0.30 instead:

  • Buy at the ask: 100.02.
  • The bid falls to 99.70. Sell at the bid: 99.70.
  • Gross result: 99.70 minus 100.02 = -0.32.
  • Fees: about 0.10.
  • Net result: about -0.42.

Two moves of equal size produce about +0.18 and -0.42. Spread and fees are subtracted from the gain and added to the loss. Slippage would widen the difference further.

The demands of the style

  • Attention. Continuous, undivided focus for the whole session. There is no time to think a decision over.
  • Speed. Fast, reliable order entry and a stable connection.
  • Volume of decisions. Hundreds of decisions a day, each exposed to fatigue and to overtrading.
  • Cost control. A fee tier or spread that is slightly worse can decide the outcome on its own.

What it does NOT mean

  • Small moves do not mean small risk. Position sizes and leverage are often large in order to make tiny moves matter, and a single fast move can undo many small gains.
  • Many trades do not mean many chances to gain. Each trade pays costs, so more trades means more costs first of all.
  • Short holding does not mean less exposure to surprises. Sudden moves happen within seconds, and fills during them can be far from the expected price.
  • Scalping is not a beginner’s shortcut. It is commonly described as one of the most demanding styles in terms of execution and costs.

Common mistakes

  1. Ignoring the spread. A chart shows one price. A trade is done at the bid or the ask.
  2. Counting gross results. Results before fees can look positive while results after fees are negative.
  3. Scalping illiquid markets. Thin books turn a planned small loss into a large one.
  4. Widening a loss “just this once”. A style built on tiny gains has no room for one large loss.
  5. Assuming a demo result transfers. Simulated fills usually ignore queue position, slippage and partial fills.

Risks and limitations

  • Costs are a structural headwind: the style has to overcome spread and fees on every trade before anything else.
  • Results are extremely sensitive to execution quality, which a retail participant controls only partly.
  • Exchange outages, lag or sudden spread widening can leave a position open longer than intended.
  • The workload and stress are high, and fatigue degrades decisions.
  • Fee schedules, order types and rules differ between exchanges.

Key takeaway

Scalping is defined by very short holding periods and very small moves, repeated many times. Its logic and its weakness are the same thing: when the move is tiny, fees, spread and execution decide a large share of the outcome.
