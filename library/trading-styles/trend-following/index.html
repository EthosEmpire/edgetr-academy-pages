Trend Following

Trend following is an approach that acts in the direction of an established trend and makes no attempt to predict tops or bottoms. It endures many small losses in sideways markets.

  • 4 min read

Why it matters

“The trend is your friend” is one of the oldest sayings in markets, and trend following is the approach built on it. The idea is simple enough to state in a sentence, which makes it easy to underestimate.

What the saying leaves out is the other half of the description. Markets spend a large part of their time not trending, and in those periods a trend follower loses repeatedly. Understanding the approach means understanding both halves.

How it works

Trend following is built on the idea that a trend that is already under way may continue. Practitioners of this approach do not try to forecast when a trend will start or end. They wait until a trend is visible, act in its direction, and step aside when it has visibly weakened or reversed.

Because the approach reacts, it is always late. It misses the start, since nothing can be followed before it exists, and it misses the end, since the end is only recognised afterwards.

In plain words

A surfer does not predict waves. They wait until one has clearly formed, ride it, and get off when it collapses. Many waves that looked promising fade as soon as the surfer starts paddling. That wasted effort is part of surfing, not a sign of doing it wrong.

What trend followers typically watch

Tool What it is used to describe
Market structure Higher highs and higher lows in an uptrend, lower highs and lower lows in a downtrend
Moving averages The direction and slope of average price
Crossovers A faster average moving above or below a slower one
ADX Whether a trend is strong or weak, without regard to direction
Channels Price reaching a new high or low for a set number of periods

All of these are calculated from past prices. They describe what has happened and lag behind it.

Going deeper: the sideways problem

In a range, price rises enough to look like the start of an uptrend, then turns back, then falls enough to look like a downtrend, then turns again. Each false start is called a whipsaw. A trend follower acting on them takes one small loss after another.

This is not a flaw that better settings remove. Faster settings react sooner and produce more whipsaws. Slower settings produce fewer whipsaws and give up more of each trend. The trade-off cannot be tuned away.

The approach is therefore commonly described as producing many small losses and a smaller number of larger gains. Nothing ensures that the gains outweigh the losses. Long stretches without a sustained trend occur, and the losing sequence continues for as long as they last.

Six hypothetical attempts, two outcomesHypothetical illustration

A trend follower makes six attempts over several months. On each, the planned loss if the trend fails is 2.

Outcome A: the market stays sideways.

  • All six attempts fail: 6 x -2 = -12.

Outcome B: one trend develops.

  • Attempts 1 to 4 fail: 4 x -2 = -8.
  • Attempt 5 coincides with a sustained trend: +15.
  • Attempt 6 fails: -2.
  • Total: -8 + 15 - 2 = +5.

In outcome B, the whole result depends on a single attempt, and at the moment it began it looked the same as the other five. In outcome A that attempt never arrives. Trading costs, ignored here, would lower both totals.

What it does NOT mean

  • A visible trend does not mean it will continue. Every trend is intact until the moment it is not. The approach assumes persistence. It cannot confirm it.
  • Trend following is not prediction. It makes no claim about how far or how long a trend will run.
  • A string of losses does not mean the method is being applied wrongly. It can be the normal cost of a sideways market. It can also mean conditions have changed. The two are hard to tell apart.
  • “Trend” is not a single fact. A market can be trending up on a weekly chart and down on an hourly chart at the same time.

Common mistakes

  1. Expecting to catch tops and bottoms. The approach gives up both ends by design.
  2. Abandoning it after a run of small losses. Then switching back after the next trend has already started.
  3. Tuning settings to past data. Settings that fit the last trend perfectly were chosen with hindsight.
  4. Ignoring the timeframe. A trend claim is incomplete without saying on which chart.
  5. Letting one loss grow large. An approach built on small losses stops working when a loss is allowed to become a big one.

Risks and limitations

  • Sideways markets produce repeated losses, and such markets can last for months.
  • Long losing sequences lead to large drawdowns and are psychologically difficult to sit through.
  • Late exits give back part of the open gain on every trend that ends.
  • Sudden reversals and gaps can turn a planned small loss into a larger one.
  • A trend that has run for a long time attracts latecomers, which is when trend transitions often occur.

Key takeaway

Trend following acts in the direction of a trend that is already visible and gives up its start and its end. Its characteristic cost is a long series of small losses whenever the market moves sideways, with no assurance that a sustained trend follows.
