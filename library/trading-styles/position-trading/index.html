Position Trading

Position trading is a style in which positions are held for weeks to months or longer, with attention on the larger trend and little on day-to-day movement.

Why it matters

Position trading is the longest of the commonly named trading styles. It is often presented as the relaxed option: few trades, little screen time, low costs. Those features are real.

What the relaxed picture leaves out is the experience of holding. A position kept for months passes through large moves against it, and each one looks, at the time, exactly like the start of something worse.

How it works

Practitioners of this style typically hold a position for weeks to months, sometimes longer, with the aim of taking part in a large part of a major move. They pay attention to the direction of the larger trend and deliberately ignore most daily movement.

The style overlaps with long-term holding. The usual distinction, covered in trading and investing, is that a position trade still has a planned exit tied to price behaviour, where an investment is tied to a view of long-term value.

In plain words

A ship’s captain crossing an ocean does not change course for every wave. The heading is set by the destination and adjusted for weather systems, not ripples. The cost of that steadiness is sailing through some very rough days instead of around them.

What position traders typically watch

Area What is observed
Timeframes Weekly and daily charts; sometimes the monthly for context
Trend Whether the larger structure is an uptrend, a downtrend or a range
Long averages Slow moving averages as a rough guide to the major direction
Cycles Where the market may be within a broader market cycle
Fundamentals Often some longer-term background, in addition to the chart

Going deeper: what the style demands

Sitting through declines. Large trends contain large pullbacks and corrections. In volatile markets such as crypto, a fall of 20% or more inside a longer advance is not unusual. A position trader has to hold through a decline without knowing whether it is a pause or the end.

Giving back open gains. A gain that has not been realised can shrink or disappear. Watching that happen is one of the defining pressures of the style and is closely tied to how people experience drawdown.

Wide room, small size. Because the normal swings are large, the distance to any planned exit is wide. For a given amount at risk, that implies a small position relative to the account.

Time-based costs. Trading fees are a minor item when there are few trades. A leveraged position, however, pays funding or interest for as long as it is open, and over months this can become significant. Long holding periods and leverage combine poorly, because one deep swing can cause liquidation long before the larger idea is tested.

Very slow feedback. A position trader may make only a few trades a year. It can take years to form any reasonable view of whether a method behaves as expected.

The same hold, seen at week 30Hypothetical illustration

A hypothetical position is opened at 100. At week 24 the price is 150, an open gain of 50 per unit. By week 30 the price is 118.

  • Fall from the peak: 150 minus 118 = 32, which is 32 / 150 = about 21%.
  • Open gain remaining: 118 minus 100 = 18.
  • Share of the peak open gain given back: 32 / 50 = 64%.

At week 30 two continuations are possible, and the chart up to that point is identical for both.

  • Continuation A: the decline ends and price reaches 160 by week 40.
  • Continuation B: the decline continues and price reaches 90, below the starting point.

The holder at week 30 does not know which of these is ahead. The style consists largely of deciding, in advance, what would count as the larger trend having ended, and accepting the swings that fall short of it.

What it does NOT mean

  • Long holding does not mean low risk. The position is exposed to every event during the holding period, including trend transitions that erase months of open gains.
  • Low activity does not mean low effort. The work is mostly in tolerating discomfort and not reacting to noise.
  • A longer chart is not a more reliable chart. Weekly trends end too, and they can end faster than they formed.
  • Holding is not the same as being right. A position that is kept open has not been proved correct. It has only not been closed.

Common mistakes

  1. Watching a months-long position on a 5-minute chart. The short chart produces alarms the holding period was designed to ignore.
  2. Having no definition of “the trend ended”. Without one, every decline is either a panic or a hope.
  3. Using high leverage on a long hold. Normal swings can reach the liquidation price while the larger trend is still intact.
  4. Counting open gains as final. Until a position is closed, the result is not fixed.
  5. Renaming a failed short-term trade. A position that was meant to last a day does not become a position trade by being held.

Risks and limitations

  • A long hold can end with a large loss, and some assets never return to earlier prices.
  • Capital is committed for long periods and is not available for anything else.
  • The small number of trades makes results hard to evaluate and easy to attribute to skill when chance played a large part.
  • Patience is tested for months at a time, and decisions made late in a long decline are often poor ones.

Key takeaway

Position trading means holding for weeks to months to take part in a large move. Its costs are not paid in fees but in large swings against the position, open gains that can vanish, and feedback that arrives very slowly.
