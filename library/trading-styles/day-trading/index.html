Day Trading

Day trading is a style in which positions are opened and closed within the same day, so nothing is held overnight. It demands hours of attention and many trades.

  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Day trading is the style most people picture when they hear the word “trader”: someone at a screen, buying and selling within hours. It is heavily advertised, and the advertising tends to show the activity without the arithmetic.

Understanding how the style is defined, and what it demands, is useful even for someone who never intends to do it. Regulators publish specific warnings about it for a reason.

How it works

A day trade is a position opened and closed within the same trading day. Practitioners of this style typically hold for minutes to a few hours and end each day with no open positions. The intended moves are larger than a scalper’s and smaller than a swing trader’s.

The reason for closing by the end of the day is to avoid overnight risk: news or price moves that arrive while the trader is not watching. In stock markets this is a clear boundary, because the market closes. Crypto markets never close, so the “day” is a convention, and a day trader simply chooses when the session ends.

In plain words

A day trader is like a shop that sells everything on its shelves before locking up. Nothing can be stolen or spoiled overnight. The price of that comfort is having to restock and sell, from zero, every single day.

What day traders typically watch

Area What is observed
Timeframes 1-minute to 1-hour charts, with a higher timeframe for context
Levels The previous day’s high and low, and intraday support and resistance
Volume Whether activity is rising or fading at a level
Time of day Activity is uneven; it clusters around market opens and scheduled news
Volatility Whether the day’s range is unusually wide or narrow

The approach used inside the day varies. A day trader may follow intraday trends, work within a range or watch for breakouts. “Day trading” only describes the holding period.

Going deeper: what the style demands

Time. Several hours of focused attention per session, plus preparation and review. For most people this competes directly with a job.

Decisions. Many decisions a day, each made quickly. This exposes the trader to fatigue, to overtrading and to revenge trading after a loss.

Costs. Fees and spread are paid on every trade, and day trading involves many trades.

In the hypothetical figure, a cost of 0.12% per round trip adds up to 7.2% of the traded amount in a month. That total has to be earned back from price moves before any net gain exists.

Rules. Some brokers and jurisdictions apply special requirements to accounts that day trade frequently with borrowed money. The details differ by country, broker and asset type.

A hypothetical day, before and after costsHypothetical illustration

A trader makes four trades in one day, each with a position of 1,000. The results from price alone are:

  • Trade 1: +8
  • Trade 2: -5
  • Trade 3: +6
  • Trade 4: -4

Total from price: 8 - 5 + 6 - 4 = +5.

Each round trip costs 0.12% of 1,000, which is 1.20. Four trades cost 4 x 1.20 = 4.80.

Net result for the day: 5 - 4.80 = +0.20.

The day looked positive on the chart and was close to zero in the account. With one more trade that broke even on price, the day would have been negative.

What it does NOT mean

  • No overnight positions does not mean low risk. Large moves happen within minutes, and the U.S. SEC’s investor education material says day trading can be highly risky.
  • Being active is not the same as being productive. The number of trades says nothing about the result, apart from the costs it creates.
  • A day trade is not a small trade. Position sizes and leverage are often high to make intraday moves meaningful, which also magnifies losses.
  • It is not a job with a salary. There is no income floor. A day, a month or a year can end with less money than it started with.

Common mistakes

  1. Judging results before costs. Gross and net results can have different signs.
  2. Trading every hour of the session. Markets are often quiet, and trades taken out of boredom still pay full costs.
  3. Carrying a losing day trade overnight. The position then has a holding period, and a risk, that was never planned.
  4. Using borrowed money to “make it worthwhile”. Leverage raises the cost of every mistake.
  5. Mistaking screen time for skill. Hours watched do not turn into an advantage by themselves.

Risks and limitations

  • Investor information published by the SEC warns that day traders typically suffer severe losses early on and that the activity is stressful and expensive.
  • Cumulative costs are a constant drag that grows with the number of trades.
  • Intraday price movement contains a great deal of noise, so patterns on short charts fail often.
  • The style requires time during market hours that many people do not have.
  • Platform outages and fast markets can prevent an exit at the intended time or price.

Key takeaway

Day trading means opening and closing positions within the same day. It removes overnight exposure and replaces it with heavy demands on time, attention and costs, and regulators warn plainly about its risks.
