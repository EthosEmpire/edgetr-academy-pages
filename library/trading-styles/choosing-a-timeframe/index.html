Choosing a Timeframe

A chart timeframe sets how much time each candle covers. The timeframe a trader uses usually follows from the holding period, the time available and the weight of costs.

  • 4 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Open any chart and the first setting is the timeframe: 1 minute, 1 hour, 1 day. The same market looks calm on one and chaotic on another, and a newcomer can easily conclude that one of them is the “real” picture.

None of them is. A timeframe is a way of grouping the same trades. Which grouping a person looks at is usually a consequence of other facts about what they are doing. It is not a setting with a correct answer.

How it works

A timeframe is the amount of time each candle covers. On a daily chart, one candle summarises a full day. On a 1-hour chart, the same day is 24 candles. The trades underneath are identical. Only the grouping changes.

In plain words

A map of a country and a street map of one town are both accurate. One is useless for finding a house and the other for planning a road trip. The question is never which map is correct. It is what the map is being used for.

How timeframe relates to holding period

A candle is the smallest unit a chart can show. A person whose positions last days gains little from watching one-minute candles, and a person whose positions last minutes cannot see anything useful on a weekly chart. The typical pairings, described across this shelf, are:

Holding period Charts typically watched Covered in
Seconds to minutes Tick to 5-minute Scalping
Minutes to hours 1-minute to 1-hour Day trading
Days to weeks 1-hour to daily Swing trading
Weeks to months Daily to weekly Position trading

These are conventions, and sources differ on the exact boundaries.

Going deeper: what changes with the timeframe

Noise. A single large order can shape a one-minute candle. It takes far more activity to shape a daily candle. Shorter timeframes therefore contain a higher proportion of movement that reflects nothing lasting.

Number of patterns. More candles mean more shapes that resemble patterns. Most of the additional ones are noise. Fewer candles mean fewer patterns and a long wait to learn anything from them.

Costs. Moves on short timeframes are small, so a fixed fee and spread take a larger share of each one.

Time available. A 5-minute chart produces a new candle twelve times an hour and is of little use to someone who can look twice a day. A person’s schedule limits which timeframes they can follow at all. Crypto markets never close, so even a daily candle closes at a fixed clock time that may fall in the middle of the night.

Reading more than one timeframe

Practitioners often look at two or three timeframes together: a longer one for context and a shorter one for detail. A commonly cited convention spaces them by a factor of roughly four to six, such as daily with 4-hour, or 1-hour with 15-minute. This is the subject of multi-timeframe structure.

The timeframes will often disagree. That is not an error. A decline on the hourly chart can be a small pullback on the daily chart, and both descriptions are true at once.

One hypothetical day, two chartsHypothetical illustration

An asset opens the day at 100, trades as low as 98 and as high as 104, and closes at 103.

On the daily chart this is one bullish candle: a body from 100 to 103, a lower wick to 98 and an upper wick to 104. The day’s range is 104 minus 98 = 6.

On the 1-hour chart the same day is 24 candles. For five hours in the morning, price fell from 101 to 98, with lower highs and lower lows. Anyone watching only that chart saw a downtrend.

Both charts are correct. The morning decline of 3 was real, and it was also half of the day’s range, absorbed into one lower wick. Whether it mattered depends on whether the observer’s holding period was hours or weeks.

What it does NOT mean

  • A higher timeframe is not “more true”. It is slower and less noisy. It also reacts later and hides everything that happens inside a candle.
  • A lower timeframe does not give more opportunity. It gives more candles, each with a smaller move and a larger share taken by costs.
  • No timeframe makes a pattern reliable. Patterns fail on every chart.
  • There is no best timeframe. A page that names one is describing its author’s circumstances.

Common mistakes

  1. Switching timeframes to find the answer one wants. Any view can be supported by some chart. This is confirmation bias with a zoom control.
  2. Managing a long hold on a short chart. The short chart produces alarms the holding period was meant to ignore.
  3. Moving to a shorter chart after losses. Faster charts feel like more control and mostly add noise and costs.
  4. Assuming indicator settings transfer. A 14-period measure covers 14 minutes on one chart and 14 days on another.

Risks and limitations

  • Timeframe boundaries are arbitrary. A daily candle depends on where the exchange or charting tool sets the daily close.
  • Candles on very short timeframes can differ between exchanges, especially in thin markets.
  • Nothing about a timeframe reduces the risk of loss. It changes the form the risk takes.

Key takeaway

A timeframe is a way of grouping the same trades, and none is the correct one. The timeframe a person watches generally follows from their holding period, available time and costs, and each choice exchanges noise and cost for wider swings and slower feedback.
