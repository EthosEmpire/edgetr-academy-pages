Leverage Risk

Leverage risk is the way borrowed exposure multiplies losses on a trader's own capital and adds the possibility of forced closure, called liquidation.

  • Intermediate
  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026
On this page

  2. How it works
  3. How to read the distance to liquidation
  4. Going deeper
  5. What it does NOT mean

Why it matters

Leverage lets a position be larger than the capital behind it. The market does not change, but the effect of every price move on the trader’s own money does. A move that would be a small dent without leverage can remove the entire collateral with it.

How it works

With leverage, a trader posts margin, a deposit of collateral, and controls a position several times larger. The ratio between the two is the leverage.

change in margin (%) ≈ leverage × price move (%)At 10x, a 1% move against the position costs about 10% of the margin. Simplified: ignores fees and funding.

The multiplication works in both directions. What makes it a risk topic is that losses reach a hard limit: once the margin is nearly used up, the exchange closes the position. This forced closure is called liquidation.

In plain words

Picture carrying a long plank across a room. Held in the middle, a small wobble of the hand moves the ends a little. Held at one end, the same wobble swings the far end across the room. Leverage is holding the plank by the end: your hand moves the same amount, and the result is much larger.

How to read the distance to liquidation

As a rough guide, a position at leverage L runs out of margin after a move of about 100 / L percent against it: about 50% at 2x, 10% at 10x and 2% at 50x. Real liquidation comes sooner, because exchanges require a maintenance margin and charge fees. In the chart above, the 10x position is closed after a fall of 9.5%, not 10%.

Rules differ between exchanges

Liquidation prices, maintenance margin, liquidation fees and the treatment of remaining collateral vary by exchange, product and jurisdiction. The numbers on this page are simplified illustrations, not the terms of any venue.

Going deeper

Nominal and effective leverage

The leverage setting on one position is its nominal leverage. Effective leverage is the total position value divided by the whole account. They can be very different.

A position of 10,000 opened at 10x with 1,000 of isolated margin in an account of 10,000 has an effective leverage of 1x. If it is liquidated, roughly the 1,000 of margin is lost, which is 10% of the account. With cross margin, the rest of the account balance can be drawn on to support the position, so more than the initial margin is exposed.

Costs that come with leverage

  • Funding or interest. Holding leveraged positions usually costs money over time. On perpetual futures this is the funding rate.
  • Liquidation fees. Many venues charge a fee when they close a position.
  • Fees on the full position. Trading fees are charged on the position value, not on the margin, so they are a larger share of the trader’s own capital.

Fast markets

Crypto assets can move several percent within a day and occasionally within minutes. At high leverage, such a move is enough to reach liquidation before a person can react. When many leveraged positions are closed at once, the forced orders can push price further, a chain reaction described in liquidation cascades.

One position, three leverage settingsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical long position worth 2,000 is planned with a stop 5% below the entry, so the planned loss is 100.

  • No leverage. 2,000 of the trader’s own funds are used. Price would have to fall to zero to lose it all. The stop is reached first.
  • 5x, isolated margin of 400. Margin runs out after a fall of roughly 20%. The stop at 5% is reached first, and the planned loss is still about 100.
  • 25x, isolated margin of 80. Margin runs out after a fall of roughly 4%, and in practice a little sooner. The position is liquidated before the stop at 5% is reached. About 80 is lost, plus any liquidation fee, at a price the trader did not choose.

The position and the market are the same in all three cases. Only the distance to forced closure differs.

What it does NOT mean

  • Leverage does not change the odds of a trade. It changes the size of the result relative to the capital posted.
  • High leverage with small margin is not “low risk”. The amount that can be lost may be small, but it is lost after a very small move, and such moves happen constantly.
  • A stop loss does not make leverage safe. Gaps and slippage can carry price past the stop, and liquidation may sit in front of it.
  • Liquidation is not the same as a stop. A stop is chosen by the trader. Liquidation is imposed by the exchange. See stop loss vs liquidation.
  • The maximum leverage an exchange offers is not a suggestion. It is a product limit, not guidance about what is sensible.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating the leverage slider as the measure of risk. Position size and stop distance decide the planned loss. The slider decides how close liquidation is.
  2. Ignoring the liquidation price. A plan with a stop beyond the liquidation price has no working stop.
  3. Using cross margin without realising what backs the position. The whole balance may be at stake.
  4. Forgetting funding and fees. A leveraged position held for a long time can lose value even if price does not move.

Risks and limitations

  • Losses on leveraged positions can be rapid and can consume all posted collateral. On some products and venues, losses can exceed it.
  • Short positions with leverage face rising losses as price rises, with no upper limit on price.
  • Exchange outages, wide spreads and thin liquidity tend to appear at the moments when leveraged positions most need to be managed.
  • The simplified formulas here leave out maintenance margin, fees and funding. Real outcomes are worse than the approximations.

Key takeaway

Leverage multiplies the effect of every price move on the trader’s own capital and brings liquidation closer: roughly 100 / L percent away, and sooner in practice. The risk lies less in the multiplier itself than in how little room it leaves for ordinary price movement.
