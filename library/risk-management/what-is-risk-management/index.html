What Is Risk Management?

Why it matters

Most beginners start with the question “which way is price going?”. Risk management starts with a different one: “how much is lost if this idea is wrong?”. Nobody can answer the first question with certainty. The second can be answered, at least approximately, before a trade is opened.

That is why risk management is often described as the part of trading that is under a person’s control. No method of analysis is right every time, so the size of the losses decides how long an account can survive a run of wrong ideas.

How it works

Risk management is the set of decisions that limit how much a single trade, and the account as a whole, can lose. The decisions are made in advance, while the outcome is still unknown.

In this context risk has a narrow meaning: the amount that would be lost in a defined bad outcome, such as price reaching a stop loss. It is a number that can be written down, either as an amount or as a percentage of the account.

In plain words

A seat belt does not steer the car and does not make the journey faster. It limits the damage when something goes wrong, and it is fastened before the car moves, not during the skid. Risk management plays the same part: it is set up before the outcome is known.

The building blocks

Building block The question it answers
Risk per trade How much of the account is lost if this one idea fails?
Position sizing How large can the position be for that loss to stay at the chosen amount?
Stop placement At what price is the idea considered wrong?
Risk/reward ratio How does the planned loss compare with the planned gain?
Drawdown How far has the account fallen from its peak, and what does recovery take?
Diversification and correlation Do the open positions behave like several ideas or like one?
Trading plan Are the rules written down before the session starts?

The pieces form a chain. The stop defines where the idea is wrong, the risk per trade defines what that may cost, and the two together produce the position size.

Going deeper

Kinds of risk

A price chart shows only one kind of risk. Others sit beside it:

  • Market risk. Price moves against the position.
  • Leverage risk. Borrowed exposure magnifies every move and adds the possibility of forced closure. See leverage risk.
  • Liquidity risk. An order fills at a worse price than expected, which is called slippage, or does not fill at all.
  • Counterparty risk. The exchange, broker or protocol holding the funds fails. See counterparty risk.
  • Operational risk. A wrong order size, a lost key, a mistyped address.
  • Behavioural risk. A plan exists and is abandoned under stress. See why psychology matters.

Why the size of losses matters so much

Losses and gains are not symmetrical. An account that falls 50% needs a 100% gain to return to where it began, because the gain is earned on a smaller balance. In general, a loss of L needs a gain of L / (1 - L) to be undone.

Five losing trades in a rowHypothetical illustration

Two hypothetical accounts start at 10,000. Each loses five trades in a row, which can happen with any approach.

  • Account A loses 1% of its current balance each time. After five losses it holds 10,000 × 0.99 × 0.99 × 0.99 × 0.99 × 0.99, about 9,510. That is a fall of 4.9%, and a gain of about 5.2% returns it to 10,000.
  • Account B loses 10% each time. It holds about 5,905, a fall of about 41%. Returning to 10,000 needs a gain of about 69%.

The trades and the market were identical. The only difference was the size of each loss.

A commonly cited convention in trading literature is to keep the loss on any single trade to a small fixed fraction of the account, such as 1 to 2 percent. It is a convention that authors describe, not a rule that fits every person, market or method.

What it does NOT mean

  • Risk management does not make trades profitable. It limits the cost of being wrong. Whether an approach gains over time depends on other things too.
  • It does not remove risk. Stops can be jumped by gaps, venues can fail and assets that looked unrelated can fall together. The aim is to limit losses, not to abolish them.
  • It is not the same as trading small. A small position with no defined exit is still an unmeasured risk.
  • A stop loss alone is not risk management. A stop says where the exit is. Without a matching position size it does not say how much is lost.
  • Planning for a loss is not predicting one. It is an admission that the outcome is unknown.

Common mistakes

  1. Choosing the size first. Picking a position size because it “feels right” and fitting the stop around it reverses the logic.
  2. Judging each trade in isolation. Several positions that move together carry the combined risk of all of them.
  3. Ignoring costs. Fees, funding payments and slippage are losses too, and they occur on winning trades as well.
  4. Changing the rules mid-trade. Moving a stop further away after entry increases the loss beyond what was planned.
  5. Counting only chart risk. Funds can also be lost through a failed venue, a scam or a simple mistake.

Risks and limitations

  • A planned loss is an estimate. Fast markets, gaps and thin liquidity can make the real loss larger.
  • Rules protect only while they are followed. The hardest moment to follow them is usually just after a loss.
  • Percentages hide scale. A small fraction of money that someone cannot afford to lose is still too much for that person.
  • No set of risk rules can turn an approach that loses on average into one that gains.

Key takeaway

Risk management is deciding, before the outcome is known, how much a trade and an account are allowed to lose. It does not improve the odds of being right. It controls the cost of being wrong.
