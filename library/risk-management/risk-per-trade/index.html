Risk Per Trade

Risk per trade is the amount an account would lose if one trade is closed at its planned stop, usually expressed as a percentage of the account.

Why it matters

Any approach to trading produces losing trades, and sometimes several in a row. Risk per trade is the number that decides what such a run does to an account: a dent, a deep hole, or the end of it.

It is also the starting point for position sizing. Until the acceptable loss on one trade is known, there is no logical way to work out how large the position can be.

How it works

Risk per trade is the amount that would be lost if a trade is closed at its planned exit, the stop loss. It is usually written as a percentage of the account.

risk per trade = position size × stop distanceA position of 2,000 with a stop 5% away from the entry risks 2,000 × 0.05 = 100. On an account of 10,000 that is 1%.

Read the curve from left to right. At 1% per trade it takes about 69 losses in a row to halve an account. At 5% it takes about 14. At 20% it takes 4: three losses leave about 51%, and a fourth takes the account below half.

In plain words

Think of the account as a bag of matches and each trade as striking one. If every strike uses one match from a hundred, a run of duds is an annoyance. If every strike uses twenty, five duds empty the bag, however good the fire-lighting technique is.

Three numbers that are easy to confuse

  • Risk per trade is what is lost if the stop is reached.
  • Position size is the total value of what is held. A position of 2,000 loses 2,000 only if the price goes to zero.
  • Margin is the collateral posted for a leveraged position. See margin and collateral.

The same risk per trade can sit behind very different position sizes, depending on how far away the stop is.

Going deeper

Fixed percentage and fixed amount

There are two common ways to express the number.

Method How it works What follows
Fixed percentage (also called fixed fractional) The same share of the current balance on every trade, for example 1% The amount shrinks after losses and grows after gains
Fixed amount The same amount of money on every trade, for example 100 Simpler, but the share of the account grows as the balance falls

With a fixed percentage, each loss is slightly smaller than the one before, because it is taken from a smaller balance. This is why ten losses of 1% leave 90.4% of the account and not exactly 90%.

The convention of small fractions

A commonly cited convention in trading books is to risk a small fixed fraction of the account on each trade, such as 1 to 2 percent. The reasoning is arithmetic, not magic: at small fractions, long losing streaks remain survivable, and the gain needed to recover stays close to the size of the loss. The figure is a convention that authors describe. It is not a rule that suits every person, method or market.

One hypothetical trade, start to finishHypothetical illustration

An account holds 10,000. The plan for one trade allows a loss of 1%, which is 100.

  • The entry is at 50 and the stop is at 47.50, which is 5% below the entry.
  • A position of 2,000 loses 5% of its value at the stop: 2,000 × 0.05 = 100.
  • If the stop is reached, the account holds 9,900. The next trade at 1% would risk 99.

Now suppose the same account takes ten such losses in a row. It holds about 9,044, a fall of 9.6%. At 5% per trade the same ten losses would leave about 5,987, a fall of 40.1%, and a gain of about 67% would be needed to get back.

What it does NOT mean

  • Risk per trade is not the position size. A trade can be large in value and small in risk, or small in value and large in risk. The stop distance connects the two.
  • It is not a maximum that the market respects. A gap or slippage can fill a stop at a worse price, so the real loss can exceed the planned one.
  • A small percentage does not make a trade safe. It limits one loss. It says nothing about whether the idea was sound.
  • It does not describe total exposure. Five open trades at 1% each can lose 5% together if they move as one. See diversification and correlation.
  • It is not a recommendation. The percentages on this page are illustrations of arithmetic, not suggested values.

Common mistakes

  1. Having no stop and calling the risk small. Without a defined exit, the risk per trade is not a known number.
  2. Raising the risk after a loss. Trying to win back a loss quickly with a larger trade speeds up the decline. See revenge trading.
  3. Confusing margin with risk. Posting 100 of margin on a leveraged position does not mean only 100 is at stake in every situation, especially with cross margin.
  4. Forgetting costs. Fees and funding come on top of the planned loss.
  5. Counting trades separately when they are really one bet. Several positions in closely related assets share one risk.

Risks and limitations

  • The planned loss assumes the stop order fills near its price. In fast or thin markets it may not.
  • The arithmetic of losing streaks assumes each loss is the same share of the balance. Real losses vary.
  • A percentage says nothing about whether the money can be spared. That is a personal matter outside the scope of any formula.
  • Keeping each loss small extends survival. It does not create an edge, meaning an advantage that holds over many trades.

Key takeaway

Risk per trade is the loss at the planned stop, expressed as a share of the account. It decides how much damage a losing streak does, and it is the first input to position sizing.
