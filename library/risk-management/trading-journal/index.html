Trading Journal

A trading journal is a record of each trade's reasoning, risk, result and circumstances, kept so that decisions can be reviewed against evidence and not memory.

Why it matters

Memory is a poor accountant. People remember vivid trades, forget dull ones, and quietly rewrite the reasons for decisions once the outcome is known. A trading journal replaces that memory with a record.

It is also the most reliable source of the reasoning and state of mind behind a trader’s own statistics. Figures such as win rate, expectancy and drawdown cannot be known for one’s own trading unless the trades have been written down.

What it looks like

A journal can be a notebook, a spreadsheet or a dedicated app. The format matters much less than two properties: every trade is in it, including the embarrassing ones, and the reasoning is written before the outcome is known.

In plain words

A ship’s log records the heading, the weather and the decisions taken, hour by hour. Nobody writes it to feel good about the voyage. It exists so that, when something goes wrong, the crew can see what was actually done instead of arguing about what they remember.

What is recorded

Field Why it is there
Date, market and direction Basic identification
Reason for the trade What the chart showed and which rule of the trading plan applied
Entry, stop and target The plan for the trade in numbers
Size and planned loss Links the trade to risk per trade
Result, in money and in R R is the planned loss, so +2R means a gain of twice that amount
Fees and slippage Costs are part of the result
Rules followed? A plain yes or no, with a note if no
State of mind Calm, rushed, annoyed, bored, eager to make back a loss
Screenshot The chart as it looked at entry, not as it looked afterwards

Some people also record trades they did not take: setups that were skipped, and impulses that were resisted. Those entries show whether the plan’s filters are doing anything.

How to read it

Process and outcome

A single trade can be well made and lose, or badly made and win. The journal lets the two be judged separately. A win that came from breaking the rules is the most dangerous entry in the book, because it rewards the behaviour most likely to cause damage later.

Numbers over time

Read in bulk, a journal produces the statistics that describe a record: win rate, average win, average loss, average result per trade, longest losing streak and deepest drawdown. It can also split them, for example by market, by time of day or by state of mind.

Going deeper

What a journal guards against

Several well-documented habits of mind distort how people remember their decisions:

  • Hindsight. Once the result is known, it feels as if it had been obvious. A note written beforehand shows what was really expected.
  • Selective memory. Evidence that supports an existing view is recalled more easily. See confirmation bias.
  • Weight on the recent. The last few trades feel like the whole truth. See recency bias.

A written record does not remove these tendencies. It gives something fixed to check them against.

Review

A journal that is never read is only a diary. Review is usually done at a set interval, away from live prices, and looks for patterns across many trades instead of reasons for the latest one.

Reviewing a hypothetical journalHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical journal holds 40 trades. There are 18 winners averaging +1.8R and 22 losers averaging -1.0R.

  • Win rate: 18 / 40 = 45%.
  • Average result: 0.45 × 1.8 - 0.55 × 1.0 = 0.81 - 0.55 = +0.26R per trade.
  • Check: 18 × 1.8 - 22 × 1.0 = 32.4 - 22 = +10.4R in total, and 10.4 / 40 = 0.26.

The “rules followed?” column shows that 31 trades followed the plan and 9 did not. Split that way, the 31 trades total +14.4R and the 9 off-plan trades total -4.0R.

The journal does not prove that the plan is sound. Forty trades are few, and the next forty could look different. What it shows is something memory would have blurred: in this record, the off-plan trades cost a noticeable part of the total, and most of them were entered in a state noted as “annoyed” or “rushed”.

What it does NOT mean

  • A journal does not improve results by existing. It supplies evidence. What is done with the evidence is a separate matter.
  • A good-looking journal is not proof of skill. A short record can be flattering by chance. See overconfidence.
  • A losing trade in the journal is not a mistake by definition. Losses that followed the rules are part of any approach.
  • A winning trade is not a good decision by definition. The outcome of one trade says little about the quality of the choice.
  • Past statistics are not a forecast. They describe trades already taken under conditions that may have changed.

Common mistakes

  1. Recording only the winners. A journal with gaps produces statistics that are worse than none, because they look trustworthy.
  2. Filling in the reasoning afterwards. Reasons written after the result are shaped by the result.
  3. Recording prices and nothing else. Without the reason and the state of mind, the journal cannot explain behaviour.
  4. Never reviewing. The value is in reading many entries together.
  5. Drawing conclusions from a few trades. Ten entries can support almost any story.

Risks and limitations

  • A journal is only as accurate as its keeper is honest. Nobody else checks it.
  • Statistics from a small or mixed set of trades can mislead more than they inform.
  • Detailed logging takes time, and elaborate systems are often abandoned. A simple record that is kept beats a thorough one that is not.
  • A journal may contain sensitive financial details. Where and how it is stored is worth some thought.

Key takeaway

A trading journal records the reasoning, risk and result of every trade, with the reasoning written before the outcome is known. It turns trading into something that can be measured and reviewed, and it separates the quality of a decision from the luck of a single result.

Sources and further reading

  • Trading in the Zone, Mark Douglas (2000). On separating the quality of a decision from the outcome of a single trade
  • Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom, Van K. Tharp. On recording results as multiples of initial risk
  • Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman (2011). On hindsight and the unreliability of remembered judgments
