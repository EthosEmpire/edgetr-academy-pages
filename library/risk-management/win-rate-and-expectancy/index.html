Win Rate & Expectancy

Win rate is the share of trades that end in a gain. Expectancy combines it with the average size of wins and losses to give the average result per trade.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

“How often do you win?” is the first question people ask about any trading approach, and it is the wrong one to stop at. Win rate on its own says very little. A record that wins nine times in ten can lose money, and one that wins three times in ten can gain.

Expectancy is the number that joins win rate with the size of wins and losses. It describes the average result per trade over a long series, and it is the closest thing to a single summary of whether a record has been gaining or losing.

How it works

  • Win rate is the number of winning trades divided by the total number of trades.
  • Loss rate is the rest: 100% minus the win rate. Break-even trades are usually counted separately or grouped with the losses.
  • Average win and average loss are the mean sizes of each, measured in money or in units of risk (R). See risk/reward ratio.
expectancy = (win rate × average win) - (loss rate × average loss)The result is the average gain or loss per trade. Positive means the record gained on average, negative means it lost.

In plain words

A shop that sells a cheap item to nearly every visitor can still go out of business if each rare refund costs more than a week of sales. Another shop might sell to one visitor in ten and do well because each sale is large. Counting customers is not enough. You have to count what each one brings in or costs.

How it is calculated

The same inputs give the break-even win rate, the point where expectancy is zero:

break-even win rate = average loss / (average win + average loss)With an average win twice the average loss, this is 1 / 3 = 33.3%. With equal sizes it is 50%. With wins three times the losses it is 25%.

Costs belong inside the averages. Trading fees, funding and slippage make average wins smaller and average losses larger, so a record that looks slightly positive before costs can be negative after them.

Going deeper

Expectancy is an average, not a schedule

A positive expectancy describes the long-run average of many trades. It does not say what the next trade does, and it does not prevent long runs of losses.

This is why expectancy is always read together with risk per trade and drawdown. A record can have positive expectancy and still be wiped out by a losing streak if each loss is too large. See risk of ruin.

Sample size

Win rate and expectancy calculated from a few trades are unreliable. With twenty trades, changing the outcome of two of them can flip the sign of the result. Estimates settle down only over a large number of trades taken under similar conditions, and markets do not stay similar for ever.

The past record is not the future

Expectancy is calculated from trades that have already happened. Conditions change: a trending market becomes a range, volatility shifts, costs rise. A number from one period may not hold in the next.

Twenty hypothetical tradesHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical journal shows 20 trades.

  • 8 winners with an average gain of 150.
  • 12 losers with an average loss of 80.

Win rate: 8 / 20 = 40%. Loss rate: 60%.

Expectancy: 0.4 × 150 - 0.6 × 80 = 60 - 48 = +12 per trade. As a check, the total is 8 × 150 - 12 × 80 = 1,200 - 960 = 240, and 240 / 20 = 12.

Break-even win rate: 80 / (150 + 80) = 34.8%.

Now change two of the winners into average losers. There are 6 winners and 14 losers: 6 × 150 - 14 × 80 = 900 - 1,120 = -220, which is -11 per trade. Two trades out of twenty turned a positive figure into a negative one. Twenty trades are too few to draw a conclusion.

What it does NOT mean

  • A high win rate does not mean a record is gaining. A few large losses can outweigh many small wins.
  • A low win rate does not mean a record is losing. Large wins can outweigh frequent small losses.
  • Positive expectancy is not a promise about any single trade. It is an average over many.
  • Expectancy measured on past trades is not a forecast. It describes what happened under past conditions.
  • A published win rate is not evidence of anything by itself. Without average win, average loss, costs and the number of trades, the figure cannot be interpreted. Be especially sceptical of win rates used in advertising.

Common mistakes

  1. Chasing a high win rate. Taking small gains quickly and letting losses run produces many wins and a negative average.
  2. Leaving out costs. Fees and slippage on every trade can erase a thin positive figure.
  3. Trusting a small sample. A few dozen trades can look far better or worse than the underlying average.
  4. Mixing different kinds of trades. Averaging trades taken for unrelated reasons hides what each kind is doing.
  5. Reading recent results as the true rate. The last ten trades feel more real than the last two hundred. See recency bias.

Risks and limitations

  • Averages hide the spread of results. Two records with the same expectancy can have very different drawdowns.
  • One unusually large win or loss can dominate the average.
  • The calculation assumes trades are comparable and independent. In practice, sizes vary and outcomes can cluster.
  • Records are only as honest as the person keeping them. A trading journal that omits bad trades produces a flattering and useless number.

Key takeaway

Win rate says how often trades win. Expectancy says what the average trade has gained or lost: (win rate × average win) - (loss rate × average loss). Neither is meaningful without the other, the costs and a large enough sample.
