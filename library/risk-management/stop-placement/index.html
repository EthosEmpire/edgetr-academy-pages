Stop Placement

Stop placement is the choice of the price at which a trade idea is treated as wrong, and it sets the stop distance that position sizing depends on.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A stop loss is the planned exit when a trade goes wrong. Stop placement is the question of where exactly it goes. That single price decides two things at once: how often the trade is closed by ordinary movement, and, through position sizing, how large the position can be.

A stop that is too close turns sound ideas into a string of small losses. A stop with no logic behind it turns the risk calculation into guesswork.

What it looks like

The core idea in most trading literature is that a stop marks the invalidation level: the price at which the reason for taking the trade no longer holds. Under that logic, the question is not “how much am I willing to lose?” but “where is this idea wrong?”. The amount lost is then controlled by the size of the position, not by squeezing the stop.

In plain words

A smoke alarm placed directly above the toaster goes off every morning and teaches you nothing. One placed in the hallway rings only when something is really burning. A stop works in a similar way: it is meant to be reached when the idea has failed, not every time the market breathes.

How stops are commonly placed

Method The stop is placed... Limitation
Structure-based Beyond a support or resistance area, swing point or trend line Levels are zones, and obvious levels attract many stops
Volatility-based A multiple of a volatility measure such as ATR away from the entry The measure lags, and volatility can jump
Percentage-based A fixed percentage from the entry Ignores both structure and volatility
Time-based The trade is closed if nothing happens within a set time Not a price stop, so it does not cap the loss

These methods are often combined, for example a stop beyond a structure level with a volatility buffer added.

How to read the trade-off

Stop distance and position size are linked by one formula: position size = amount risked / stop distance. Halving the stop distance doubles the position for the same planned loss. It also places the stop where normal fluctuation is more likely to reach it.

Going deeper

Stops gather in obvious places

Many market participants place stops just beyond the same visible levels: recent swing lows, round numbers, the edge of a range. When price reaches such an area, the stops become market orders and can push price a little further before it turns back. This is one of the mechanics behind fakeouts.

A stop is an order, and orders have limits

A stop is usually implemented as a stop order. A stop-market order fills at the next available price, which in a fast market or after a gap can be worse than the stop price. A stop-limit order controls the price and may not fill at all. The planned loss is therefore an estimate.

Moving a stop

A stop moved in the direction of a winning trade, often called a trailing stop, reduces the open risk. A stop moved further away from the entry after the trade has started increases the loss beyond what was planned and sized for. The second case is closely tied to loss aversion.

The same idea with two different stopsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical long is opened at 100. Typical movement per period (ATR) is 2, and there is a support zone at 96 to 97. The plan allows a loss of 100.

  • Stop at 99. The distance is 1%, so the position is 100 / 0.01 = 10,000, or 100 units. The stop sits half an ATR from the entry.
  • Stop at 95. The distance is 5%, so the position is 100 / 0.05 = 2,000, or 20 units. The stop sits beyond the support zone.

Now suppose each stop fills 0.5 worse than its price because of slippage. The first loses 100 units × 1.5 = 150, which is 50% more than planned. The second loses 20 units × 5.5 = 110, which is 10% more than planned. The same slippage does more damage to the tight stop.

What it does NOT mean

  • A stop does not guarantee the exit price. It is an instruction to exit, and the fill depends on the market at that moment.
  • A wider stop is not a bigger risk by itself. With the position sized to match, the planned loss is the same. Without resizing, it is larger.
  • A tight stop is not a sign of discipline. If it sits inside ordinary movement, it mostly records noise.
  • Being stopped out does not prove the analysis was wrong, and avoiding a stop does not prove it was right. Price can reverse just after a stop is reached, and it can also keep going.
  • No placement method finds the “correct” price. Each is a convention with a known weakness.

Common mistakes

  1. Placing the stop to fit a desired position size. In the convention described here the logic runs the other way: the stop level comes first and the size follows from it.
  2. Putting the stop exactly on an obvious level. Levels are zones, and price often probes slightly beyond them.
  3. Using one fixed percentage for every market. The same distance can be noise in one asset and a large move in another.
  4. Widening the stop as price approaches it. This changes the plan at the moment it matters most.
  5. Keeping a “mental stop” only. A stop that exists only in the mind depends on a decision made under stress.

Risks and limitations

  • Gaps, thin liquidity and fast moves can produce fills far from the stop price.
  • With leverage, the liquidation price may be closer than the stop, in which case the stop is never reached.
  • Volatility measures describe the recent past. They can understate movement just before volatility expands.
  • No stop method removes the trade-off between being closed out too early and losing too much.

Key takeaway

In the convention most trading books describe, a stop sits where the trade idea is no longer valid, and the position is then sized so that the loss at that price is the planned one. Distance, size and the frequency of stop-outs are tied together, and no placement removes that trade-off.

Sources and further reading

  • New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, J. Welles Wilder Jr. (1978). Introduced Average True Range, widely used to measure typical price movement
