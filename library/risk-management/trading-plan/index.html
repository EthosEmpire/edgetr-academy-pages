Trading Plan

A trading plan is a written set of rules, made in advance, that describes what a person trades, when, how much is at risk and how each trade ends.

Why it matters

Decisions made while money is moving are made under pressure. Fear, hope and the urge to act all push in directions that have little to do with analysis. A trading plan moves the important decisions to an earlier, calmer moment and writes them down.

A plan does not make anyone right about the market. Its job is narrower: to make behaviour consistent, so that results can be measured and so that one bad moment does not undo months of care.

What it looks like

A trading plan is a written document. It can be short. What makes it a plan is that it is specific enough for someone else to read it and say, for any given trade, whether the rules were followed.

In plain words

A pilot runs through a checklist before every take-off, including on days when everything seems fine. The checklist does not fly the aircraft. It makes sure the same important steps happen every time, especially when the pilot is tired, rushed or distracted.

How to read the parts

Section A vague version A specific version (hypothetical)
Conditions “Trade when the chart looks strong” “Consider a long only if price is above a rising 50-period moving average on the daily chart”
Exit if wrong “Get out if it goes badly” “Stop below the most recent swing low, placed as an order at entry”
Size “Not too big” “Loss at the stop equals a fixed share of the account, written here as a number”
Limits “Stop when tired” “No new trades after a set number of losses in one day”

The right-hand column is there to show the form of a rule, not to suggest its content. The numbers and conditions in a real plan are personal choices that depend on the person, the market and the method.

Two parts are often missing from beginners’ plans:

  • Conditions for doing nothing. Most of the time, the conditions in a plan are not met. A plan that describes when not to trade treats holding no position as a normal state, not as a failure.
  • Limits on the whole account. Rules for a single trade do not cap the damage from a bad day. Many plans add a ceiling on total open risk and a point at which trading stops for the session.

Going deeper

The plan and the risk pages

Each number in the risk section of a plan comes from a concept covered elsewhere in this shelf: risk per trade, position sizing, stop placement and risk/reward ratio. The plan is where those separate ideas become one set of instructions to oneself.

A plan is tested by a record

A plan and a trading journal work as a pair. The plan says what is supposed to happen. The journal shows what did happen. Without the journal there is no way to tell whether poor results come from the rules or from not following them.

When a plan changes

Plans are revised, and markets change. The convention described in most trading literature is that changes are made away from the screen, on the evidence of a meaningful number of trades, and not in the middle of a losing trade. A rule changed during a trade is usually not a revision. It is the plan being abandoned.

A hypothetical plan at work on one eveningHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical trader has a one-page plan. Among its rules: trades are considered only on the daily chart, the stop is placed as an order at entry, the loss at the stop is a fixed share of the account, and no trade is taken within an hour of a scheduled news release.

One evening a coin on the watchlist jumps 12% in twenty minutes after a rumour. Social media is full of excitement.

  • Conditions. The move is on a five-minute chart and follows a news event. The plan’s conditions are not met.
  • Action by the plan. No trade. The event is noted in the journal, together with the urge to join in.

Whether the coin went on rising or fell back is unknown at the moment of the decision and plays no part in judging it. By the plan’s standard the decision was correct either way, because the standard is whether the rules were followed.

What it does NOT mean

  • A plan does not make trades profitable. Consistent rules that lose on average produce consistent losses. A plan makes results measurable, not positive.
  • A plan is not a prediction. It states what will be done under certain conditions. It does not claim to know what the market does next.
  • Having a plan is not the same as following one. A plan protects only at the moments it is obeyed.
  • A detailed plan is not a better plan. Long rulebooks are harder to follow. Clarity matters more than length.
  • A plan is not permanent. Rules that fitted one kind of market can stop fitting when conditions change.

Common mistakes

  1. Keeping the plan in the head. Unwritten rules bend to fit whatever the trader wants to do in the moment.
  2. Writing rules that cannot be checked. “Trade with the trend” is a preference. A rule states how the trend is identified.
  3. Planning entries and leaving exits open. The exit decides the result of a trade, and it is the harder decision to make under pressure.
  4. Rewriting the plan after every loss. A handful of trades is too small a sample to judge any rule. See recency bias.
  5. Copying someone else’s plan. Rules built for another person’s time, capital and temperament are hard to follow when they are tested.

Risks and limitations

  • A plan cannot foresee every situation. Gaps, outages and unusual events fall outside any rulebook.
  • Rules reduce impulsive decisions. They do not remove the emotions behind them. See patience and discipline.
  • A plan built by fitting rules to past charts can look excellent in hindsight and fail on new data.
  • Following a plan faithfully does not protect against losing the money involved.

Key takeaway

A trading plan is a written set of rules, made in advance, covering what is traded, when, how much is at risk and how each trade ends. It makes behaviour consistent and measurable. It does not make outcomes certain.
