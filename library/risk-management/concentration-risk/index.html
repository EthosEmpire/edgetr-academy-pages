Concentration Risk

Concentration risk is the danger that comes from having a large share of capital depend on a single asset, theme, venue or outcome.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A portfolio can look sensible position by position and still depend almost entirely on one thing going right. Concentration risk is the name for that dependence. When the one thing fails, there is nothing else to absorb the loss.

It is the mirror image of diversification, and it tends to build up unnoticed, especially after a holding has done well.

How it works

The weight of a holding is its share of the total account. The effect of a price move on the account is the weight multiplied by the move.

account impact = weight × move of the assetAn asset that is 20% of the account and falls 50% takes 0.20 × 0.50 = 10% off the account.

Concentration means that one weight, or a group of weights that behave as one, is large enough for a single event to cause serious damage to the whole.

In plain words

A table with one thick leg in the middle can hold a lot of weight until someone kicks that leg. A table with four legs wobbles when one is kicked and stays standing. Concentration is not about how strong the leg looks. It is about what happens to the table when that leg goes.

Where it hides

Concentration in a single asset is easy to see. The other forms are less obvious:

  • Theme. Several tokens from the same sector often move together. Ten holdings in one narrative behave much like one holding. This is concentration through correlation.
  • Direction. A set of positions that are all long gains or loses together in a market-wide move.
  • Venue. Funds kept on one exchange or in one protocol share its counterparty risk or smart contract risk, whatever the assets are.
  • Dependency. Holdings that all rely on one stablecoin, chain or bridge share that single point of failure. See stablecoin risks.

Going deeper

Concentration builds by itself

A holding that rises faster than the rest becomes a larger share of the account without any new purchase. This is sometimes called drift. A portfolio that began balanced can become concentrated simply because one part did well, which is also the moment when its owner is least inclined to question it. See overconfidence.

Concentration cuts both ways

A concentrated account has a wider range of outcomes than a spread one. If the single holding rises, the whole account rises with it. If it falls, nothing cushions the fall. This page describes the arithmetic of that wider range. It does not say what mix of holdings is right for anyone.

Concentration and leverage

Leverage raises the effective weight of a position. A position worth twice the account has a weight of 200%, so a 10% fall in the asset takes 20% off the account. Concentration and leverage together compound the effect. See leverage risk.

A concentrated account and a drifting oneHypothetical illustration

Concentration. A hypothetical account of 10,000 holds 6,000 in asset X, a weight of 60%, and 4,000 spread elsewhere. Asset X falls 40% while the rest is unchanged.

  • Loss: 6,000 × 0.40 = 2,400, which is 24% of the account.
  • The account now holds 7,600. Returning to 10,000 needs a gain of 2,400 / 7,600, about 31.6%.

With a weight of 10%, the same fall in X would have cost 1,000 × 0.40 = 400, or 4% of the account.

Drift. Another hypothetical account starts with 25 in each of four assets, 100 in total. One of them triples to 75 while the others stay at 25. The account is now worth 150, and the asset that tripled makes up 75 / 150 = 50% of it. No purchase was made, and the account went from evenly spread to half in one asset.

What it does NOT mean

  • Concentration is not the same as a large loss. It describes exposure, not outcome. A concentrated account can go a long time without the feared event.
  • Many holdings do not mean low concentration. If they share a theme, a venue or a dependency, the concentration is still there.
  • A strong conviction does not reduce the risk. How certain someone feels has no effect on what happens to the asset.
  • Past gains from a concentrated position are not evidence that concentration was wise. The same exposure could have produced an equally large loss.
  • Spreading across assets does not remove venue risk. Ten assets on one failed exchange are lost together.

Common mistakes

  1. Looking only at the list of names. The question is what the holdings depend on, not how many there are.
  2. Ignoring drift. Weights change as prices move, so a past check of the mix goes out of date.
  3. Treating stablecoins and exchange balances as riskless. They carry issuer and venue risk of their own.
  4. Adding to a falling position without checking its weight. Each addition raises the share of the account that depends on one outcome. See sunk cost fallacy.
  5. Forgetting leverage when calculating weight. The weight that matters is position value over account value, not margin over account value.

Risks and limitations

  • The formula uses a single price move. In reality a failing asset can keep falling, and liquidity can vanish, making an exit difficult.
  • Hidden links between holdings often become visible only during a crisis.
  • Reducing concentration has costs as well: trading fees, possible taxes, and exposure to assets that are less well understood. Rules on tax differ by jurisdiction.
  • There is no universal threshold at which a weight becomes “too concentrated”. It depends on circumstances this page cannot know.

Key takeaway

Concentration risk is a large share of capital depending on one asset, theme, venue or dependency. The effect on the account is the weight multiplied by the move, and the weight can grow unnoticed as prices change.
