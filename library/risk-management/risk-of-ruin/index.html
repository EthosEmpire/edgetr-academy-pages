Risk of Ruin

Risk of ruin is the probability that an account falls to a level where trading cannot continue, given a win rate, a payoff ratio and a risk per trade.

Why it matters

An approach can gain on average and still destroy the account that follows it. The average only helps a trader who is still there to collect it. Risk of ruin is the probability of not being there: of losses accumulating to the point where trading cannot continue.

It is the idea that ties together win rate and expectancy, risk per trade and drawdown. Its main lesson is that survival depends heavily on the size of each loss, the input most directly under a trader’s control.

How it works

Ruin needs a definition before it can be measured. In the strict sense it means losing all capital. In practice it is often defined as a drawdown deep enough to end trading, such as 50%, because the gain needed to recover from there is already 100%.

Risk of ruin is then the probability of reaching that level, given three things: how often trades win, how large wins are compared with losses, and what share of capital each loss removes.

In plain words

Imagine a board game where you move forward two squares on a good roll and back one on a bad roll. On average you advance. But there is a cliff a few squares behind the start. Begin close to the cliff, and a short run of bad rolls ends the game before the average has time to work. Begin far from it, and the same run is only a setback. Risk per trade decides how far from the cliff you start.

How it is calculated

Losses in a row

The simplest calculation asks how many consecutive losses reach the ruin level. With each loss taken as a fixed percentage of the remaining balance:

Risk per trade Losses in a row to fall at least 50%
1% 69
2% 35
5% 14
10% 7
25% 3

A run of 69 losses is extremely unlikely for most records. A run of 7 is not, over a long enough series. See the streak figures in win rate and expectancy.

The classical model

Probability textbooks treat a simplified version called the gambler’s ruin problem. A player stakes one unit on each round, wins or loses one unit, wins with probability p, starts with n units and keeps playing indefinitely.

probability of ruin = ((1 - p) / p) ^ n when p is above 0.5When p is 0.5 or below, ruin is certain in this model if play continues long enough. A larger n only delays it.

With p = 0.55, the ratio (1 - p) / p is 0.45 / 0.55, about 0.818. Starting with 10 units gives about 13.4%. Starting with 20 units, which means staking half as much each time, gives about 1.8%. Halving the stake did not halve the risk. It squared it.

Going deeper

What the model leaves out

Real trading breaks every assumption in the classical model. Wins and losses differ in size. The win rate is unknown and has to be estimated from a limited record. It changes as markets change. Losses can cluster. Slippage and gaps make some losses larger than planned. More elaborate formulas and simulations exist, and they share one weakness: the answer is only as good as the inputs.

For that reason, risk of ruin is best read as a way to compare situations, not as a precise forecast. Its direction is robust even when its numbers are not: larger risk per trade, lower win rate and smaller wins relative to losses all push it up.

Fixed fraction and practical ruin

When each trade risks a percentage of the current balance, the balance can never reach exactly zero, because each loss is taken from a smaller amount. That does not make the account safe. It can still shrink to a level from which recovery is unrealistic, which is ruin in every practical sense.

The same hypothetical record at three stake sizesHypothetical illustration

Take the simplified model with a win probability of 55% and wins equal in size to losses. Expectancy is positive: 0.55 × 1 - 0.45 × 1 = +0.10 units per trade.

  • Stake of 1/10 of starting capital. Ruin probability: 0.818 multiplied by itself 10 times, about 13.4%. Roughly one such account in seven or eight is eventually wiped out, despite the positive average.
  • Stake of 1/20. About 1.8%.
  • Stake of 1/50. Under 0.01%.

Now lower the win probability to 50%. Expectancy is zero, and in this model ruin becomes certain if play continues indefinitely, whatever the stake. A smaller stake only makes it take longer.

What it does NOT mean

  • A positive expectancy does not make ruin impossible. It lowers the probability only if the stake is small enough to survive the losing streaks along the way.
  • A calculated risk of ruin is not a measured fact. It rests on a win rate and payoff ratio that are estimates from the past.
  • A low figure is not a safety certificate. The model cannot see changing markets, exchange failure, errors or a trader abandoning the rules.
  • Ruin is not only “zero”. A loss deep enough to stop someone trading has the same effect.
  • Risk of ruin does not say when. It gives a probability over a long horizon, not a date.

Common mistakes

  1. Looking only at average results. An average says nothing about whether the path to it is survivable.
  2. Feeding the formula an optimistic win rate. A rate taken from a short winning period makes the answer look far better than it is.
  3. Assuming losses arrive evenly spaced. Streaks are a normal feature of random sequences.
  4. Raising the stake after gains without recalculating. Growing confidence often raises risk per trade at the moment a losing streak would hurt most.

Risks and limitations

  • Every formula for risk of ruin depends on assumptions that markets do not honour: stable odds, independent outcomes and losses of a known size.
  • The true win rate of any approach is never known in advance and can decline without warning.
  • Small changes in the inputs can change the result greatly, particularly near a win rate where expectancy is zero.
  • With leverage, one liquidation can remove all of a position’s collateral in a single move, a path to ruin that models of many small losses do not capture.

Key takeaway

Risk of ruin is the probability that losses end an account before the long-run average can show. It rises steeply with the share of capital risked on each trade, which is why a positive expectancy alone does not ensure survival.
