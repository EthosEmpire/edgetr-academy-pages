Diversification & Correlation

Diversification spreads capital across holdings that do not all move together. Correlation, from -1 to +1, measures how closely two of them have moved together.

Why it matters

Holding several positions feels safer than holding one. Whether it is safer depends on a single question: do they move together? Five positions that rise and fall as one are, for risk purposes, one large position with five names.

Correlation is the measure that answers the question, and diversification is the practice built on it.

How it works

Correlation describes how closely the returns of two assets have moved together over a chosen period. It is expressed as a number between -1 and +1, called the correlation coefficient.

  • Near +1: the two have tended to rise and fall at the same times.
  • Near 0: no consistent relationship.
  • Near -1: when one rose, the other tended to fall.

Diversification means spreading capital across holdings whose correlations are well below +1, so that a loss in one is less likely to coincide with losses in all the others.

In plain words

A street stall that sells only umbrellas has a bad month whenever the sun shines. A stall that sells umbrellas and sunglasses has something to sell in either weather. Adding a second kind of umbrella would not help at all. Variety only counts when the items respond to different conditions.

How to read it

The practical consequence is that risk has to be added up across positions, not only measured one by one. If risk per trade is 1% and five highly correlated positions are open in the same direction, a single adverse move can cost about 5%. The combined position behaves like one trade with 5% at risk.

Two details are often missed:

  • Direction matters. A long and a short in two highly correlated assets partly offset each other. This is the idea behind hedging.
  • Correlation is not size. Two assets can move in the same direction every day while one moves three times as far. Correlation captures the consistency of the link, not the magnitude of each move.

Going deeper

Two kinds of risk

Finance textbooks separate risk into two parts. Specific risk belongs to one asset: a failed project, a hack, a delisting. Market risk, also called systematic risk, affects nearly everything at once. Diversification reduces the first. It does little against the second.

Correlation in crypto

Many altcoins have historically tended to move in the same direction as bitcoin, particularly during sharp market-wide falls. A portfolio of ten different tokens may therefore be far less diversified than the number suggests. Spreading across genuinely different asset classes is a different thing from spreading across similar tokens.

Correlation is not stable

A correlation figure is calculated from past prices over a chosen window, and a different window gives a different figure. Relationships shift as conditions change. A commonly observed pattern is that correlations between risky assets rise during market stress, so diversification can weaken at the moment it is most wanted.

Two hypothetical portfoliosHypothetical illustration

Each portfolio holds five assets with 20% of its value in each.

Portfolio A holds five closely related tokens. On a bad day all five fall 10%. The portfolio falls 10%, exactly as if it held one asset.

Portfolio B holds five assets with weaker links. On the same day two fall 10%, two are unchanged and one rises 5%.

  • Two falling assets: 2 × 20% × -10% = -4%.
  • Two unchanged assets: 0%.
  • One rising asset: 20% × 5% = +1%.
  • Total: -3%.

Portfolio B lost less, and it still lost. Had all five of its assets fallen together on a day of market-wide stress, the result would have matched Portfolio A.

What it does NOT mean

  • Diversification does not prevent losses. It reduces the effect of any single holding. A market-wide fall can still take everything down together.
  • More holdings do not automatically mean more diversification. Twenty assets that move as one offer the protection of one.
  • Correlation is not causation. Two assets moving together does not mean one drives the other. Both may be responding to the same outside factor.
  • A low correlation in the past is not a promise for the future. Relationships between assets change, sometimes abruptly.
  • Correlation near zero does not mean “safe”. An uncorrelated asset can still fall to nothing for reasons of its own.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting names, not behaviour. A list of different tickers can hide a single shared exposure.
  2. Adding up risk per trade as if positions were independent. Correlated positions in the same direction stack.
  3. Relying on one correlation figure. A number from a calm period can mislead about a turbulent one.
  4. Forgetting shared dependencies. Assets on the same chain, held on the same exchange or backed by the same stablecoin share risks that no price correlation shows. See concentration risk.
  5. Diversifying into things that are not understood. Spreading capital adds variety and also adds unfamiliar risks.

Risks and limitations

  • Correlation measures only straight-line relationships in past data. It can miss links that appear only in extreme moves.
  • Estimates from short histories are noisy, and many crypto assets have short histories.
  • Spreading capital widely also dilutes the effect of any holding that does well. Diversification narrows the range of outcomes in both directions.
  • No mix of assets removes counterparty risk if everything is held in one place.

Key takeaway

Correlation runs from -1 to +1 and describes how closely two assets have moved together. Positions that are highly correlated behave like one larger position, so diversification depends on how holdings behave, not on how many there are.
