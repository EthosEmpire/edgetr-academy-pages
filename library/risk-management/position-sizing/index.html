Position Sizing

Position sizing is the calculation of how large a trade can be so that the loss at the planned stop equals the amount chosen as risk per trade.

Why it matters

Two people can take the identical trade, with the same entry and the same exit, and end with very different results because one held ten times as much as the other. Position sizing is the step that turns an idea about price into a specific quantity.

It is also where risk per trade stops being an intention and becomes a number of units on an order ticket.

How it works

Position sizing answers one question: how large can the position be so that, if price reaches the stop, the loss equals the amount chosen as risk?

Three inputs are needed: the account size, the risk per trade, and the stop distance, which is the gap between the entry price and the stop loss.

position size = amount risked / stop distanceWith the stop distance written as a fraction of the entry price. Risking 100 with a stop 5% away: 100 / 0.05 = 2,000.

The same calculation in units of the asset:

units = amount risked / (entry price - stop price)For a long position. For a short position the stop is above the entry, so the difference is taken the other way round.

In plain words

Imagine carrying water across a room and being allowed to spill one cup at most. If the floor is smooth, a big bucket is fine. If the floor is bumpy, the bucket has to be smaller to keep the spill to one cup. The bumpiness is the stop distance. The cup is the risk. The bucket is the position.

How to read the result

The size is an output, not a choice. Once the risk and the stop are set, the position follows from one division. Three consequences are worth noticing:

  • A wider stop means a smaller position. The loss at the stop is the same; only the quantity changes.
  • Position size is not the amount at risk. A position of 2,000 with a stop 5% away risks 100, not 2,000.
  • A very close stop can demand more than the account holds. Risking 100 with a stop 0.5% away gives 100 / 0.005 = 20,000. On an account of 10,000 that size is only possible with leverage, which brings risks of its own.

Going deeper

Other sizing models

The percent-risk model above is the one most trading books describe. Others exist:

Model Idea Limitation
Fixed units The same number of units every time Ignores price and stop distance
Fixed value The same amount of money every time The loss at the stop varies from trade to trade
Percent risk Size from risk and stop distance Needs a defined stop
Volatility-based Stop distance set from a volatility measure such as ATR Volatility changes, and the measure lags

In a volatility-based model, a market with larger daily swings gets a wider stop and therefore a smaller position. The intention is for each trade to carry a similar risk regardless of how wildly the asset moves.

Costs and imperfect fills

The formula assumes the exit happens exactly at the stop price. In practice, trading fees add to the loss, and slippage can move the fill beyond the stop. Some people shrink the calculated size a little to leave room for both.

Sizing one hypothetical longHypothetical illustration

An account holds 10,000 and the plan allows a loss of 1% on one trade: 100.

  • Entry price: 50. Stop price: 47.50. The stop distance is 2.50 per unit, which is 5% of the entry.
  • Units: 100 / 2.50 = 40.
  • Position value: 40 × 50 = 2,000, which is 20% of the account.

If the stop fills at 47.50, the loss is 40 × 2.50 = 100, as planned. If the market is moving fast and the stop fills at 47.00, the loss is 40 × 3.00 = 120. The plan was 1% and the result is 1.2%.

What it does NOT mean

  • Size is not a measure of conviction. A position that grows with a feeling of certainty reflects a behaviour pattern, not a calculation. See overconfidence.
  • Position sizing does not improve the odds of a trade. It controls what a loss costs. It has no effect on which way price moves.
  • The calculated loss is not a ceiling. Gaps, slippage and fees can make the real loss larger.
  • A correct size on each trade does not cap total risk. Several positions that move together add up.
  • The formula does not choose the inputs. How much to risk and where the idea is wrong remain human decisions.

Common mistakes

  1. Moving the stop to justify a bigger size. Bringing the stop closer only to allow a larger position puts it where ordinary price movement can reach it. See stop placement.
  2. Sizing from the account balance alone. “Ten percent of the account on every trade” ignores the stop distance, so the loss differs every time.
  3. Mixing up percent and fraction. A 5% stop distance is 0.05 in the formula, not 5.
  4. Using leverage to reach the calculated size without checking the liquidation price. If liquidation sits closer than the stop, the stop is never reached.
  5. Recalculating after entry. Adding to a losing position changes the size and the risk at once.

Risks and limitations

  • The method needs a stop that is actually placed and honoured. Without one, the arithmetic is fiction.
  • Stop orders do not promise a fill price. The planned loss is an estimate.
  • Minimum order sizes and price increments on an exchange can force rounding.
  • Sizing every trade correctly does nothing for an approach that loses on average. See win rate and expectancy.

Key takeaway

Position size is the amount risked divided by the stop distance. It is a result of two earlier decisions, how much to risk and where the idea is wrong, not a number chosen first.
