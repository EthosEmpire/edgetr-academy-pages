Drawdown

A drawdown is the decline of an account or an asset from a peak to a later low, measured as a percentage of the peak.

  • Beginner
  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Every account and every asset spends time below its previous high. Drawdown is the measure of how far below. It is one of the plainest ways to describe risk, because it answers a question people actually feel: “how much of what I had is gone?”.

It also carries an arithmetic trap. A loss and the gain needed to undo it are not the same size, and the gap between them widens quickly as losses deepen.

What it looks like

A drawdown is the fall from a peak to a later trough, written as a percentage of the peak.

drawdown = (peak - trough) / peakFrom a peak of 120 to a trough of 96: (120 - 96) / 120 = 20%.

Three related terms are used:

  • Maximum drawdown is the largest peak-to-trough fall over a whole period.
  • Drawdown duration is the time from the peak until a new high is made. An account in this state is sometimes described as “underwater”.
  • Open drawdown includes losses on positions that are still open. See realized and unrealized P&L.

In plain words

Imagine climbing a hill and then walking down into a valley. The drawdown is how far below the hilltop you are standing, not how far above the car park where you started. You can be higher than where you began and still be in a deep drawdown.

How the recovery is calculated

After a loss, the account is smaller, so the same percentage gain produces less money. The gain needed to get back to the peak is therefore always larger than the loss.

gain needed to recover = L / (1 - L)With the loss L written as a fraction. A 20% loss: 0.20 / 0.80 = 25%. A 50% loss: 0.50 / 0.50 = 100%.
Drawdown Gain needed to return to the peak
5% 5.3%
10% 11.1%
20% 25%
30% 42.9%
40% 66.7%
50% 100%
75% 300%
90% 900%

For small drawdowns the two numbers are close. Beyond about 30% they separate fast.

Going deeper

Where drawdowns come from

A drawdown in a trading account is usually the result of a losing streak, and its depth depends on risk per trade. Ten losses in a row of 2% of the balance each leave about 81.7% of the account, a drawdown of 18.3%. Ten losses of 5% each leave about 59.9%, a drawdown of 40.1%.

Losing streaks appear in any sequence of uncertain outcomes, including approaches that gain on average. See win rate and expectancy.

Drawdown of an asset

The same measure applies to a price. A hypothetical asset that falls from 200 to 60 is in a 70% drawdown and needs a gain of about 233% to return to 200. Deep drawdowns have been common in individual crypto assets, and some never returned to their earlier highs. A past recovery in one asset says nothing about another.

Depth and time

Two drawdowns of the same depth can feel very different. One that recovers in a week is easier to sit through than one that lasts a year. Long drawdowns are where plans are most often abandoned, which is why the topic is closely tied to loss aversion and revenge trading.

Reading the hypothetical account aboveHypothetical illustration

The account starts at 100, climbs to 120, falls to 96 and later reaches 121.

  • Depth. (120 - 96) / 120 = 20%. Measured against the starting value of 100, the account is only 4% down, but drawdown is counted from the peak.
  • Recovery. From 96, returning to 120 takes a gain of 24, and 24 / 96 = 25%.
  • Duration. The peak is at period 3 and the new high at period 10, so the drawdown lasts 7 periods: 3 on the way down and 4 on the way back.

If the fall had continued to 60, the drawdown would be 50% and the gain needed would be 60 / 60 = 100%.

What it does NOT mean

  • A drawdown does not mean an approach is broken. Declines from a peak are a normal part of any uncertain activity. It also does not mean the approach is fine.
  • A drawdown is not a promise of recovery. Nothing obliges an account or an asset to return to its previous high.
  • Maximum drawdown is not the worst case. It is the largest fall so far. A larger one can still lie ahead.
  • An unrealized loss is not harmless because it has not been “booked”. The value has fallen whether or not the position is closed.
  • A small drawdown does not prove low risk. A short history may simply not include a bad period yet.

Common mistakes

  1. Measuring from the starting balance. Drawdown is counted from the highest point reached, which is usually a harsher number.
  2. Expecting the same percentage to undo a loss. A 50% fall followed by a 50% rise leaves the account at 75% of its peak.
  3. Raising risk to recover faster. Larger trades during a drawdown deepen it if the losing streak continues.
  4. Ignoring duration. Depth is only half of the description. Time below the peak matters to anyone who has to live through it.
  5. Trusting a short record. A handful of trades or a few weeks of prices cannot show what a deep drawdown looks like.

Risks and limitations

  • Drawdown describes the past. It measures what has happened, not what can happen.
  • The figure depends on how often value is recorded. A daily record can miss a deeper low reached within a day.
  • With leverage, a drawdown can end in forced closure before any recovery is possible.
  • One number hides the path. Two accounts with the same maximum drawdown may have had very different journeys.

Key takeaway

Drawdown is the fall from a peak to a later low, as a percentage of the peak. The gain needed to recover is L / (1 - L): 25% after a 20% loss and 100% after a 50% loss, which is why deep losses are so hard to undo.
