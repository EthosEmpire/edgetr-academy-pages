Where we are

In Chapter 1 you saw that a price is the most recent trade, and that an hour of trading can be summed up in a handful of numbers. This chapter shows how charts store those numbers, and how the length of the time period changes the picture.

By the end you will be able to read the four prices behind any chart, tell the main chart types apart, and explain why two people looking at the same market can honestly describe it in opposite ways.

Four prices per period: OHLC

A busy market produces thousands of trades an hour. A chart makes that readable by cutting time into equal periods and keeping just four prices from each one:

  • Open: the first trade of the period.
  • High: the highest trade of the period.
  • Low: the lowest trade of the period.
  • Close: the last trade of the period.

Together they are called OHLC. The hypothetical hour at the end of Chapter 1 looks like this:

Everything else is thrown away. OHLC does not record whether the high came before the low, how long price stayed at any level, or how many times it crossed the open. A chart is a summary, and every summary leaves things out.

The close gets the most attention because it is the final agreed price of the period. Most indicators, which you will meet in Chapter 6, are calculated from closing prices.

Chart types

A chart type is a way of drawing the same data. Changing the type never changes the prices underneath.

Chart type What it draws Strength Cost
Line Closing prices joined by a line Clean, shows the overall direction Hides highs, lows and the range of each period
Bar (OHLC bar) A vertical line from low to high, with a tick on the left for the open and on the right for the close Shows all four prices Slower to read at a glance
Candlestick The same four prices, with a thick body between open and close, coloured by direction Shows all four prices and is quick to read Can look busy on a crowded screen
Heikin Ashi Candles built from averaged prices Smooths the picture The candles do not show the real open and close

Candlesticks are the most common choice on modern platforms and the one used across this site. Chapter 3 is devoted to reading them. The Chart Types page compares the alternatives in more detail.

The price axis has a type too

A linear scale gives equal space to equal price differences. A logarithmic scale gives equal space to equal percentage changes, which matters on charts that span large moves. See Price Scales: Log vs Linear.

Timeframes

The timeframe is the length of each period. On a one-hour chart, every candle or bar summarises one hour of trading. Platforms abbreviate timeframes with a number and a letter.

Timeframe One period covers Periods in 24 hours
1m One minute 1,440
5m Five minutes 288
15m Fifteen minutes 96
1h One hour 24
4h Four hours 6
1d One day 1

The last column assumes a market that never closes, as most crypto markets do. Stock exchanges are open for only part of the day, so they print fewer intraday periods. Because crypto has no closing bell, the moment a daily period ends is a convention of the platform, often midnight UTC. See A Market That Never Closes.

Weekly (1w) and monthly (1M) charts follow the same logic. More on Timeframes on a Chart.

Why the timeframe changes what you see

Every timeframe shows the same trades. What changes is how much is packed into each period.

A low timeframe shows more detail and more noise: small moves caused by ordinary order flow that carry little meaning. A high timeframe shows less detail and less noise, because each period averages over far more trades.

This is why one market can be rising on the daily chart and falling on the 15-minute chart at the same moment. Both descriptions are true. They answer different questions: what has price done over recent weeks, and what has it done over recent hours.

In plain words

A coastline looks smooth from a plane and jagged from the beach. Neither view is wrong. The plane shows where the coast is heading, and the beach shows where the rocks are.

Three consequences follow:

  • The word “trend” needs a timeframe. Saying a market is going up means little until you say over what period.
  • Low timeframes change character quickly. A 5-minute picture can reverse several times in one afternoon while the daily picture stays the same.
  • A period is not final until it ends. A daily candle keeps changing all day, and its shape at noon may look nothing like its shape at the close.

Zoom and context

Zoom is a separate setting from timeframe. The timeframe decides how much time each period covers. Zoom decides how many periods fit on the screen.

Most charts rescale the price axis to fit whatever is visible. Zoom in on twenty quiet candles and a move of 1% can stretch from the bottom of the screen to the top. Zoom out to five hundred candles and the same move becomes a ripple. The move did not change. The frame around it did.

Many chart readers therefore look at a higher timeframe first to see the larger picture, then step down to lower ones for detail. This habit is often called top-down analysis, and it is covered on the Multi-Timeframe Structure page. Which timeframes suit which approach is discussed on Choosing a Timeframe.

The same hypothetical day, seen three waysHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset opens the day at 100, falls to a low of 96 around midday, climbs to a high of 105 in the evening and closes at 104.

  • Daily chart: one green candle. Open 100, high 105, low 96, close 104. The day shows a gain of 4%.
  • 4-hour chart: six candles. The first three step down from 100 towards 96. The last three climb to 104.
  • 15-minute chart: 96 candles. Dozens of small rises and falls, including stretches during the evening climb where price fell for an hour.

Someone watching the 15-minute chart at 11:00 saw a falling market. Someone who looked only at the finished daily candle saw a rising one. Both read their chart correctly.

What it does NOT mean

  • A lower timeframe is not more accurate. It shows more detail of the same trades, and more noise with it.
  • A higher timeframe is not the truth either. It is a broader summary that hides how price got from open to close.
  • A dramatic-looking candle is not always a big move. Check the numbers on the price axis before judging size.
  • A different chart type is not different data. Line, bar and candlestick charts draw the same prices.
  • A chart does not show the future. It is a compressed record of trades that have already happened.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. Which of the four OHLC prices does a line chart usually keep, and what does it leave out?
  2. On a market that trades around the clock, how many 4-hour periods make up one daily period?
  3. Price is rising on the daily chart and falling on the 15-minute chart. Is one of the charts wrong?

Key takeaway

A chart cuts time into equal periods and keeps four prices from each: open, high, low and close. The timeframe and zoom you choose decide what the summary shows and hides, so every statement about a chart needs a timeframe attached.
