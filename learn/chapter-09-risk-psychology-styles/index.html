Where we are

Chapter 8 showed how a position works and how leverage can turn a small move into a large loss. This chapter covers the three things that sit around every position: how much is at risk, how the mind reacts to that risk, and how long a position is meant to be held.

By the end you will be able to calculate a position size from a chosen risk, explain why drawdowns are hard to recover from, recognise two common emotional traps, and tell the main trading styles apart.

Risk is decided before the outcome

No analysis from the earlier chapters is certain. Risk management starts from that fact. It is the practice of deciding, before a position is opened, how much can be lost if the idea is wrong. Direction is a guess. The size of the possible loss is a choice. See What Is Risk Management?.

Position sizing

Position sizing works backwards from the loss a person accepts to the size of the position.

It needs three inputs: the account size, the share of the account put at risk on one idea, and the distance from entry to the stop loss.

Position size = amount at risk / stop distanceWith the stop distance as a fraction of the entry price, the result is the position's value.

In the figure, 1% of 10,000 is 100. With a stop 5% from entry, a position of 2,000 loses 100 if the stop is filled at its level. A tighter stop allows a larger position for the same risk, and a wider stop a smaller one. Size is the output of the calculation, not a number picked first. See Position Sizing and Risk Per Trade.

Risk/reward

The risk/reward ratio compares the distance from entry to stop with the distance from entry to target.

A plan that risks 4 to aim for 8 has a ratio of 1:2. The ratio and the share of ideas that work out are linked by arithmetic. At 1:2, a series of trades breaks even before costs if one in three reaches its target. At 1:1, it takes one in two.

The ratio is a property of the plan, not of the market. A distant target does not make price more likely to reach it. See Risk/Reward Ratio and Win Rate & Expectancy.

Drawdown

A drawdown is the fall in an account’s value from a peak to the following low, usually given as a percentage.

Losses and the gains needed to undo them are not symmetrical, because the gain has to be earned on a smaller balance:

Loss Gain needed to return to the start
10% About 11%
20% 25%
50% 100%
80% 400%

This is why the share risked on each idea matters so much. Ten losses in a row at 1% each leave an account about 10% down. Ten in a row at 5% each leave it about 40% down, and at 10% each about 65% down. Long losing streaks are uncommon in any short run of trades, but over enough trades they happen. See Drawdown.

Why the mind gets in the way

The arithmetic above is simple. Following it under pressure is not, because human judgement is biased in consistent ways.

The figure is a teaching model of how crowd mood tends to follow price: most confident near highs and most fearful near lows.

Research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky found that losses tend to weigh more heavily on people than gains of the same size. This is called loss aversion. In trading it shows up as holding a losing position in the hope of getting back to even, and closing a winning one early to lock in relief. Other well-documented biases include confirmation bias, noticing only the evidence that supports a view already held, and recency bias, giving the latest events too much weight. See Why Psychology Matters and Loss Aversion.

Two patterns are common enough to have their own names.

FOMO

FOMO, the fear of missing out, is the urge to enter because price is moving fast and other people appear to be gaining. It tends to be strongest after a large move, when the distance to any sensible invalidation level is greatest. The decision is driven by the move itself, with no plan behind it. See FOMO.

Revenge trading

Revenge trading is opening a new position straight after a loss in order to win the money back. It is usually larger and less planned than the trade that lost. The loss has changed the person’s emotional state, and nothing about the market. See Revenge Trading.

Why written rules exist

Fixed position sizes, pre-set stops and written plans are tools for making decisions while calm, so they do not have to be made while afraid or excited. They reduce the room for impulse. They do not remove it.

Trading styles

A trading style describes how long positions are typically held. The holding time decides which chart timeframes matter, how often decisions are made and how much costs weigh.

Style Typical holding time Typical timeframes What it demands
Scalping Seconds to minutes 1m to 5m Constant attention. Fees and spread are large next to each small move
Day trading Minutes to hours 5m to 1h Hours of focus. No positions held overnight
Swing trading Days to weeks 4h to 1d Tolerance for overnight and weekend moves
Position trading Weeks to months or more 1d to 1w Patience through large moves against the position

Separately from holding time, styles differ in the market state they are built around.

Trend following works in the direction of an established trend, using the structure from Chapter 4. Range trading works between the support and resistance zones from Chapter 5. The two dimensions combine: a swing trader may follow trends, and a day trader may trade ranges.

No style is better than another, and none removes risk. Shorter styles mean more decisions and more costs. Longer styles mean fewer decisions and larger swings to sit through. See Overview of Trading Styles and Trading vs Investing.

The same hypothetical idea at two sizesHypothetical illustration

An account holds 5,000. A made-up asset trades at 100, and the plan places the stop at 96 and the target at 108. The stop distance is 4% and the ratio is 1:2.

  • Sized by risk. Risking 1% means 50. Position size is 50 / 0.04 = 1,250, or 12.5 units. If the stop is filled at 96, the loss is 12.5 x 4 = 50.
  • Sized by feeling. The whole 5,000 is used, or 50 units. If the stop is filled at 96, the loss is 50 x 4 = 200, which is 4% of the account.

Now suppose five ideas in a row fail. The first account is down about 5%. The second is down about 18%. The chart, the entry, the stop and the target were identical. Only the size differed.

What it does NOT mean

  • A good risk/reward ratio does not make an idea likely to work. The ratio describes distances in a plan and says nothing about probability.
  • Small risk per trade does not prevent losses. It limits how fast an account shrinks during a losing streak.
  • Knowing about a bias does not switch it off. People who can name loss aversion still feel it.
  • A trading style is not a strategy. It describes holding time and market state, not when to enter or exit.
  • A stop distance is not a fixed loss. Slippage and gaps can make the real loss larger than the calculation.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. An account of 2,000 risks 1% on an idea with a stop 2% from entry. What is the position size?
  2. Why does a 50% loss need a 100% gain to recover?
  3. What separates revenge trading from simply taking the next planned trade after a loss?

Key takeaway

Risk is the part of a trade that can be chosen in advance: size follows from the loss a person accepts and the distance to the stop. Drawdowns grow harder to recover from as they deepen, emotions push against the plan at the worst moments, and a style only describes how long and in which market state a person trades.
