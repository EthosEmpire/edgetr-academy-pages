Start here

This is the first chapter and it needs no prior knowledge. If you have never opened a trading app or looked at a price chart, you are in the right place.

By the end you will be able to explain what a market is, where a price comes from, and what people mean by volume, volatility, spot and derivatives. Every later chapter builds on these words.

What a market is

A market is any place, physical or digital, where people who want to buy something meet people who want to sell it. A fruit stall is a market. So is a website where millions of people trade shares or bitcoin around the clock.

Every trade needs two sides. A buyer gives up money to receive the thing. A seller gives up the thing to receive money. Neither can trade alone, which leads to a fact that surprises many beginners: in every trade, the amount bought equals the amount sold. Price does not move because there are more buyers than sellers. It moves because one side is more eager and accepts a worse price to get the trade done.

In plain words

Think of a second-hand car. The seller hopes for 10,000. The buyer hopes to pay 9,000. Nothing happens until one of them moves. If they shake hands at 9,500, that is the price, and it tells you only what those two people agreed on that day.

The What Is a Market? page goes further.

Where price comes from

The price you see on a screen is the price of the most recent trade. It is a record of the last agreement, not an official value set by anyone.

On most exchanges, waiting orders are collected in an order book: a live list of the prices at which people are willing to buy and sell.

  • The bid is the highest price a buyer is currently offering.
  • The ask is the lowest price a seller is currently accepting.
  • The gap between them is the spread.

A trade happens when someone accepts the other side’s price. If eager buyers keep accepting the ask, the cheapest sell orders are used up and the next trade happens higher. If eager sellers keep accepting the bid, the opposite happens. A rising or falling price is nothing more than a series of trades, each agreed a little higher or lower than the last. See Buyers, Sellers & Price and Supply & Demand.

Volume: how much was traded

Volume is the amount of an asset that changed hands during a period, such as one hour or one day. If 500 coins were traded between 09:00 and 10:00, the volume for that hour is 500.

Volume shows how much participation stood behind a price move. A move on high volume involved a lot of the asset changing hands. The same move on low volume involved little, so it took less effort to cause. Volume does not say who is right or what happens next. More on Trading Volume Explained.

A related idea is liquidity: how easily an asset can be traded without moving its price. A liquid market has many orders waiting close to the current price and a small spread. In an illiquid market, even a modest order can push the price noticeably.

Volatility: how much price moves

Volatility describes how far and how fast price moves, in either direction.

A calm market might move half a percent in a day. A volatile one might move ten percent. Volatility is not good or bad in itself, and it has no direction: a market can be volatile while rising, falling or going nowhere. It matters because larger moves mean larger possible gains and larger possible losses over the same stretch of time. Read more on the Volatility page.

Assets: the things being traded

An asset is anything with value that can be owned and traded. Assets are grouped into asset classes whose members behave in broadly similar ways.

Asset class What is owned or traded
Stocks (shares) A small piece of ownership in a company
Bonds A loan to a government or company that pays interest
Commodities Raw goods such as gold, oil or wheat
Currencies (forex) One national currency priced in another
Crypto assets Digital tokens recorded on a blockchain

Assets are quoted in pairs. A made-up quote of ABC/USD at 100 means one unit of ABC costs 100 US dollars. See Assets & Asset Classes and What Is Cryptocurrency?.

Exchanges: where trading happens

An exchange is a venue that brings buyers and sellers together, keeps the order book and matches orders according to fixed rules. A broker is an intermediary that passes a customer’s order to an exchange. In stock markets, most individuals reach the exchange through a broker.

Crypto has two broad kinds of venue:

  • A centralized exchange (CEX) is run by a company. It holds customer funds and matches orders, a little like a stock exchange and a broker combined.
  • A decentralized exchange (DEX) is run by software on a blockchain. Users trade from their own wallets, often against a pool of tokens instead of an order book.

Each carries different risks. Details are on Exchanges & Brokers and in the CEX vs DEX comparison.

Spot and derivatives

There are two fundamentally different ways to trade an asset.

In the spot market, money is exchanged for the asset itself, straight away. After a spot purchase the buyer owns the thing: the share, the coin, the gold.

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from the price of something else. The holder does not own the underlying asset. They hold an agreement that gains or loses value as the asset’s price moves. Futures and options are derivatives. In crypto, the most widely traded derivative is the perpetual future, a futures-style contract with no expiry date.

Derivatives make it possible to trade with borrowed exposure, called leverage, and to gain from falling prices. They also add risks that spot does not have, including the forced closure of a position. Chapter 8 explains those mechanics. For now, the point to keep is the difference between owning an asset and holding a contract about its price. See What Are Derivatives?.

Crypto markets and stock markets

Both are markets in the sense described above: buyers, sellers, order books, price and volume. The differences lie in what is traded and how the venues work.

Two cautions apply. First, the table describes general tendencies, and rules differ between countries and between venues. Second, crypto is not one thing. A large, widely held asset and a token created last week are both crypto assets, and they can differ enormously in liquidity and risk. The Crypto Markets and Stock Markets page covers this in more depth.

One hypothetical hour in a marketHypothetical illustration

A made-up token has a best bid of 99.9 and a best ask of 100 as a new hour begins.

  1. A buyer in a hurry accepts the ask. The first trade of the hour prints at 100. That is now the price.
  2. More eager buyers arrive and use up the sell orders at 100, 100.1, 100.2 and 100.3. The price is now 100.3, although nobody announced a change.
  3. A large seller then accepts bids all the way down to 99.6.
  4. The hour ends with the last trade at 99.8. In total, 2,000 tokens changed hands.

The hour in five numbers: first trade 100, highest 100.3, lowest 99.6, last 99.8, volume 2,000. Those are exactly the numbers a chart records.

What it does NOT mean

  • Price is not value. The price is the last agreement between two parties. Whether the asset is worth that is a separate question, and people disagree about it constantly.
  • A rising price does not mean more buyers than sellers. Every trade has one of each. A rise means buyers were the more eager side.
  • High volume does not mean a move is correct. It shows participation, not who turns out to be right.
  • Volatile does not mean falling. Volatility measures the size of moves, not their direction.
  • Holding a derivative is not owning the asset. It is a contract with its own rules, costs and risks.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. If every trade has one buyer and one seller, what makes a price go up?
  2. Two assets both moved 3% today. One traded ten times its usual volume and the other a tenth. What is different about the two moves?
  3. What does a spot buyer hold after the trade that a perpetual futures trader does not?

Key takeaway

A market is buyers and sellers agreeing on prices, one trade at a time. Price is the last agreement, volume is how much was traded, volatility is how much price moves, and spot means owning the asset while a derivative is only a contract about its price.
