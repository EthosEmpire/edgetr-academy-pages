Where we are

In Chapter 4 you marked swing highs and swing lows and used them to name a market’s state. Those swings do not form at random prices. They tend to cluster in certain areas, and this chapter is about those areas.

By the end you will be able to mark support and resistance as zones, name what price does when it meets one, and explain why every one of those names is only certain in hindsight.

Support and resistance

Support is an area below the current price where falling prices have stopped and turned up before. At those prices, buying interest was strong enough to absorb the selling.

Resistance is an area above the current price where rising prices have stopped and turned down before. At those prices, selling interest was strong enough to absorb the buying.

The usual explanation is memory. People who bought in an area and saw price rise may buy there again. People who missed the move may be waiting for a second chance. People who are losing on a position may be waiting to get out at the price they entered. Their orders collect in the same region, so price tends to react there. That is a description of common behaviour, not a law. See Support & Resistance Basics.

In plain words

Think of a level as a crowded doorway, not a wall. People slow down there, and some turn back. With enough pressure the crowd goes through, and nobody can say in advance which push will be the one.

Why levels are areas, not lines

Look closely at any support area and the lows never match exactly. One swing low sits at 100.2, the next at 99.6, a third at 100.9. There are good reasons for that:

  • orders are spread across a range of prices, not stacked on one;
  • different traders anchor on different points, some on wicks and some on closes;
  • the same asset trades at slightly different prices on different exchanges;
  • a level seen on the daily chart is wide when viewed on a 5-minute chart.

So chart readers usually mark a zone: a band that covers the cluster of wicks and closes where price reacted. How wide to draw it is a judgement. Zones tend to be wider on higher timeframes and on more volatile assets.

Thinking in zones changes how a move is read. A wick that dips below the middle of a zone and closes back inside it has not broken anything. More on Zones, Not Lines.

Tests

Each time price returns to a zone and reacts, that is called a test. The numbered markers in the first figure are tests.

There are two common views on repeated tests, and they pull in opposite directions. One holds that every reaction shows the area still matters and draws more attention to it. The other holds that every test uses up some of the orders waiting there, so a zone tested many times in quick succession may be weakening. Neither is a rule. Both are worth knowing because both are widely believed.

Breakouts and breakdowns

A breakout is a move above a resistance zone that holds beyond it. A breakdown is the same event in the other direction: a move below a support zone.

What counts as “beyond” is a convention. Common ones include:

  • a candle close beyond the zone on the chosen timeframe, not merely a wick;
  • more than one consecutive close beyond it;
  • a rise in volume on the break, showing that more participants were involved.

Each convention trades speed for evidence. Waiting for a close filters out some false moves and means the price has already travelled further. None of them makes the outcome certain. See Breakouts and Breakdowns.

Fakeouts

A fakeout, or false breakout, is a move beyond a level that quickly reverses back through it. Fakeouts are common, and they are the main reason this chapter keeps using cautious language.

One often-cited mechanism is the clustering of orders. Many stop orders and breakout orders sit just beyond obvious levels. When price reaches them, they trigger together and cause a short burst. If no further interest follows, price falls back into the old range.

A fakeout and a real breakout look identical at the moment of the break. The difference only appears afterwards. More on Fakeouts.

Retests and role reversal

After a breakout, price often returns to the level it just broke. That return is called a retest.

If the old resistance now behaves as support, the level has changed roles. This is called role reversal, and the same logic applies when broken support later acts as resistance. A retest that holds is commonly read as extra context that the market has accepted the new side of the level.

Two limits apply. A retest does not always happen, and price can leave without looking back. When a retest does happen, it can fail, and the breakout then becomes a fakeout in hindsight. See Retests and Role Reversal.

Confluence

Confluence means that several independent observations point to the same price area. A horizontal zone, a rising trend line, a widely watched moving average and a round number might all sit near the same price. More observations mean more people are likely to be paying attention there.

The word that matters is independent. Three indicators built from the same closing prices are one observation counted three times. And an area with strong confluence is still only an area where a reaction is more widely expected. Expectation is not outcome. See Confluence.

The vocabulary in one table

Term What happened
Test Price reached a zone and reacted
Breakout Price moved above resistance and held, by the chosen convention
Breakdown Price moved below support and held, by the chosen convention
Fakeout Price moved beyond a level, then quickly returned through it
Retest Price came back to a broken level from the other side
Role reversal A broken level then acted in the opposite role

Every term in the table is a label for something that has already happened. None of them names something that is about to happen.

One hypothetical level, start to finishHypothetical illustration

Over several weeks, a made-up asset turns down from 119.6, 120.4 and 120.1 on its daily chart. A reader marks a resistance zone from 119.5 to 120.5.

  1. On the fourth visit, a candle reaches 121.0 and closes at 119.8. The wick went beyond the zone and the close did not. By the close-based convention this is a test.
  2. On the fifth visit, a daily candle closes at 122.3 on higher volume than recent days. By the same convention, this is a breakout.
  3. Three days later, price drifts back to 120.6 and the day closes at 121.9. This is a retest that has held so far, with old resistance acting as support.

Now change only step 3. Suppose that day had closed at 118.9, back below the zone. The candle in step 2 would be exactly the same candle, and it would now be called a fakeout. The label depends on what came after.

What it does NOT mean

  • A level is not a barrier. It is an area where reactions happened before. Past reactions do not oblige price to react again.
  • A breakout does not mean the move continues. Many breakouts fail, including ones with high volume and a clean close.
  • The number of touches is not a strength score. Repeated tests can be read as strengthening or weakening a zone, and both readings are sometimes right.
  • Confluence is not confirmation. Several reasons to watch an area do not add up to knowledge of what happens there.
  • A zone on your chart is not a fact about the market. It is your judgement about where to look, and another careful reader may draw it differently.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. Three swing lows sit at 49.7, 50.4 and 50.1. Why is a zone a better description than a line at 50?
  2. A candle’s wick goes above a resistance zone and the candle closes back inside it. By the close-based convention, what is that called?
  3. What has to happen after a breakout for it to be relabelled as a fakeout?

Key takeaway

Support and resistance are zones where price has reacted before, and tests, breakouts, breakdowns, fakeouts and retests are names for what price did when it met them. The names are assigned in hindsight, so a level tells you where to pay attention and never what the outcome is.
