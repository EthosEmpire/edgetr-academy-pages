Where we are

In Chapter 3 you learned to read a single candle, and saw that a candle means little without knowing where it sits. This chapter steps back from single candles to the shape that hundreds of them make together, which is called market structure.

By the end you will be able to mark the turning points on a chart, say whether a market is in an uptrend, a downtrend or a range on a given timeframe, and describe what a change of state would look like.

Swing points: the skeleton of a chart

Price does not travel in straight lines. It moves in waves: a push, a pause or partial retreat, then another push. The turning points of those waves are called swing points.

  • A swing high is a peak: a candle whose high is above the highs of the candles on both sides of it.
  • A swing low is a trough: a candle whose low is below the lows of the candles on both sides of it.

How many candles on each side are needed is a convention, not a law. Some people require two, others five. Requiring more candles gives fewer, more significant swings. What matters is using the same rule across the whole chart.

One limitation is built in. A swing point can only be identified after the fact, because it needs candles to its right. The most recent high on a chart is not yet a confirmed swing high. More on Swing Highs & Swing Lows.

Three states a market can be in

Once the swing points are marked, compare each one with the previous one of its kind. Is this high above or below the last high? Is this low above or below the last low? The answers sort a market into one of three states.

Uptrend: higher highs and higher lows

In an uptrend, each swing high is above the previous one (a higher high, HH) and each swing low is above the previous one (a higher low, HL). In plain terms, buyers were willing to pay more on each push and stepped back in earlier on each dip.

The dips inside an uptrend are called pullbacks. They are a normal part of a trend, not a sign that it has failed. See Uptrend and Pullbacks & Corrections.

Downtrend: lower highs and lower lows

A downtrend is the mirror image. Each swing high is below the previous one (a lower high, LH) and each swing low is below the previous one (a lower low, LL). Sellers accepted lower prices on each push and returned earlier on each bounce. The rises inside a downtrend are often called bounces or relief rallies. See Downtrend.

Range: no progress in either direction

In a range, swing highs form around one area and swing lows form around another. Price moves sideways between them. Neither side is making progress. Markets spend a good deal of their time in ranges, and a range can be wide and active. See Range.

Uptrend Higher Higher Buyers have been making progress
Downtrend Lower Lower Sellers have been making progress
Range Roughly equal Roughly equal Neither side has been making progress

Real charts are messier than diagrams. Sometimes a market makes a higher high and then a lower low, or the swings are too small to judge. “Unclear” is a valid reading, and often the most honest one.

In plain words

An uptrend is like a tide coming in. Each wave runs a little further up the beach than the last, and each time the water draws back it does not retreat as far. No single wave tells you the tide is rising. The sequence does.

Reading structure step by step

Describing structure is a small, repeatable routine:

  1. Fix one timeframe. Structure on the daily chart and structure on the 15-minute chart are separate questions.
  2. Mark the obvious swing points. Use the ones a person would notice from across the room, not every small wiggle.
  3. Compare each swing with the previous one of its kind. Label them HH, HL, LH or LL.
  4. Name the state. Uptrend, downtrend, range or unclear.

The result is a description of what price has done so far. It is not a forecast. A market labelled as an uptrend can stop being one with the very next swing.

Many chart readers also draw a trend line under the rising lows of an uptrend, or over the falling highs of a downtrend, as a visual aid. The Trend Lines page explains how they are drawn and why they are approximate.

Trend transitions

Trends end, but they seldom flip in a single candle.

Seen through structure, the end of an uptrend usually shows up in two steps. First, a push fails to exceed the previous high and leaves a lower high. Then price falls below the most recent higher low and creates a lower low. Many traders call that second event a break of structure.

At that point the description “higher highs and higher lows” is no longer true. That is all that can be said with confidence. What follows varies:

  • the market settles into a range;
  • a new downtrend develops, with further lower highs and lower lows;
  • the drop turns out to be a deeper pullback on a higher timeframe, and the rise resumes.

A common sequence is trend, then range, then either a new direction or a continuation of the old one. See Trend Transitions and Break of Structure.

The lasting value of structure is that it defines in advance what would change the picture. In an uptrend, the most recent higher low is the price below which the uptrend label stops fitting. Chart readers call such a point an invalidation level. You will meet it again in the chapters on position mechanics and risk.

Structure depends on the timeframe

Chapter 2 showed that one market can look different on different timeframes. Structure is where that matters most. A daily uptrend contains pullbacks, and each pullback, seen on the one-hour chart, is a small downtrend with its own lower highs and lower lows.

Neither label is wrong. The hourly downtrend sits inside the daily uptrend. Reading several timeframes together is covered on the Multi-Timeframe Structure page.

Labelling a hypothetical chartHypothetical illustration

Suppose the confirmed swing points on a made-up chart are, in order: low 100, high 112, low 106, high 121, low 113, high 119, low 108.

  • High 121 is above high 112: a higher high.
  • Lows 106 and 113 are each above the low before them: higher lows. So far, an uptrend.
  • High 119 is below high 121: a lower high. This is the first change.
  • Low 108 is below low 113: a lower low. The uptrend description no longer fits.

What can be said: the sequence of higher highs and higher lows has been broken. What cannot be said: that a downtrend has begun. Price is still above the earlier low at 106. This could become a range, a downtrend, or a deep pullback inside a larger rise. Only later swings will show which.

What it does NOT mean

  • An uptrend does not mean price keeps rising. The label describes swings that have already formed. Every uptrend in history has ended at some point.
  • One broken swing low is not a confirmed reversal. It ends one description. It does not establish the opposite one.
  • Structure is not objective to the last tick. Two careful people can mark different swing points and reach different labels.
  • A range does not mean nothing is happening. Ranges can be wide, fast and volatile.
  • A trend on one timeframe is not a trend on all of them. A label always belongs to a timeframe.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. A chart shows swing highs at 110, 118 and 115, and swing lows at 104, 109 and 107. How would you describe the structure?
  2. Why can the most recent peak on a chart not yet be called a swing high?
  3. A market is in an uptrend on the daily chart and a downtrend on the one-hour chart. How can both be true?

Key takeaway

Market structure is the pattern of swing highs and swing lows: higher and higher in an uptrend, lower and lower in a downtrend, roughly level in a range. It describes what price has done on one timeframe and marks what would change that description, and it does not say what comes next.
