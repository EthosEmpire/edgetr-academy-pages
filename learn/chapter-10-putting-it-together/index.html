Where we are

Chapter 9 covered risk, psychology and styles, and each chapter before it added one way of looking at a market. This last chapter adds no new tool. It shows how the pieces relate to each other, and where they stop.

By the end you will be able to describe a chart in layers, say what each layer contributes, notice when the layers disagree, and explain why “no position” is a legitimate conclusion.

Layers, not steps

Every earlier chapter supplies one layer of context:

  • Structure on a chosen timeframe (Chapters 2 and 4): trend, range or unclear.
  • Levels (Chapter 5): the zones where price has reacted before.
  • Candles (Chapter 3): what price did when it reached those zones.
  • Indicators and volume (Chapter 6): speed, volatility and participation.
  • Risk (Chapters 8 and 9): where an idea stops being valid and what is lost there.

The order runs from large to small because each layer gives meaning to the ones below it. A candle with a long lower wick is one thing in the middle of a range and another at a support zone inside an uptrend. The candle is the same. The context is not.

This is not a procedure that ends in a trade. The layers are questions, and the honest answers are often “unclear” or “they conflict”. There is no point at which enough ticked boxes turn into knowledge of what happens next.

In plain words

A weather forecaster looks at pressure, wind, satellite images and the season, then says there is a 70% chance of rain. Sometimes it stays dry. That is not a failure of method. It is what a 70% chance means. Unlike a weather model, a chart reading has no agreed, tested probabilities; the point is only that a careful process can still end in a loss. And when the picture is mixed, a good forecaster says so.

What each layer contributes

Layer Question it helps with What it cannot answer
Structure Is this timeframe trending, ranging or unclear? How long that state lasts
Levels Where has price reacted before? Whether it reacts there again
Candles What did price do on arrival? What the next candle does
Indicators and volume How fast, how volatile, how much participation? Anything not already in past price and volume
Risk Where is the idea wrong, and what is lost there? Whether the idea is right

Read the right-hand column as carefully as the middle one. Each layer describes something that has already happened. Stacking five descriptions of the past gives a fuller description of the past.

When the layers agree

Sometimes several layers point to the same area. The daily structure shows higher highs and higher lows. Price has pulled back into a zone that was resistance earlier. A rising trend line passes through the same prices. This is the confluence from Chapter 5, now across layers and not only across levels.

Agreement means more people are likely to be watching that area for similar reasons. It is commonly read as stronger context. It is still context:

  • The observations must be independent. A moving average, MACD and RSI all agreeing is closer to one observation than three, as Combining Indicators explains.
  • Well-aligned pictures fail regularly. Many of the fakeouts in Chapter 5 looked excellent before they failed.
  • A picture that everyone finds obvious is one that everyone has already had the chance to act on.

When the layers disagree

Disagreement is more common than agreement. The daily chart shows an uptrend while the one-hour chart makes lower highs. Price sits on a support zone and the candles show no reaction at all. Momentum is fading while structure is intact.

Conflict is information. It says the picture is unclear at this moment. The temptation, described as confirmation bias in Chapter 9, is to keep the layers that fit a view already held and quietly drop the one that does not. An honest reading keeps the inconvenient layer in view and accepts the conclusion that follows: the picture is mixed. See Multi-Timeframe Structure and Candlestick Context & Confirmation.

The layer that does not depend on being right

The first four layers are interpretation, and interpretation can be wrong. The risk layer is arithmetic. Given an invalidation level, a distance and a size, the loss if wrong is a number that can be estimated in advance, assuming the exit fills near the planned level. Gaps, slippage and liquidation can make the real loss larger.

That makes it the layer most directly under a person’s control, and it also works as a test of the other four. If the structure is too unclear to say where an idea would be wrong, there is nothing to measure a stop distance from, and so nothing to size against. An idea without a point of invalidation is not a plan. Many practitioners write these elements down beforehand in a trading plan.

Waiting is an outcome

Holding no position, often called being in cash or flat, is a state with its own properties: no exposure, no running costs and no forced decision. Practitioners commonly describe standing aside when:

  • the structure is unclear on the timeframe that matters to them;
  • the layers conflict;
  • the invalidation level is so far away that no sensible size fits;
  • their own state is off, for example straight after a loss or under the pull of FOMO.

A market has no duty to offer a clear picture, and for much of the time it does not. Concluding “there is nothing here for me to act on” is the analysis working, not failing. See Cash & No Position and Patience & Discipline.

Uncertainty stays

However carefully the layers are read, three things keep the outcome open:

  • every layer is built from past data;
  • other participants see the same chart and act on it, which changes what happens;
  • events from outside the chart, such as news or a sudden loss of liquidity, arrive without warning.

What a layered reading does provide is modest and real: an organised description, a defined point where the idea is wrong, and a loss that can be estimated in advance if that point is reached, although gaps and slippage can make the actual loss larger. Writers such as Mark Douglas describe this as thinking in probabilities. Any single outcome is uncertain, so a careful reading can be followed by a loss and a careless one by a gain. A trading journal is the usual way to compare the reasoning with what followed, across many cases and not just one.

Reading one hypothetical chart in layersHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset closes the day at 117. Its daily chart resembles the confluence figure above.

  • Structure: swing highs at 118 and 127, with a swing low at 109 between them. Price has since pulled back to 116. The confirmed swings show a higher high. The current low is not yet confirmed.
  • Levels: the zone from 115 to 118 was resistance earlier. Price has returned to it from above, and a rising trend line passes through the same area.
  • Candles: the latest daily candle dipped into the zone, left a long lower wick and closed near its high.
  • Indicators and volume: RSI has cooled from 70 at the high to 52. Volume during the pullback was lower than during the rise before it.
  • Risk: a reader using the zone as reference would call the idea wrong on a daily close below 115. That is 2 below the close, or about 1.7%.

Three careful readers can leave this chart with three conclusions. One sees aligned context. A second notes that the 4-hour chart is still making lower highs and prefers to wait until that changes. A third has no interest because the holding time does not match their style. All three are consistent with the reading.

What happened next is left out on purpose. The figure shows one continuation. A close at 113 the following day was equally possible, and the reading would have been just as careful.

What it does NOT mean

  • Aligned layers are not a buy or sell signal. They are a fuller description of the past and of where attention is concentrated.
  • This chapter is not a strategy. It describes how observations relate to each other. It contains no rules for entering or exiting.
  • Waiting is not a missed opportunity. Having no position is one of the possible conclusions, and often the one the evidence supports.
  • A careful analysis does not earn a good outcome. Process and result are linked only loosely, and only across many cases.
  • Knowing the vocabulary is not the same as skill. These chapters explain how things work. They do not make outcomes predictable.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head:

  1. Why does the order of the layers run from structure down to single candles, and not the other way round?
  2. The daily chart shows an uptrend and the one-hour chart shows a downtrend. What does that conflict tell you?
  3. Why is the risk layer described as the only one that does not depend on being right?

Key takeaway

Structure, levels, candles, indicators and risk are layers of context that give each other meaning. Together they organise what is known, define where an idea is wrong and leave the outcome uncertain, which is why waiting with no position is always one of the valid conclusions.
