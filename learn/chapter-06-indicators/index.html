Where we are

In Chapter 5 you marked the areas where price has reacted before. Everything so far has been read straight from price. This chapter introduces indicators: calculations drawn on top of the chart or in a panel below it.

By the end you will be able to say what each of eight common indicators measures, which family it belongs to, and why none of them can tell you what happens next.

What an indicator is

An indicator is a formula applied to past prices, past volume or both. The result is drawn as a line, a band or a row of bars.

An indicator holds no information that is not already in the chart. It rearranges that information so that one property, such as direction, speed, spread or participation, is easier to see. Two consequences follow:

  • Every indicator lags. It can only react after price has moved.
  • Indicators from the same family overlap. Two momentum tools built from the same closes will mostly agree, because they are measuring the same thing.

In plain words

A speedometer reports how fast the car is going right now, worked out from how the wheels turned a moment ago. It is useful, and it knows nothing about the bend ahead.

More background is on How Indicators Work.

Trend: SMA and EMA

A moving average smooths price into a single line so the general direction is easier to see.

  • The simple moving average (SMA) is the plain average of the last N closing prices. Every close counts equally.
  • The exponential moving average (EMA) gives more weight to recent closes. It turns sooner than an SMA of the same length, and it is also thrown around more by short-lived moves.

Lengths such as 20, 50 and 200 are popular conventions, not special numbers. Readers mostly look at two things: the slope of the line, and whether price is above or below it. In a range, price crosses a moving average back and forth and the line says very little. See Moving Averages and the EMA vs SMA comparison.

Momentum: RSI and MACD

The Relative Strength Index (RSI) compares the size of recent gains with the size of recent losses, by default over 14 periods, and scales the result from 0 to 100.

RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + RS), where RS = average gain / average lossDefault length: 14 periods.

Readings above 70 are conventionally called overbought and readings below 30 oversold. Those words are misleading. A high RSI says recent gains have been large compared with recent losses. It does not say a fall is due, and in a strong trend RSI can stay above 70 for a long time. See RSI.

MACD (moving average convergence divergence) is built from moving averages. The MACD line is the 12-period EMA minus the 26-period EMA. The signal line is a 9-period EMA of the MACD line. The histogram is the gap between the two.

When the MACD line is above zero, the shorter average is above the longer one, meaning recent prices are above the longer-term average. MACD restates what two moving averages already show, in a form that makes changes in speed easier to notice. See MACD.

Volatility: Bollinger Bands and ATR

Bollinger Bands place a 20-period SMA in the middle, with one band two standard deviations above it and another two standard deviations below. Standard deviation is a measure of how spread out recent closes have been. The bands widen when the market is volatile and narrow when it is quiet.

A touch of the upper band does not mean price is too high. In a strong trend, price can travel along a band for many candles. See Bollinger Bands.

Average True Range (ATR) is the average size of recent candles from high to low, adjusted for any gap from the previous close, by default over 14 periods. It is stated in price units. An ATR of 3 on an asset trading at 100 means recent candles have spanned about 3 on average.

ATR has no direction. It answers one question: how large is a normal move right now? See ATR.

Volume and VWAP

Volume, from Chapter 1, is the simplest indicator of all: one bar per period showing how much was traded. It adds a sense of participation to a price move. See Volume.

VWAP (volume-weighted average price) is the average price paid during a session, with each trade weighted by its size. Prices where a lot was traded count for more than prices where little was traded. It normally resets at the start of each session, commonly each day.

VWAP shows whether the current price is above or below the average price paid so far in that session. Large market participants have long used it as a benchmark for judging the price they obtained. See VWAP.

Trend strength: ADX

The Average Directional Index (ADX) measures how strong a trend has been, on a scale from 0 to 100, by default over 14 periods. It does not show direction. A steep fall and a steep rise can both produce a high ADX.

By convention, readings above about 25 are described as trending and readings below about 20 as weak or absent trend. Those thresholds are habits, not boundaries. See ADX.

Why indicators are context, not guarantees

Four properties limit every indicator, whatever its name:

  • Lag. The calculation uses past data, so the line turns after price turns.
  • Arbitrary settings. Change the length and the picture changes. Defaults are conventions that happen to be popular. Nobody has shown them to be the right values.
  • Redundancy. Three momentum indicators agreeing is one observation shown three times.
  • Dependence on market state. A moving average is informative in a trend and noisy in a range. Extreme RSI readings behave differently in a range than in a trend.

For these reasons, practitioners commonly keep few indicators, often one per family, and read them alongside the structure and levels from the previous two chapters. See Combining Indicators and Lagging vs Leading Indicators.

One hypothetical chart, four readingsHypothetical illustration

A made-up asset trades at 112 after rising from 100 over three weeks. Its daily chart shows:

  • price above a rising 50-period EMA at 106: the recent direction has been up;
  • RSI at 74: recent gains have been large compared with recent losses;
  • ATR at 2.5: a normal daily candle has spanned about 2.5, a little over 2% of the price;
  • ADX at 31: the move has been strong by the usual convention.

Together they describe a fast, strong rise. Every one of those readings is about the past three weeks. A sharp fall tomorrow would be fully consistent with all four, and so would a further rise.

What it does NOT mean

  • An overbought reading does not mean price must fall. It describes the speed of the recent move, and fast moves can continue.
  • A crossover is not a buy or sell signal. It marks a moment when two averages of past prices traded places.
  • More indicators do not mean more information. Tools from the same family repeat one another.
  • Default settings are not optimal settings. They are conventions, and no setting is correct for all markets.
  • An indicator cannot see what is not in past price and volume. News, changes in rules and sudden shifts in liquidity arrive without warning on any indicator.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. RSI and MACD both point the same way. Why is that weaker evidence than it first appears?
  2. ADX rises from 15 to 35. What does that tell you, and what does it leave out?
  3. Why does a moving average say more in a trend than in a range?

Key takeaway

An indicator is a formula applied to past price and volume that makes one property of the chart easier to see: trend, momentum, volatility or participation. It always lags, it depends on settings someone chose, and it describes what has happened without knowing what comes next.
