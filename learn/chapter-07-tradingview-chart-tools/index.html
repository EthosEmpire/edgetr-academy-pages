Where we are

Chapters 2 to 6 gave you things to look for on a chart: timeframes, candles, structure, levels and indicators. Chapter 6 ended with the limits of indicators. This chapter covers the tools a charting platform provides for marking and organising all of it.

By the end you will know what each common tool does, what it does not do, and how to recognise the same tool on any platform.

About the platform named in the title

TradingView is one widely used charting platform among several. This site is independent of it, and similar tools exist elsewhere under similar names. Menus, names and plan limits change, so this chapter describes functions in general terms and leaves the current details to the platform’s own help pages.

The parts of a charting screen

Most charting platforms share the same building blocks. A control selects the symbol, the short code for the market being shown. Another selects the timeframe. The chart itself fills the centre, with price on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal one. Around it sit the drawing tools, the indicator menu, and panels for watchlists and alerts.

Nothing on the screen is hidden knowledge. Every element is either market data, a calculation from that data, or a mark placed by the user. See Chart Interface Basics.

Layouts and timeframes

A layout is a saved arrangement of a chart: the symbol, timeframe, chart type, indicators and drawings. Saving a layout means the same view can be reopened later without rebuilding it.

Many platforms also offer multi-chart layouts, which show several charts side by side. A common use is the same asset on two timeframes, such as daily and one-hour, which supports the top-down reading described in Chapter 2.

Switching timeframe redraws the candles and leaves the drawings where they are. A drawing is anchored to a price and a time, so a zone marked on the daily chart is still there on the 15-minute chart. It can look surprisingly thick or far away, which is a useful reminder of how the two views relate. Platforms usually let the user choose on which timeframes each drawing is visible. See Timeframes on a Chart.

Drawing tools

A drawing is an annotation. The platform calculates nothing and simply draws what it is told. Whatever meaning a drawing has comes from the person who placed it.

Horizontal levels and zones

A horizontal line marks one price across the whole chart. A horizontal ray starts at a chosen candle and extends to the right. A rectangle marks a zone, which fits the idea from Chapter 5 that levels are areas. See Horizontal Levels & Zones.

Trend lines and channels

A trend line is a straight line through two points the user selects, usually swing lows in an uptrend or swing highs in a downtrend. Two points are enough to draw a line. A long-standing convention is to give it weight only after price has reacted to it a third time.

Small choices move the line. Anchoring on wicks gives a different line from anchoring on candle bodies. Switching the price axis between linear and logarithmic changes where a sloped line sits. A trend line is therefore an approximate guide. See Drawing Trend Lines.

Measurement

A measuring tool is dragged between two points on the chart. It reports the price change, the percentage change, the number of candles and the time between them. It is used to compare the size of moves, or to see how far price is from a level in percentage terms.

Many platforms also provide a drawing that shows a planned entry, stop and target as coloured boxes with the distances between them. It is a diagram. It sends nothing to any exchange. See Measuring Tools.

Indicators and their settings

Adding an indicator places it either on the price chart, as with moving averages and bands, or in a separate pane below, as with RSI and MACD. See Adding Indicators.

Each indicator has two kinds of settings:

  • Inputs change the calculation: the length, the price used (usually the close) and any multipliers.
  • Style changes only the appearance: colours, line thickness and which parts are shown.

Adjusting inputs until an indicator matches past price closely is called curve fitting. A line tuned to fit history says little about the future, because it was shaped by the very data it appears to explain. One more detail from Chapter 2 applies here: on the newest candle, an indicator’s value keeps changing until that candle closes. See Indicator Settings.

Alerts

An alert is a notification that fires when a condition becomes true. The condition might be price crossing a level, price touching a drawing, or an indicator reaching a value. Its purpose is to remove the need to watch a screen.

Platforms usually let the user choose whether an alert fires the moment the condition is touched or only once a candle has closed. That is the same wick-versus-close distinction that mattered for breakouts in Chapter 5. See Alerts.

Watchlists

A watchlist is a saved list of symbols, usually shown with the latest price and the change over the day. It makes it quick to move between charts and to keep groups of markets together.

One detail catches beginners. The same asset can appear under several symbols: one for each exchange, and separate ones for spot and for derivative contracts. Their prices and volumes differ slightly, and any drawing belongs to the symbol on which it was made. See Watchlists & Layouts.

What each tool does and does not do

Tool What it does What it does not do
Layout Saves a view so it can be reopened Keep the analysis up to date
Horizontal line or zone Marks a price or price area Make that area important
Trend line Joins chosen swing points Hold price on one side of it
Measuring tool Reports distance in price, percent and time Say how far price can travel
Indicator settings Change the calculation or its look Reveal a correct setting
Alert Notifies when a condition is met Place or close a trade
Watchlist Keeps markets within easy reach Rank or recommend them

A crowded chart is a common side effect of having many tools. Every extra line and pane hides some of the price underneath, so many chart readers keep only the marks they are still using.

Marking up a hypothetical chartHypothetical illustration

A learner wants to apply Chapters 4 and 5 to the daily chart of a made-up asset.

  1. Two rectangles go on the chart: one from 98 to 100, where price turned up twice, and one from 119 to 121, where it turned down three times.
  2. A trend line is drawn under two higher lows at 100 and 106.
  3. The measuring tool, dragged from 100 to 120, reports a distance of 20, or 20%.
  4. An alert is set for price crossing 119, so the chart does not need to be watched.
  5. The layout is saved.

Nothing in these steps says what price is going to do, and no order has been placed anywhere. The chart now records where the learner’s attention is, and that is the whole job of the tools.

What it does NOT mean

  • A drawing is not an order. Lines, boxes and position diagrams exist only on the chart.
  • A neat line is not a correct line. A level drawn to two decimal places is still a judgement about an area.
  • An alert firing is not a reason to act. It reports that a condition became true, exactly as it was set.
  • More tools on the chart do not mean better analysis. Clutter hides price, which is the thing being studied.
  • A feature described here is not permanent. Platforms rename, move and limit tools, so check the current help pages.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. What is the difference between an indicator’s inputs and its style settings?
  2. A zone drawn on the daily chart looks very thick on the 5-minute chart. Why?
  3. An alert set at a resistance level fires. What exactly has it told you?

Key takeaway

Chart tools make marks: layouts save a view, drawings record where you are looking, measuring tools report distances, settings shape indicators, and alerts and watchlists save attention. They make an analysis tidier and easier to return to, and they add no knowledge about what price does next.
