Where we are

In Chapter 2 you learned that a chart splits time into equal periods and that each period is summarised by four prices: open, high, low and close. This chapter shows how those four prices are drawn as a candlestick, and how to read one at a glance.

By the end you will be able to look at any candle and say what happened inside it. That is the whole skill. Pattern names come second.

One candle, four prices

Every candle has two parts:

  • The body is the thick part. It spans from the open to the close.
  • The wicks (also called shadows) are the thin lines above and below. The top of the upper wick is the highest price of the period; the bottom of the lower wick is the lowest.

Colour tells you the direction of the body:

  • Green (bullish): the close is above the open. Price ended the period higher than it started.
  • Red (bearish): the close is below the open. Price ended the period lower than it started.

Colour is a convention

Green-up and red-down is the most common scheme, and the one used across this site. Some platforms use white and black, or hollow and filled candles. In parts of East Asia the colours are reversed: red means up. Always check the legend of the chart you are reading.

Reading a candle like a short story

A candle is a tiny story about a fight between buyers and sellers.

Three questions decode almost any candle:

  1. How big is the body? A large body means one side clearly won the period. A small body means it was close.
  2. Where are the wicks? A long wick shows a price that was tried and rejected. A long lower wick means sellers pushed down and buyers pushed back. A long upper wick means the opposite.
  3. Where did it close within its range? A close near the high shows buyers finishing strong. A close near the low shows sellers finishing strong.

Four shapes worth knowing first

Doji: a draw

The open and close are almost equal, so there is hardly any body. Price moved, but nobody won. Read more on the Doji page.

Hammer: a rejected low

A small body at the top with a long lower wick, appearing after a decline. Sellers drove price down during the period, and buyers brought it most of the way back. More on the Hammer page.

Shooting star: a rejected high

The upside-down version, appearing after an advance: a small body at the bottom with a long upper wick. Buyers pushed up, and sellers took it back. More on the Shooting Star page.

Engulfing: a change of control

Two candles. The second body completely covers the first and points the other way. It shows that the side that lost the first period won the second one decisively. See Bullish Engulfing and Bearish Engulfing.

Context comes first

The same candle can mean very different things depending on where it sits.

A hammer in the middle of a sideways market is just a candle with a long wick. A hammer that forms after a clear decline, right on an area where price has bounced before, tells a more interesting story. You will learn to find those areas in Chapter 4 and Chapter 5.

Three things experienced chart readers check before giving a candle any weight:

  • The trend before it. What was price doing in the candles leading up to it?
  • The location. Is it near a level that mattered before?
  • The timeframe. A daily candle summarises a day of trading. A one-minute candle summarises almost nothing.

What it does NOT mean

  • A candle pattern is not a buy or sell signal. It is a description of what already happened.
  • A named pattern does not guarantee what comes next. Hammers fail. Engulfing patterns fail. All of them fail regularly.
  • More pattern names do not mean more skill. Reading body, wicks and close correctly matters far more than memorising a catalogue.
  • A candle is not finished until it closes. A shape that looks like a hammer mid-period can end as something else entirely.

Check your understanding

Try answering these in your head before moving on:

  1. A candle has a long upper wick and a small body near its low. What happened during the period?
  2. Why does a Doji on a daily chart carry more information than a Doji on a one-minute chart?
  3. A hammer appears in the middle of a sideways range. Why is that less meaningful than a hammer after a long decline?

Key takeaway

A candle shows four prices and one short story: who pushed, who pushed back, and who was ahead at the close. Read the story first, the pattern name second, and never treat either as a prediction.
