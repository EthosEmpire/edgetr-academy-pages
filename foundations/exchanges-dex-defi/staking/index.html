Staking

Staking means locking tokens to help secure a proof-of-stake blockchain in exchange for rewards, while accepting lock-up periods, slashing risk and changes in the token's price.

  • 5 min read

Why it matters

Staking is how proof-of-stake blockchains are secured, and it is the most widely advertised way of receiving rewards on crypto holdings. The same word is also attached to products that have little to do with securing a network.

Knowing what staking does, and what a staker gives up, separates the mechanism from the marketing.

How it works

A proof-of-stake network chooses participants called validators to propose and check new blocks. To be eligible, a validator must lock up the network’s token as a stake. The stake is a security deposit: a validator that follows the rules is rewarded, and one that breaks them can lose part of it.

Staking is the act of committing tokens to that deposit. Rewards are paid in the same token and come from two sources: new tokens issued by the network, and fees paid by users.

In plain words

It works like a deposit paid by a security guard. The guard puts money down to get the job, is paid for each shift worked properly, and loses part of the deposit if caught cheating. The deposit cannot be collected on the day the guard decides to quit.

Ways of staking

Running a validator requires technical skill and often a minimum amount of tokens. Most holders therefore stake through someone else. In delegation, tokens are assigned to a validator who shares the rewards after a commission. An exchange may offer staking as a service, which adds the exchange as a counterparty.

In liquid staking, a protocol stakes deposited tokens and hands back a liquid staking token, a receipt that can be traded or used elsewhere in DeFi while the original tokens stay locked.

What a staker accepts

Risk What it means
Lock-up and unbonding Staked tokens cannot be moved at once. Leaving takes from hours to several weeks, depending on the network and on how many others are leaving
Slashing Part of the stake is destroyed if the validator provably breaks the rules. Smaller penalties can apply for being offline. Delegators usually share the loss
Price Rewards are paid in the token. Its market price can fall by more than the reward
Validator or custodian The party doing the staking can perform badly, raise its commission or fail
Liquid staking Adds smart contract risk, and the receipt token can trade below the value of the tokens behind it (a de-peg)

Reward rates are not guaranteed

A quoted staking rate is variable. It changes with the number of tokens staked across the network, with network activity and with the validator’s performance and commission. It is not guaranteed, it is paid in a token whose price can fall, and it comes with every risk in the table above. A rate of 5% in tokens can go together with a loss in ordinary money, and staked tokens cannot be sold quickly when the price is falling.

Going deeper

Rewards and dilution. Where rewards come mostly from newly issued tokens, the supply grows. A staker’s share of the network rises only relative to holders who do not stake. If everyone staked, everyone would hold more tokens and the same share. See token unlocks and emissions.

The rate moves with participation. On many networks the reward rate per token falls as more tokens are staked, because the rewards are spread across a larger total.

Liquid staking tokens in DeFi. These receipt tokens are often used as collateral or placed in liquidity pools. The underlying stake then supports several positions at once, and a fall in the receipt token’s price can trigger liquidations elsewhere. Smart contract risk applies at every layer.

The word is used loosely. Some products labeled “staking” lock a token in an application or lend it out, and secure no network at all. Their rewards come from somewhere else, and so do their risks.

Rules and tax. How staking services and rewards are treated differs between jurisdictions.

A hypothetical year of stakingHypothetical illustration

Made-up numbers. Someone stakes 100 tokens priced at 10, worth 1,000. The reward rate is 5% a year and, for simplicity, stays constant.

  • After one year the stake is 100 x 1.05 = 105 tokens.
  • At an unchanged price of 10, that is worth 1,050.
  • At a price of 8, it is worth 105 x 8 = 840, a fall of 16% in ordinary money.
  • The price at which the staker is level: 1,000 / 105 = about 9.52. A price fall of roughly 4.8% cancels the whole year’s reward.

Suppose the network has a 21-day unbonding period. A staker who decides to leave on the day the price starts falling can sell only three weeks later, at whatever the price is then.

What it does NOT mean

  • Staking is not a savings account. There is no fixed rate, no deposit protection and no fixed value.
  • A reward rate is not a return in ordinary money. It counts tokens, not what they are worth.
  • Staked does not mean safe from loss. Slashing, validator failure and price falls all apply.
  • A liquid staking token is not the same as the token behind it. It is a claim issued by a protocol, priced by the market.
  • Not everything called staking secures a blockchain. The label is applied to many unrelated products.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Comparing reward rates across tokens. A higher rate often reflects faster issuance, which dilutes every holder.
  2. Ignoring the exit time. The unbonding period matters most exactly when leaving is urgent.
  3. Assuming delegation carries no risk. A delegator typically shares in the validator’s penalties.
  4. Treating a receipt token as redeemable at once. Redemption may queue, and the market price can differ from the underlying value.

Risks and limits

  • Token price changes usually outweigh the reward rate, in either direction.
  • Staked tokens are illiquid for the length of the unbonding period.
  • Slashing and downtime penalties reduce the stake itself, not only the rewards.
  • Staking through a company or protocol adds counterparty or contract risk.
  • Reward rates, commissions and network rules can change.

Key takeaway

Staking locks tokens as a security deposit for a proof-of-stake network and pays variable rewards in that token. The staker accepts waiting periods, possible slashing and full exposure to the token’s price, and liquid staking adds contract and de-peg risk on top.
