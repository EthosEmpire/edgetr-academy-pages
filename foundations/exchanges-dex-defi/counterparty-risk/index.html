Counterparty Risk

Counterparty risk is the risk that the other party to an arrangement, such as an exchange, a lender or a token issuer, cannot or will not do what it promised.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Whenever someone else holds your assets or owes you something, the outcome depends on them. Price charts do not show this risk, and it tends to be ignored until the day it matters.

In crypto it has mattered repeatedly. Exchange and lender failures have left customers unable to withdraw, with balances that were still displayed on screen but could not be collected.

How it works

A counterparty is the other side of an agreement. Counterparty risk is the chance that it fails to keep its side, because it cannot (insolvency, theft, a frozen bank account) or will not (fraud, a dispute, a legal order).

The key idea is the difference between owning an asset and owning a claim on someone who is supposed to have it. Coins in a wallet whose keys you hold are an asset. A balance at a custodial service is a claim.

In plain words

Lending a friend your bicycle is not risky because bicycles are risky. It is risky because the friend might lose it, sell it or move away. The bicycle’s market value tells you nothing about whether it comes back.

Who the counterparty is

Arrangement Counterparty What it has promised
Balance on a centralized exchange The exchange To return the balance on request
Coins lent to a company for a rate The company and its borrowers To repay, with the rate
Fiat-backed stablecoin The issuer and its banks To redeem tokens at face value
Derivative position The exchange and, indirectly, other traders To pay what the contract owes
Wrapped or bridged token The custodian or bridge To hold the original asset one for one
DeFi protocol No company in the simplest case Nothing: the code runs as written

The last row is not an absence of risk. It exchanges counterparty risk for smart contract risk, and administrators or token issuers often bring a counterparty back in.

A rate offered for lending or depositing assets is payment for carrying this risk. Such rates are variable, are not guaranteed, are often paid in a token whose price can fall, and come with the risks of whatever the counterparty does with the assets.

How failures unfold

A custodian is sound when its assets match what it owes, in the same assets, and are available on demand. A shortfall arises when customer assets have been lost, stolen, lent out to borrowers who cannot repay, or used for the company’s own purposes. Rehypothecation, the reuse of customer assets as collateral for the firm’s own borrowing, is one route to it.

Going deeper

What traditional finance does about it. Established markets limit counterparty risk with rules built up over decades: customer assets kept apart from company assets, capital requirements, independent audits, clearing houses standing between traders, and deposit or investor protection schemes. Exchanges and brokers covers the structure. In crypto these protections may be absent, partial or untested, and they vary by jurisdiction.

Proof of reserves. Some exchanges publish evidence of the assets they hold. This is more than nothing and less than an audit. It is a snapshot of one moment, and assets say little without a reliable account of liabilities, including debts recorded nowhere on a blockchain.

Contagion. Firms lend to each other. One failure can cause losses at its lenders, which then freeze withdrawals in turn.

Exposure adds up. The risk grows with the amount held with one counterparty and the time it stays there, a form of concentration risk.

Insolvency is slow. Customers of a failed company are often treated as unsecured creditors. Claims may be valued in ordinary money at the date of the filing, not returned as coins, and legal costs are paid before customers are.

A hypothetical exchange failureHypothetical illustration

Made-up numbers. An exchange owes its customers assets worth 100 million. It holds 70 million. One customer’s account shows a balance worth 1,000.

  • Coverage: 70 / 100 = 70%. The shortfall is 30 million.
  • For as long as withdrawal requests are small, every one of them is paid in full, and nothing looks wrong.
  • Rumors start. Customers request 80 million in a week. The exchange holds only 70 million, so it pauses all withdrawals.

In the insolvency that follows, suppose the process itself costs 5 million. That leaves 70 - 5 = 65 million for claims of 100 million, or 65%. The customer with 1,000 on screen would receive about 650, and perhaps only after several years.

The price of the coins played no part in this loss. It came entirely from who was holding them.

What it does NOT mean

  • A displayed balance is not proof of assets. It is a record of what the company says it owes.
  • A large or famous counterparty is not a safe one. Size has not prevented failure.
  • Counterparty risk is not market risk. It can cause a loss while prices are rising.
  • Proof of reserves is not proof of solvency. Solvency depends on liabilities as well as assets.
  • Avoiding custodians does not avoid risk. Self-custody replaces it with responsibility for keys, and DeFi replaces it with reliance on code.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Judging safety by how the service has behaved so far. A shortfall is invisible until withdrawals exceed liquid assets.
  2. Reading a higher rate as a better offer. A higher rate usually signals that the counterparty is doing something riskier with the assets.
  3. Assuming protection schemes apply. Deposit insurance and investor compensation often do not cover crypto held with a platform.
  4. Overlooking indirect counterparties. A stablecoin, a wrapped token or a bridge each add an issuer or custodian.
  5. Expecting a quick resolution. Insolvency proceedings commonly run for years.

Risks and limits

  • Outsiders can rarely verify a private company’s finances, so this risk is hard to measure in advance.
  • Warning signs often appear only shortly before a freeze.
  • Legal rights depend on the terms of service, the legal entity and the country.
  • Recovery, where it happens, is often partial or slow, and is uncertain.

Key takeaway

Counterparty risk is the risk that whoever holds your assets or owes you something does not deliver. A balance with a company is a claim, not an asset, and it is worth only what that company can and will pay out.
