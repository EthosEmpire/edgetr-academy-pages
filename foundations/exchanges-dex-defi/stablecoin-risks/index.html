Stablecoin Risks

Stablecoins aim to hold a fixed value and can fail to: reserves can be weaker than claimed, redemption can be limited, market prices can de-peg and algorithmic designs can collapse.

  • Intermediate
  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Stablecoins are treated as the cash of crypto markets. They sit on the other side of most trading pairs, serve as collateral in lending and make up much of what is held in liquidity pools.

That role invites a dangerous assumption: that one token is always worth one unit of currency. A stablecoin is a design that tries to hold a price. This page covers the ways it can fail to.

How a peg is held

A peg is the target price, usually one U.S. dollar. Two different prices are involved:

  • the redemption value: what the issuer or the protocol will give for one token;
  • the market price: what one token fetches on an exchange.

For a fiat-backed stablecoin, the two are tied together by redemption. If the market price falls to 0.99, a firm that can redeem at 1.00 buys tokens and redeems them, and that buying lifts the price. This is arbitrage, and it works only while redemption is open, quick and trusted.

In plain words

A stablecoin is like a cloakroom ticket that people have started using as money. It passes at full value for as long as everyone believes the coat is in the cloakroom and can be collected. Doubt about either is enough to change what the ticket is worth.

What can go wrong

Risk What it means
Reserve quality Reserves may include assets that are risky, hard to sell or worth less than stated
Redemption limits Direct redemption may be open only to approved firms, carry minimum sizes and fees, or be paused
De-peg The market price moves away from the target, briefly or for good
Banking and custody Reserves sit with banks and custodians that can themselves fail or freeze accounts
Freezing Some issuers can freeze tokens at specific addresses
Collateral crash For crypto-backed designs, collateral can fall faster than it can be liquidated
Algorithmic collapse A design with little collateral can lose its peg entirely

Reserves

Issuers commonly publish attestations: an accountant’s report on the reserves at a given date. An attestation is narrower than a full audit. It is a snapshot, and it may say little about how quickly the assets could be sold in a crisis. The issuer is a counterparty in the full sense described in counterparty risk.

De-pegs

Small deviations of a fraction of a percent are routine. Larger ones tend to occur when holders doubt the reserves, when redemption is closed (for example outside banking hours) or when sellers overwhelm the available liquidity. A de-peg may reverse once redemption resumes. It may also be the market correctly pricing a real shortfall.

Algorithmic designs

An algorithmic stablecoin tries to hold its price through supply rules and a second, linked token, instead of through reserves. The linked token has value only while people expect the system to work. When that expectation breaks, redemptions create more of the linked token, its price falls, and the support beneath the stablecoin shrinks with it. In 2022, one of the largest algorithmic stablecoins lost its peg and fell to a small fraction of its target within days.

Going deeper

Knock-on effects. Because stablecoins are built into DeFi, a de-peg spreads. Loans backed by the stablecoin can be liquidated, and a liquidity pool holding it fills up with the failing token as traders remove the sound one. See DeFi lending and borrowing.

Contracts and bridges. A stablecoin is also a smart contract, and versions of it on other chains often depend on a bridge. Smart contract risk applies.

Rates on stablecoins. A rate offered on a stablecoin deposit is variable, is not guaranteed and is often paid partly in another token whose price can fall. It comes with the risks of the mechanism producing it, and a token that aims at a stable price does not make the deposit stable.

Regulation. Rules on reserves, redemption rights and disclosure differ between jurisdictions and are changing.

A hypothetical weekend de-pegHypothetical illustration

Made-up numbers. A holder has 10,000 tokens of a fiat-backed stablecoin. On a Saturday, reports question the issuer’s reserves. Banks are closed, so redemptions are not being processed, and the market price falls to 0.94.

  • Selling at once would bring 10,000 x 0.94 = 9,400. That fixes a loss of 600, or 6%.
  • Direct redemption has a minimum of 100,000 tokens, so this holder could not redeem even on a weekday.

What waiting leads to depends on a fact the holder cannot check:

  • If reserves are complete, redemptions resume, arbitrage buyers return and the price moves back toward 1.00.
  • If reserves are worth 0.90 per token, as in the figure above, the backing behind 10,000 tokens is about 9,000, less than the weekend price.

Both stories look the same on the Saturday.

What it does NOT mean

  • “Stable” is an aim, not a property. The price holds only while the mechanism behind it holds.
  • One token is not one dollar in a bank. It is a claim on an issuer or a position in a protocol, without deposit insurance.
  • Fully backed does not mean redeemable by everyone. Many holders can exit only by selling on the market.
  • A de-peg is not always temporary. Some recover within days and some never do.
  • A long record at 1.00 does not prove sound reserves. A peg can hold right up to the point where it is tested.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Treating all stablecoins as the same. Backing, redemption terms and issuers differ widely.
  2. Reading the market price as the reserve value. Price reflects confidence and liquidity. Reserves are a separate question.
  3. Assuming an attestation is an audit. It covers one date and a limited scope.
  4. Ignoring which chain the token is on. A bridged version adds the bridge’s risk to the issuer’s.
  5. Regarding a high stablecoin rate as low risk. The rate is payment for a risk, whatever value the token aims to hold.

Risks and limits

  • Holders usually cannot verify reserves themselves.
  • De-pegs develop quickly, often outside banking hours, when redemption is slowest.
  • A failure of a widely used stablecoin affects pools, loans and exchanges that depend on it.
  • Issuers can freeze tokens or change terms, and legal rights vary by jurisdiction.

Key takeaway

A stablecoin holds its price only as long as its reserves, its redemption process or its mechanism hold. Reserve quality, limits on redemption, de-pegs and, for algorithmic designs, outright collapse are the ways that promise can break.
