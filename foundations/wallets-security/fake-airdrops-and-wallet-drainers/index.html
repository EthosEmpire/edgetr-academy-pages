Fake Airdrops & Wallet Drainers

A fake airdrop lures people to a website with the promise of tokens to claim. There a wallet drainer presents signature requests that hand the attacker control of the assets in the wallet.

  • 5 min read

Why it matters

A genuine airdrop is a distribution of tokens to wallets, usually to reward early users of a project. Real ones have happened often enough that the idea of unexpected tokens waiting to be claimed sounds plausible.

Scammers use that expectation. The scam matters for a particular reason: it defeats the one rule most people have learned. The victim never shares a seed phrase. They press “claim” and approve a prompt.

How the attack works

A wallet drainer is malicious code built into a website. Once a wallet connects, the site can see the wallet’s public address and therefore everything it holds, since balances are public. It then presents one or more signature requests designed to move those holdings to the attacker.

What the victim sees is ordinary: a page in the style of a real project, a button, a wallet prompt. The website’s wording says “claim”, “mint” or “verify”. The wallet prompt describes something else, and the wallet prompt is the part that counts.

In plain words

A stranger at the door says you have won a prize and holds out a form to sign for delivery. The top line reads “Prize receipt”. The small print further down authorises the collection of everything in the house. Nobody asked for your keys. The signature did the work.

The lure

The bait reaches people in a few standard ways.

  • Surprise tokens. A token or NFT that the owner never bought appears in the wallet. Its name or description contains a web address, or it appears to be worth a large sum. Selling it seems to require visiting that site.
  • Posts and replies. Messages on social platforms announce that a claim is open, often from hijacked or look-alike accounts and often under a real project’s announcement.
  • Adverts and direct messages. A fake claim page is promoted above the real site, or a “team member” writes to say the victim is eligible.

Receiving an unknown token does no harm in itself. Anyone can send tokens to any address. The risk begins with interaction: visiting the linked site, connecting and signing.

What the prompt really asks

What the page says What the wallet prompt may say Effect if signed
“Claim your tokens” Approve a spender for a token you hold A token approval the attacker can use at once
“Free mint” Set approval for all, for an NFT collection Every item of that collection can be moved
“Verify your wallet. No fee” Sign a message with spender, amount and deadline A permit: an approval granted by signature
“Confirm eligibility” Send coins to an address or contract A direct transfer of the amount shown

Each of these is a normal kind of request that honest applications also use, which is why the wallet does not block them. The background is in signing transactions.

Security

A drainer does not need a seed phrase, and some fake airdrop sites ask for one as well. No legitimate service, support agent or website ever asks for a seed phrase or private key. The only place a seed phrase is ever entered is the owner’s own wallet app or device, when the owner chooses to restore it. edgeTR Academy never asks for one and has no wallet connection, login or payment anywhere on the site.

Going deeper

What can and cannot be undone. Assets that have been transferred are gone. Approvals that have not yet been used can be revoked, and assets the attacker has not reached can be moved to a wallet that never signed anything. Speed matters, because drainers are automated.

Hardware wallets. A hardware wallet protects keys from malware. A drainer never touches the keys. It asks the owner to sign, and the device signs what its owner confirms.

Separate wallets. A practice some people describe is keeping a separate wallet that holds little for use with unfamiliar sites, so that a mistaken signature has little to act on. It limits the damage. It does not make the signature safe.

Checking the source. Whether a claim is real can be checked from the project’s official website and channels, reached independently and not through the message that announced it. Many projects never run an airdrop at all.

A hypothetical surprise tokenHypothetical illustration

Mei opens her wallet and finds 5,000 units of a token she has never heard of. The token’s name includes a web address. A price site appears to value the holding at a large amount.

She visits the address. The page congratulates her, shows a countdown of nine minutes and offers a “Claim and swap” button. She connects her wallet and presses it. The wallet opens a prompt titled “Approve”, naming a stablecoin she holds, with an amount shown as unlimited. Mei is watching the countdown. She confirms.

Within a minute her stablecoin balance is zero. The 5,000 surprise tokens are still in the wallet and cannot be sold anywhere.

The prompt named a token she already owned and an unlimited amount, on a page that claimed to be giving her something. A claim that needs permission over existing holdings is not a claim.

What it does NOT mean

  • Unexpected tokens in a wallet are not a gift. Anyone can send tokens to any address, and sending them is a cheap way to advertise a trap.
  • A displayed value is not a real value. A token with no genuine market can show any price.
  • Keeping the seed phrase secret is not full protection. A signed approval or permit is enough to move assets.
  • “Claim” on a button does not describe the request. Only the wallet prompt does.

Common mistakes

  1. Trying to sell a surprise token. The attempt leads to the scam site named in the token.
  2. Reading the page and skipping the prompt. The page is written by the attacker. The prompt is written by the wallet.
  3. Racing a countdown. The timer is there to prevent reading.
  4. Assuming a free signature is safe. Permits and orders cost nothing to sign.

Risks and limitations

  • Drainer sites copy real projects closely and are often live for only hours, so wallet warnings and blocklists miss new ones.
  • Prompts for unfamiliar contracts are often shown as raw data that few people can read.
  • Revoking and moving assets costs network fees and may come too late.
  • These scams change quickly. This page describes the pattern, and the details will differ.

Key takeaway

A fake airdrop offers tokens and asks for a signature. A drainer needs only that signature, not a seed phrase, so the wallet prompt has to be read as what it is: a request for permission over assets already held.
