Pump and Dump

A pump and dump is a scheme in which insiders buy an asset cheaply, promote it to drive the price up, and sell to the latecomers the promotion attracted, after which the price collapses.

Why it matters

A pump and dump is one of the oldest forms of market manipulation, long known from thinly traded shares and now common with small crypto tokens. It needs no hacking and no stolen keys. People buy willingly, at prices arranged by someone who is waiting to sell to them.

Recognising the pattern matters because, from the inside, it does not feel like a scam. It feels like discovering an opportunity early.

What it looks like

A steep rise on a chart can come from many causes. What defines a pump and dump is not the shape. It is the arrangement behind it: a group that bought first, created the excitement deliberately and planned to sell into it.

How it works

Accumulate. The organisers buy a large amount of an asset at low prices. Targets are usually small tokens with thin liquidity, where a modest amount of buying moves the price a long way.

Promote. The asset is pushed through social media, chat groups and paid mentions by popular accounts. The message is some version of urgency: something big is coming and time is short. Claims of certain or very large gains are typical, and they are a mark of fraud.

Pump. New buyers arrive. Their buying lifts the price, the rising price is shown as proof, and more buyers follow. This is herd behavior and FOMO put to deliberate use.

Dump. The organisers sell their holdings to the incoming buyers. Because they bought much lower, they profit even while the price is falling.

Collapse. With the promotion over and the largest holders gone, no buyers remain at the higher prices. The price falls back, often below where it started.

In plain words

Someone buys every ticket to a small local event for very little, then spreads the word that it is the event of the year and almost sold out. A crowd forms and pays ten times the price at the door. The seller leaves with the takings. Inside there is nothing much to see.

Who is on each side

A pump and dump moves money from one group to another. It creates nothing. What the insiders gain is, roughly and apart from fees, what the latecomers lose.

Pump groups. Some schemes are run openly in chat groups that announce a token at a set time and tell members to buy together. Members believe they are the insiders. The organisers bought earlier. A CFTC advisory notes that the people running such a scheme are the ones who get out first and make the most, which leaves the other members to take the loss.

Paid promotion. A person praising a token may hold it, may have been paid in it, or may have been paid to post. Disclosure is often missing.

Going deeper

Why small tokens. A low market capitalization and a shallow market mean that little money is needed to move the price. Many meme coins fit this description, though being small or popular does not make a token a scheme.

Volume can mislead. A surge in trading volume during the pump looks like broad interest. Part of it can be the organisers trading with themselves to simulate activity.

The law. In regulated securities and derivatives markets, pump and dump schemes are illegal market manipulation. How far those rules reach individual crypto tokens depends on the jurisdiction and the asset, and enforcement is uneven.

Related schemes. In a rug pull the developers remove the liquidity itself. A pump and dump can involve a token whose developers had no part in it.

A hypothetical pump with numbersHypothetical illustration

A group quietly buys 1,000,000 units of a little-traded token at an average price of 0.10, spending 100,000.

They begin a coordinated promotion. Over two days the price climbs to 0.50. The group sells its 1,000,000 units on the way up and at the top, at an average of 0.40, and receives 400,000. Their gain is 400,000 minus 100,000, which is 300,000.

Mia sees the token trending and buys 1,000 worth at 0.45, receiving about 2,222 units. With the promotion over, the price falls to 0.08. Her holding is now worth about 178, a loss of roughly 82%.

The 300,000 the group gained came from Mia and the other buyers who arrived after the promotion began.

What it does NOT mean

  • A fast rise is not proof of a pump and dump. Prices also move sharply for ordinary reasons.
  • Joining early does not make someone an insider. The real insiders bought before any announcement was made.
  • Widespread excitement is not independent evidence. One organiser can produce many voices.
  • A pump is not a trading opportunity with known timing. Only the organisers know when the selling starts.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating the rising price as the reason to buy. In a pump, the rise is the advertisement.
  2. Believing a group’s promise of early access. Being told when to buy is being told when to supply the exit.
  3. Counting on getting out first. Thin markets fall faster than orders can be filled, as described in slippage.
  4. Trusting unknown promoters. Paid and self-interested promotion is rarely labelled.

Risks and limitations

  • A pump and dump can only be confirmed afterwards. During the rise, it is indistinguishable from other fast moves.
  • The signs described here are common features, not a test. Their absence proves nothing.
  • Losses are seldom recovered, and organisers are often anonymous.
  • Taking part knowingly, as a promoter or organiser, may be unlawful in many jurisdictions.

Key takeaway

A pump and dump is coordinated buying and promotion followed by insiders selling to the people the promotion attracted. The steep rise is the bait, and the latecomers’ money is what the insiders take out.
