Security Checklist

A practical checklist of security habits for wallets and exchange accounts: protecting the seed phrase, checking addresses, reading what is signed and recognising the common scams.

Why it matters

Crypto security is mostly habit, not technology. Nearly every loss described in this shelf came from an ordinary moment: a photo of a backup, a copied address, a hurried click on a prompt, a helpful stranger.

This page gathers those lessons into one list. It is safety guidance, written in direct “do” and “do not” form. It says nothing about what to buy, hold or sell. Each section links to the page that explains the reasons.

How to use it

A checklist is not for reading once. Pilots and surgeons use them because experience does not prevent slips under pressure. Scams are built to create pressure. The three questions above are the short version for moments when there is no time for the long one.

Secrets: seed phrases and keys

  • Do write the seed phrase on paper or metal and keep it offline, in more than one place.
  • Do check that the written words are correct and legible, using the wallet’s own on-device recovery check or backup screen, or, for a hardware wallet, by restoring onto a genuine spare device that you set up yourself and reset afterwards. Never type the words into a website or a look-alike app.
  • Do not photograph it, screenshot it, email it or keep it in a notes app or cloud document.
  • Do not type it into any website, form or chat, for any reason.
  • Do not share it or a private key with anyone.

Security

No legitimate service, support agent or website ever asks for a seed phrase or private key. The only place a seed phrase is ever entered is the owner’s own wallet app or device, when the owner chooses to restore it. edgeTR Academy never asks for one and has no wallet connection, login or payment anywhere on the site.

Devices and wallets

  • Do know what kind of wallet is in use, and whether it is custodial or non-custodial.
  • Do install wallet software only from its maker’s official source, and keep it and the device up to date.
  • Do understand what a cold wallet protects against, and what it does not.
  • Do read the screen of a hardware wallet before pressing its button.
  • Do not enter a hardware wallet’s seed phrase into a phone or computer.
  • Do buy a hardware wallet only from its maker or an authorised seller, and avoid second-hand units.
  • Do not use a device that arrived with a seed phrase already supplied.

Actions: sending

  • Do compare the whole address, not only its first and last characters.
  • Do confirm the network and any memo or tag with the recipient.
  • Do send a small test amount first on any new route.
  • Do not copy an address from transaction history. See addresses and sending safely.

Actions: signing and approving

  • Do read the wallet prompt, not the website’s button. See signing transactions.
  • Do reject anything unreadable or unexpected. Rejecting costs nothing.
  • Do review old token approvals from time to time and revoke those no longer needed.
  • Do not treat a free signature as a harmless one.
  • Do not interact with tokens that appeared unasked. See fake airdrops and wallet drainers.

Accounts

  • Do use a long password that is unique to each account.
  • Do use an authenticator app or a hardware security key. See two-factor authentication.
  • Do protect the email account as carefully as the exchange account.
  • Do switch on withdrawal allow-lists and an anti-phishing code where they are offered. See exchange account security.
  • Do not read a one-time code to anyone, or type it anywhere but the service’s own sign-in page.

People and promises

  • Do reach every service through a bookmark or the official app, not through a link, advert or message. See phishing.
  • Do contact support only through the official site.
  • Do not accept help that arrives by direct message. See fake support and impersonation.
  • Do not send coins to a “giveaway” or to a “safe” address supplied by a caller.
  • Do not believe promises of certain returns. They are a mark of fraud, as described in pump and dump and rug pulls and honeypots.
  • Do not act on a deadline that someone else has set.

If something has gone wrong

What happened First steps
A seed phrase was typed or shown somewhere it should not be From a clean device, create a new wallet with a new phrase and move the remaining assets to it. The old wallet stays compromised for good
A suspicious request was signed Revoke open approvals and move what remains to a wallet that never signed it
An exchange login was entered on a fake page Sign in through a bookmark, change the password, end other sessions and contact the exchange through its official channel
The phone lost service for no reason Contact the mobile carrier at once and check the email account

Reports can be made to the platform involved and to the consumer-protection authority in the relevant country. People who offer to recover lost funds for an upfront fee are usually running a second scam.

A hypothetical ten-second pauseHypothetical illustration

Leo receives a message: his wallet “requires verification within 15 minutes” or it will be suspended. The link opens a page that looks right.

He stops and asks the three questions. Who started this? The message came to him. What is being asked? The page wants his 12 words. Can it be undone? No.

A non-custodial wallet cannot be suspended by anyone, and no real service needs the words. Leo closes the page. Nothing happens after 15 minutes, because nothing was ever going to.

What it does NOT mean

  • A checklist is not a guarantee. It lowers the odds of the common losses. New methods appear all the time.
  • This is not financial advice. Nothing here concerns what to buy, sell or hold.
  • Good habits do not remove company risk. Funds held with an exchange depend on that exchange.
  • Security is not a one-off task. Approvals accumulate, software ages and backups get lost.
  • Following the list does not make urgency safe. The most useful habit is slowing down.

Common mistakes

  1. Knowing the rules and skipping them when rushed. Pressure is the scammer’s main tool.
  2. Protecting one layer only. A hardware wallet with a photographed seed phrase is a hot wallet.
  3. Assuming experience is protection. Experienced users are targeted too.
  4. Keeping quiet after a loss. Fast action can limit damage, and reports help others.

Risks and limitations

  • No list covers every situation, and the details of scams change faster than any page.
  • Some items trade convenience for safety, and each person’s circumstances differ.
  • Features named here are not offered by every wallet or exchange.
  • Blockchain transfers are final. Prevention is nearly always the only remedy.

Key takeaway

Keep the seed phrase offline and private, check every address and network, read every prompt before signing, and treat urgency from anyone as a warning sign. No legitimate party ever needs a seed phrase or private key.

Sources and further reading

  • Ledger Academy: Buying a Ledger securely (opens in a new tab). Buy from the maker or an authorised reseller; devices from unauthorised sellers can be tampered with
  • Worried about crypto exchange losses? Don’t pay money for “help” recovering money (opens in a new tab), Federal Trade Commission (2022). Offers to recover money lost to a scam for a fee are a further scam

Related topics

Wallets & Security What Is a Crypto Wallet? A crypto wallet is software or a device that stores and uses the private keys controlling funds on a blockchain. The coins themselves are records on the blockchain, not files in the wallet. Beginner Wallets & Security Seed Phrases A seed phrase is a list of words, commonly 12 or 24, from which a wallet can regenerate all of its private keys. It is the master secret and the only backup of the wallet. Beginner Wallets & Security Signing Transactions Signing uses a private key to authorise a transaction or message. The signature is the consent, so a malicious request that gets signed can hand over assets without the seed phrase ever being revealed. Intermediate Wallets & Security Phishing Phishing is an attack that imitates a trusted service to trick a person into giving up a secret or signing a harmful request. In crypto the target is usually a seed phrase, a login or a signature. Beginner Wallets & Security Two-Factor Authentication Two-factor authentication (2FA) adds a second proof of identity to a password when signing in. Authenticator apps and hardware security keys are generally stronger second factors than SMS codes. Beginner Wallets & Security Exchange Account Security Exchange account security is the set of protections around a custodial account: a unique password, a strong second factor, a secured email address and, where offered, withdrawal allow-lists and anti-phishing codes.
