Addresses & Sending Safely

An address is a public identifier, derived from a public key, that funds are sent to. Blockchain transfers cannot be recalled, so the address and the network both have to be right before sending.

Why it matters

Sending crypto has no “undo”. A card payment can be disputed, and a bank transfer to the wrong account can sometimes be recalled. A confirmed blockchain transaction is final, and no operator exists who can pull it back.

That turns two small details into the whole risk of a transfer: the address it goes to and the network it travels on. Many losses while sending are not thefts by hacking. They are valid transactions to the wrong place.

What an address is

An address is a string of letters and numbers that identifies where funds are received. It is derived from a public key, which is derived from a private key. The derivation only works in one direction.

An address is safe to share, in the sense that nobody can spend from it without the key. It is not private. Anyone who knows an address can look up its balance and its full history on a public block explorer.

Formats differ between networks. Bitcoin addresses commonly start with 1, 3 or bc1. Ethereum addresses are 0x followed by 40 hexadecimal characters, and the same format is used on many Ethereum-compatible networks.

How a transfer happens

The sender’s wallet builds a transaction naming the recipient address and the amount, signs it and broadcasts it. A network fee is paid in the network’s own coin. Once the transaction is included in a block and confirmed, the record is permanent.

In plain words

Sending crypto is closer to posting cash in an envelope than to paying by card. If the envelope is addressed to the wrong house, the post office still delivers it. Nobody at the post office can open that door and bring it back.

How transfers go wrong

Wrong address. Many address formats contain a checksum, so a random typing error usually produces an invalid address that the wallet refuses. Ethereum’s checksum is only visible in mixed-case addresses. The danger is a different address that is perfectly valid. One cause is clipboard malware, which swaps a copied address for the attacker’s at the moment of pasting.

Wrong network. The same token can exist on several networks, including Layer 2 networks. If a token is sent on a network that the recipient’s exchange or wallet does not support, the funds arrive at an address nobody is watching. Recovery then depends on whoever controls the key for that address. An exchange may charge for it or decline, and in some cases recovery is not possible at all.

Missing memo or tag. Some networks and exchanges use one shared deposit address for many customers and tell them apart with a memo or destination tag. A deposit without it reaches the exchange and is not credited to anyone automatically.

Address poisoning

Address poisoning is a scam aimed at the habit of copying addresses from transaction history. The attacker creates an address whose first and last characters match one the victim uses, then sends a tiny or zero-value transfer so that the look-alike appears in the victim’s history.

Nothing is stolen at that moment. The loss happens later, if the victim copies the look-alike while meaning to copy the real one. Wallets usually shorten addresses to the first and last few characters, which is exactly the part the attacker has matched.

Going deeper

What careful senders check. Common habits are comparing the whole address and not only its ends, taking the address from the recipient’s wallet or a saved address book and not from history, confirming the network with the recipient, and sending a small test amount before a large one. A test costs an extra fee and catches most address and network errors.

Hardware wallet screens. A hardware wallet shows the address that will really be signed, which defeats malware that alters what the computer displays.

Moving between networks. Tokens do not move from one network to another by sending them to the same address elsewhere. That is the job of bridges, which carry their own risks.

A hypothetical poisoned historyHypothetical illustration

Dana regularly sends tokens from her wallet to her own exchange deposit address, which her wallet displays as 0x4a7c...9f3c.

One day a new line appears in her history: a transfer of zero tokens involving an address shown as 0x4a7c...9f3c. Written out in full, the two addresses differ in almost every character in the middle. Dana does not notice it.

A week later she wants to send 1,000 units. She opens her history, copies the most recent matching entry, pastes it and confirms. The transaction succeeds and is confirmed within minutes. The exchange never receives it, because the funds went to the look-alike. No support team at the wallet, the exchange or the network can reverse the transfer.

Comparing the full address, or using a saved address book entry, would have shown the difference.

What it does NOT mean

  • A successful transaction does not mean a correct one. The network confirms valid transfers. It does not know what the sender intended.
  • Matching first and last characters do not prove an address is right. Look-alike addresses are built to match exactly those characters.
  • An address being “safe to share” does not make it private. Its balance and history are public.
  • The same address on another network is not the same destination. Whether the funds are reachable depends on who holds the key and which networks they use.

Common mistakes

  1. Copying addresses from transaction history. History can contain entries that other people put there.
  2. Checking only the ends of the address. The middle is where look-alikes differ.
  3. Choosing a network by its fee. The cheapest network is the wrong one if the recipient does not support it.
  4. Leaving out the memo. On networks that use one, it is part of the address.

Risks and limitations

  • No amount of checking removes the risk fully. Malware, fake wallet apps and deceptive websites can mislead a careful sender.
  • Rules for memos, supported networks and mistaken-deposit recovery differ between exchanges and change over time.
  • Funds sent to an address for which nobody holds the key are permanently inaccessible.

Key takeaway

An address is public and safe to share, and a transfer to it is final. Before sending, the full address, the network and any memo have to be right, because a valid transaction to the wrong place cannot be recalled.
