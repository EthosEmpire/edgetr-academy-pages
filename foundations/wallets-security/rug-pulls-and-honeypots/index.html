Rug Pulls & Honeypots

A rug pull is when a project's developers abandon it and remove the liquidity or funds, leaving holders with a token they cannot sell for value. A honeypot is a token that can be bought but not sold.

Why it matters

On a decentralized exchange, anyone can create a token and open a market for it in minutes, with no approval, listing review or identity check. That openness is a design feature, and it means that some tokens are built from the start to take buyers’ money.

Rug pulls and honeypots are the two classic designs. Both exploit the same fact: a token is a smart contract, and its rules are whatever its creators wrote.

How a rug pull works

Most new tokens trade against a liquidity pool: a pot holding the new token on one side and an established asset on the other, as explained in AMMs and liquidity pools. The pool is what makes selling possible. A holder sells by putting tokens in and taking some of the established asset out.

In a rug pull, the people who launched the project take that established asset for themselves and leave. There are a few common forms.

  • Liquidity pull. The developers supplied the pool and can withdraw it. When they do, nothing is left to sell into.
  • Insider dump. The developers kept a large share of the supply, sometimes spread across many wallets, and sell it all into the pool.
  • Minting. The contract lets its owner create new tokens at will, which are then sold.
  • Slow abandonment. Often called a soft rug: the team gradually sells and stops working, with no single dramatic moment.

In plain words

A market stall sells vouchers and promises to buy them back at a rising price from its cash box. One night the owners take the cash box and leave. The vouchers still exist. There is nothing behind them.

How a honeypot works

A honeypot is a token whose contract lets people buy and prevents them from selling. The restriction lives in the token’s code. It may allow sales only from the creators’ addresses, charge a sell fee close to the whole amount, or let the owner block chosen holders.

From outside, a honeypot looks like a token in strong demand. The chart rises steadily, because purchases go through and almost no sales do. That appearance is the bait, and it plays directly on FOMO. A buyer discovers the trap only when a sale fails.

What it looks like

No single sign identifies these schemes. The ones below appear often in accounts of past cases.

Sign Why it matters Why it is not proof
Developers can withdraw the liquidity Nothing prevents a pull Many honest new projects start this way
A few wallets hold most of the supply A few sellers can empty the pool Wallets can be exchanges or locked contracts
The owner can mint, pause or change fees The rules can change after people buy Some projects have stated reasons for such controls
Trade history shows almost only buys Selling may be blocked A very new token may simply have few sellers yet
Returns are described as certain A classic mark of fraud Its absence shows nothing

Ideas such as supply concentration and vesting are covered in tokenomics. Tokens launched on attention alone, such as many meme coins, are frequent settings for both schemes.

Going deeper

Liquidity locks. Developers can place their pool share in a time-locked contract so that it cannot be withdrawn before a set date. A lock addresses one route. It does nothing about insider holdings or minting, and locks expire.

Audits and renounced ownership. A code audit is a review by a third party, and renouncing ownership removes the owner’s special powers. Both reduce particular risks. Audits can miss problems, can be faked, and may cover a different version of the code.

Related risks. A project can fail through bugs with no dishonesty at all, which is smart contract risk. A coordinated promotion followed by insider selling is a pump and dump.

Two hypothetical tokensHypothetical illustration

The rug pull. A new token launches with a pool holding 100,000 units of a stablecoin, supplied by its developers. Over three days, buyers add another 150,000, and the price rises. Lia buys 500 units’ worth. On the fourth day the developers withdraw the entire pool of 250,000. Lia’s tokens are still in her wallet. With no pool there is no price and no buyer.

The honeypot. Raj finds a token whose chart has risen for a week without a single red candle. He buys 200 units’ worth. The chart soon shows his holding at three times that. He tries to sell and the transaction fails. He tries a smaller amount and it fails again, each attempt costing a network fee. The contract only accepts sales from its creators’ addresses. The figure on his screen was never obtainable.

What it does NOT mean

  • A rising chart does not show that a token can be sold. In a honeypot, the rise is produced by blocked selling.
  • A liquidity lock is not a safety certificate. It covers one way of taking funds, for a limited time.
  • An audit does not mean a project is honest. It is an opinion about code at one moment.
  • A failed project is not always a rug pull. Many tokens lose value for ordinary reasons, and not every failure is fraud.

Common mistakes

  1. Judging by the chart alone. The chart shows trades that happened, not trades that are possible.
  2. Treating one check as clearance. A lock, an audit or a passed scan by a token-checking tool each answers one question, and contracts can be written to pass a test and behave differently later.
  3. Assuming a displayed balance can be realised. A wallet multiplies a quantity by a quoted price. It does not test the exit.
  4. Trusting popularity. Followers, chat activity and trading volume can all be manufactured.

Risks and limitations

  • These schemes cannot be identified with certainty in advance. Lists of signs come from hindsight.
  • Contract code can be hard to read even for specialists, and can be changed after launch where the design allows it.
  • Losses are rarely recovered. Developers are often anonymous and outside the reach of any authority.
  • Legal treatment differs between jurisdictions and continues to develop.

Key takeaway

In a rug pull the developers remove the liquidity or funds that gave a token its value. In a honeypot the token’s own code lets people buy and stops them selling. In both, what the chart showed before the event said nothing about the exit.
