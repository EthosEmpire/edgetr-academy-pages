Seed Phrases

A seed phrase is a list of words, commonly 12 or 24, from which a wallet can regenerate all of its private keys. It is the master secret and the only backup of the wallet.

Why it matters

A wallet may use dozens of private keys. Backing up each one separately would be impractical, so modern wallets derive all of them from a single secret that a person can write down: the seed phrase.

That makes the seed phrase the most valuable piece of information a wallet owner has. Many thefts from self-held wallets involve no hacking at all. The owner is persuaded to type the phrase somewhere.

What it looks like

A seed phrase, also called a recovery phrase or mnemonic phrase, is a list of ordinary words that the wallet displays once, when it is created. Most wallets use 12 or 24 words. The order of the words matters.

The words are not chosen by the user. They come from a fixed list of 2,048 words defined by a widely used standard called BIP-39, and together they encode a large random number. Twelve words carry 128 bits of randomness and twenty-four carry 256 bits. The final word also contains a short checksum, so most copying errors are detected when the phrase is entered again.

How it works

From the phrase the wallet computes a seed, from the seed a master key, and from the master key a tree of private keys, one for every account and address. This design is called a hierarchical deterministic wallet. Deterministic means the same phrase always produces the same keys in any compatible wallet.

So a wallet restored from its seed phrase on a new device shows the same addresses and the same balances. The balances were on the blockchain all along. The phrase recreated the keys that control them.

In plain words

A seed phrase is like the master pattern from which a locksmith can cut every key for a building. A single lost key can be cut again. Anyone who holds the pattern can cut the whole set, and the locks cannot be changed. The only remedy is to move everything to a different building.

Where a seed phrase belongs

Where it is kept Exposure
Paper, stored offline Out of reach of the internet. Vulnerable to fire, water, loss and anyone who finds it
Metal plate, stored offline Like paper, with better resistance to fire and water
Photo or screenshot Often copied to cloud backups. Readable by malware and by anyone who gets into the account
Notes app, email or cloud document Only as safe as that account. One breach exposes the wallet
Memory alone Lost through forgetting, accident or death

Wallet makers’ documentation generally treats a written, offline copy as the baseline and warns against any digital copy. More copies lower the chance of loss and raise the chance that one is found. There is no arrangement without a trade-off.

Security

No legitimate service, support agent or website ever asks for a seed phrase or private key. The only place a seed phrase is ever entered is the owner’s own wallet app or device, when the owner chooses to restore it. Anyone who asks for one is attempting theft, whatever reason they give. edgeTR Academy never asks for one and has no wallet connection, login or payment anywhere on the site.

Going deeper

The optional passphrase. Many wallets allow an extra word or sentence, sometimes called the 25th word. Each passphrase produces a completely different set of keys. A mistyped or forgotten passphrase does not raise an error. It opens a different, empty wallet. The passphrase is not part of the written 12 or 24 words, so it needs its own safe backup.

Derivation paths. Wallet apps can look in different branches of the key tree. A phrase restored in a different app may show a zero balance even though the funds are untouched, because the app is looking at other addresses.

Phrase and key. A private key controls one address. A seed phrase controls every key the wallet has made and every key it will make.

Wallets without a phrase. Some wallets use their own schemes, and some newer designs replace the phrase with other recovery methods. Each design moves the trust somewhere else. None removes it.

Two hypothetical ways to lose the same walletHypothetical illustration

Loss. Lena writes her 12 words on a sheet of paper and keeps it in a desk drawer. The paper is thrown out during a house move. A year later her phone fails. The funds are still on the blockchain under her addresses, and no person or company can produce the keys.

Theft. Omar photographs his 12 words “just in case”. The image syncs to a cloud account. Someone who gets into that account restores his wallet on their own device and sends everything to another address. Omar’s phone, PIN and wallet app were never touched.

The two cases are opposites, and both are final. A blockchain has no process for reversing either one.

What it does NOT mean

  • A seed phrase is not a login. Typing it into software gives that software a full copy of the wallet.
  • A seed phrase is not held by the wallet’s maker. With a non-custodial wallet, the developers never see it and cannot recover it.
  • A longer phrase does not fix careless storage. Twelve and twenty-four words are both far beyond guessing. Phrases are stolen and lost, not guessed.
  • A hardware wallet does not remove the phrase. The device protects the keys in daily use. The written backup still exists and still controls everything.
  • A wallet PIN or password is not the seed phrase. It only unlocks the copy of the keys on one device.

Common mistakes

  1. Photographing the words at setup. The quickest backup is also the easiest one to steal.
  2. Entering the phrase on a web page. Connecting a wallet to a site never requires it. See phishing.
  3. Giving it to a helper. People offering to fix a wallet problem are covered in fake support and impersonation.
  4. Keeping one copy in one place. A single fire, flood or clear-out then ends access for good.

Risks and limitations

  • Whoever finds a written phrase needs nothing else: no device, no PIN and no password.
  • Standards differ. Not every wallet uses BIP-39, and restoring across different wallet apps does not always work smoothly.
  • Paper fades, burns and gets thrown away. Physical storage has its own failure modes.
  • A phrase that has possibly been exposed cannot be made secret again. The funds have to move to a wallet created from a new phrase.

Key takeaway

A seed phrase is the wallet in written form: commonly 12 or 24 words that can regenerate every private key. It belongs offline, and no legitimate party ever asks for it.
