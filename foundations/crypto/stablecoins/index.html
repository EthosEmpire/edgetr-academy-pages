Stablecoins

A stablecoin is a token designed to track a reference value, usually one U.S. dollar, using reserves, crypto collateral or an algorithm, each with its own way of failing.

Why it matters

Stablecoins are among the most used assets in crypto. They are the unit most trades are priced in, the usual way to move value between platforms, and the base asset of much of decentralised finance. Their names suggest they carry no risk, and that is the misunderstanding this page exists to correct.

How it works

A stablecoin is a token designed to track a reference value. Most target one U.S. dollar, though some track other currencies or assets. The target relationship is called the peg.

A peg is a goal, not a property. Something has to keep the market price near the target, and the three main designs do it differently.

Fiat-backed. A company issues tokens and holds reserves, such as cash and short-term government debt, intended to match the tokens in circulation. Approved customers can hand tokens back and receive dollars. USDT and USDC are examples of this design. The holder relies on the issuer: that the reserves exist, are liquid and can be reached.

Crypto-collateralised. A set of smart contracts lets users lock crypto assets and create stablecoins against them. Because crypto prices swing, the collateral must be worth more than the stablecoins issued. DAI is the best-known example, and its mix of collateral has changed over time. The holder relies on the contracts, the collateral and the liquidation process.

Algorithmic. Code tries to hold the peg by expanding and shrinking supply, often through a second token that absorbs losses, with little or no outside collateral. The peg rests on continued confidence. In 2022 a large algorithmic stablecoin lost its peg and collapsed within days.

In plain words

A stablecoin is like a cloakroom ticket that says “worth one dollar”. The ticket is only as good as the coat behind the counter and the attendant’s willingness to hand it over. The three designs are three different kinds of cloakroom.

How the peg is held

For a fiat-backed coin, the peg is held by arbitrage. If the token trades below 1.00, firms that can redeem with the issuer buy it cheaply and redeem at face value, and their buying lifts the price. If it trades above 1.00, they deposit dollars, receive new tokens and sell them.

For a crypto-collateralised coin, the anchor is the collateral ratio.

When a position’s collateral falls below a set minimum, the contracts sell it to cover the stablecoins issued. This is a form of liquidation, and it works only if the collateral can be sold quickly enough at a high enough price.

Going deeper

Depegs happen in every design. Fiat-backed stablecoins have traded below their target for a time when doubts arose about an issuer’s reserves or its access to them. Collateralised designs have come under strain in sharp market falls. Algorithmic designs have failed completely. A fuller treatment is in stablecoin risks.

Issuer controls. Many fiat-backed stablecoin contracts let the issuer freeze tokens at specific addresses, for example at the request of law enforcement. A holder’s balance is therefore subject to the issuer’s decisions as well as to the blockchain’s rules.

Not a bank deposit. A stablecoin is a claim on an issuer or a position in a protocol. It is generally not covered by deposit insurance, and the holder’s legal rights depend on the issuer’s terms and on the jurisdiction. Regulation of stablecoins differs between countries and is still developing.

A collateralised position under stressHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A user locks crypto worth 150 and creates 100 stablecoins. The protocol’s minimum collateral ratio is 130%.

  • At creation: 150 / 100 = 150%.
  • The collateral’s market price falls by 20%. It is now worth 150 x 0.80 = 120, and the ratio is 120 / 100 = 120%.
  • 120% is below the 130% minimum, so the contracts can sell the collateral to buy back and cancel the 100 stablecoins.

If the collateral had instead fallen 40% before anyone could sell it, it would be worth 150 x 0.60 = 90. That is less than the 100 stablecoins outstanding, leaving 10 with nothing behind them.

A peg can break

No stablecoin design removes risk. It moves the risk to an issuer, to collateral and code, or to market confidence. A token that has held 1.00 for years can still trade below it, briefly or permanently.

What it does NOT mean

  • “Stable” does not mean safe. It describes the target, not a protection.
  • A stablecoin is not a dollar. It is a token whose market price is meant to follow the dollar, backed by whatever its design provides.
  • A long record at 1.00 is not proof of sound backing. Pegs have broken suddenly after long calm periods.
  • Published reserve reports are not all equal. A snapshot statement and a full audit are different things, and their scope varies by issuer.
  • Holding a stablecoin does not avoid risk. It exchanges price swings for issuer, contract and peg risk.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Treating all stablecoins as the same. The three designs fail in different ways and for different reasons.
  2. Assuming anyone can redeem at 1.00. Direct redemption is usually limited to approved customers with minimum sizes.
  3. Ignoring where it is held. A stablecoin on an exchange or in a protocol adds counterparty risk or smart contract risk on top of its own.

Risks and limits

  • Reserves may be less liquid or less complete than holders assume.
  • Issuers can freeze balances, suspend redemption or fail.
  • Contracts and price feeds used by collateralised designs can contain bugs.
  • Algorithmic designs depend on confidence that can vanish quickly.
  • Legal treatment varies by country and continues to change.

Key takeaway

A stablecoin is a token that aims at a fixed value and reaches it through reserves, collateral or an algorithm. The peg is a mechanism that usually holds, not a promise that cannot fail.
