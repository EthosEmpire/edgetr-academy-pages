Meme Coins

A meme coin is a crypto asset built around a joke, image or online community instead of a stated function. Its price depends almost entirely on attention and can change violently.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Meme coins are among the most visible parts of crypto and among the riskiest. They show, in a pure form, what happens when an asset’s price rests on attention alone. This page explains the mechanics and the common scam patterns. It does not treat any meme coin as an opportunity.

How it works

A meme coin is a crypto asset whose identity is a joke, a character, a slogan or a community, with little or no stated purpose beyond that. The first widely known one was created in 2013 as a parody of the many new coins appearing at the time.

A few meme coins are native coins with their own blockchains. The great majority today are tokens, created by deploying a small smart contract on an existing chain. Doing so takes minutes and costs very little. Some platforms reduce it to filling in a name and an image.

Because creation is nearly free, new meme tokens appear in very large numbers. Most attract little trading and fade within days or weeks.

In plain words

Picture a collectible sticker that anyone can print in any quantity, with a picture that happens to be popular this week. It has a price only while enough people want it at the same moment. When the crowd’s interest moves on, nothing else holds the price up.

What sets the price

A meme coin usually has no revenue, no claim on anything and no required use. Its price reflects how many people want to hold it right now, compared with how many want to sell. That makes it highly sensitive to three things.

Attention. Interest spreads through social media, and it can reverse as fast as it arrives. The psychology involved is covered in FOMO and herd behavior.

Concentration. A small number of wallets often hold most of the supply.

Liquidity. Many meme tokens trade against a small pool of another asset on a decentralised exchange. With thin liquidity, an ordinary-sized order can move the price a long way, and the price shown on screen may not be obtainable for more than a small amount. The pool mechanics are explained in AMMs and liquidity pools.

Going deeper: extreme volatility

Price moves of tens of percent in a day are ordinary for meme coins, in both directions. Falls of 80% or 90% from a peak are common, and many tokens go effectively to zero.

Timing also differs between participants. Creators and automated traders can acquire tokens in the first seconds of trading, at prices later buyers never see. People who learn of a token from a viral post are, by construction, arriving after the attention has already moved the price.

Common scam patterns

Meme tokens are a frequent vehicle for fraud, because they are cheap to create and need no explanation. Patterns reported repeatedly include:

  • Rug pull. The creators remove the pool of assets that the token trades against, or sell a large hidden holding, leaving a token that cannot be sold for anything. See rug pulls and honeypots.
  • Honeypot. The contract lets people buy and blocks them from selling.
  • Pump and dump. A group promotes a token it already holds, then sells into the buying it has encouraged. See pump and dump.
  • Impersonation. A token copies the name of a well-known asset, a celebrity or a news event to borrow its credibility.
  • Paid promotion. Public figures are paid, sometimes without disclosure, to mention a token.
  • Hidden contract powers. The code lets an administrator create new tokens, raise a tax on transfers or freeze holders.

None of these is always visible in advance, and a token with none of them can still lose its entire value.

A quoted value that cannot be realisedHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A meme token trades in a pool holding 1,000,000 tokens and 50,000 units of a stablecoin. The quoted price is 50,000 / 1,000,000 = 0.05.

A holder has 200,000 tokens. At the quoted price they are “worth” 200,000 x 0.05 = 10,000.

The pool keeps the product of its two balances constant: 1,000,000 x 50,000 = 50,000,000,000. If the holder sells all 200,000 tokens:

  • Tokens in the pool: 1,000,000 + 200,000 = 1,200,000.
  • Stablecoins left in the pool: 50,000,000,000 / 1,200,000 = about 41,667.
  • Stablecoins received: 50,000 minus 41,667 = about 8,333, before fees.

That is about 16.7% less than the quoted 10,000. The pool’s price afterwards is 41,667 / 1,200,000 = about 0.035, a fall of roughly 30% caused by this one sale. Every other holder’s quoted value fell with it.

What it does NOT mean

  • A rising price does not show that a meme coin has value behind it. It shows that buyers have recently outnumbered sellers.
  • A large community is not a safeguard. Communities form quickly around promotion and disperse just as quickly.
  • A big market cap figure is not money that can be taken out. It is a small-trade price multiplied by the whole supply. See market cap and FDV.
  • Past cases of large gains are not evidence about the next token. They are the rare visible outcomes among a very large number of failures.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Assuming a displayed balance can be sold. Thin pools and slippage decide what a sale returns, and a honeypot returns nothing.
  2. Trusting a familiar name. Anyone can create a token with any name. The contract address is the only identity.
  3. Reading a low unit price as cheap. Supplies in the billions or trillions make the price of one unit meaningless by itself.

Risks and limits

  • Total loss is a common outcome, not an exceptional one.
  • Prices can fall most of the way to zero within minutes.
  • Liquidity can be withdrawn by whoever supplied it.
  • Fraud is widespread and transactions cannot be reversed.
  • There is usually no accountable issuer, and legal protections are limited and vary by jurisdiction.

Key takeaway

A meme coin is an asset whose price rests on attention instead of function, usually with concentrated ownership and thin liquidity. Extreme swings and scams are a normal part of that market, and a quoted price is not the same as a realisable one.
