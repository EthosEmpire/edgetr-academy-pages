Token Supply: Circulating, Total & Max

Circulating supply is the number of units in public circulation, total supply is the number that exist now, and max supply is the hard cap, if the asset has one.

Why it matters

A price on its own says very little. To compare assets, or to understand how an asset might change over time, you need to know how many units exist, how many can be traded, and how many could exist in future. Those are three different numbers, and mixing them up is one of the most common errors in reading crypto data.

The three figures

Circulating supply is the number of units in public circulation: held by the public and free to move. It leaves out units that exist but are locked, reserved or otherwise unavailable.

Total supply is the number of units that exist right now. It is everything created so far, minus anything that has been destroyed.

Max supply is the hard upper limit on how many units can ever exist, if the design has one. Bitcoin’s limit is 21 million, as described in the 21 million limit. Ether has no fixed maximum, so data sites show that field as empty or “infinite”.

For any asset, circulating supply is no greater than total supply, and total supply is no greater than max supply.

In plain words

A cinema has 1,000 seats: that is the max. For tonight’s showing 600 tickets have been printed: that is the total. Of those, 250 have been handed out and 350 are still in a drawer for staff and sponsors: 250 is the circulating figure. The drawer matters, because those tickets can come out later.

How supply changes

Supply is not a fixed fact. Four processes move it:

  • Issuance creates new units, for example as rewards for miners, validators or users. This is also called minting or emission.
  • Unlocking moves existing units from locked to circulating. See token unlocks and emissions.
  • Burning destroys units, usually by sending them to an address nobody can spend from or by a function in the token’s contract. Burned units leave total supply.
  • Loss happens when keys are lost. Lost units still appear in the figures, because a blockchain cannot tell a lost coin from an untouched one.
Event Circulating Total Max
New units issued to the public Up Up Unchanged
Locked units become transferable Up Unchanged Unchanged
Units burned from circulation Down Down Unchanged in some designs, reduced in others
Keys lost Unchanged on paper Unchanged Unchanged

Going deeper

Circulating supply is an estimate. The blockchain shows every balance, but it does not label which ones are “locked” or “reserved”. Data providers decide what to exclude, often using information supplied by the project. Two sites can show different circulating figures for the same token on the same day.

A cap is only as firm as the rules behind it. For a coin, the cap is part of the protocol and changes only if the network’s participants adopt new rules. For a token, the cap is whatever the contract enforces. Some contracts fix it permanently. Others let an administrator create more.

Scheduled issuance. Some assets release new units on a published timetable. Bitcoin’s issuance is cut in half at fixed intervals, an event described in the halving.

Why the gap matters. When circulating supply is a small share of the total or the maximum, a large number of units have yet to reach the market. That gap is the difference between market cap and fully diluted valuation, and it is a central topic of tokenomics.

Reconciling the three figuresHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers for one token, in millions of units.

  • Max supply: 1,000.
  • Created so far: 620.
  • Burned so far: 20.
  • Total supply: 620 minus 20 = 600.
  • Locked for the team, investors and treasury: 350.
  • Circulating supply: 600 minus 350 = 250.

So 250 / 1,000 = 25% of the maximum is circulating. Another 350 exist but are locked, and 1,000 minus 620 = 380 have not been created yet. Whether the 20 burned units can be created again, up to the old cap, depends on how this token’s contract defines its maximum.

What it does NOT mean

  • A low max supply does not make an asset scarce in any useful sense. Units are divisible, and scarcity relative to demand is what supply and demand describes.
  • A fixed cap is not a statement about price. It limits quantity only.
  • Circulating supply is not the amount for sale. Most circulating units sit in wallets and are not on offer at any given moment.
  • A burn does not create value by itself. It reduces the count of units. What that means depends on everything else.
  • Reported figures are not audited facts. They rely on definitions and disclosures that differ between providers.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Using total supply where circulating is meant. The two can differ several times over.
  2. Ignoring the locked portion. Units that exist but cannot move yet are part of the asset’s future supply.
  3. Assuming “max supply” always exists. Many assets have none.
  4. Comparing assets by supply alone. One asset having a billion units and another a million says nothing without the price of each.
  5. Trusting a single data site. Definitions of circulating supply vary.

Risks and limits

  • Circulating supply depends on project disclosures that can be late, incomplete or wrong.
  • Contract administrators may be able to create new units outside any published schedule.
  • Supply rules can be changed by protocol upgrades or governance votes.
  • Lost and inactive units cannot be measured reliably.

Key takeaway

Circulating supply is what can move today, total supply is what exists today, and max supply is the ceiling if there is one. The gaps between the three show how much supply may still reach the market.
