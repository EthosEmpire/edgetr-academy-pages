What Is Cryptocurrency?

A cryptocurrency is a digital asset whose ownership records are kept on a blockchain, a shared ledger run by a network of computers instead of a single bank or company.

Why it matters

Cryptocurrency is the subject the rest of this shelf builds on. Wallets, exchanges, fees, token prices and scams all make more sense once the basic object is clear: what a cryptocurrency is, where it lives and who is in charge of it.

This is the start-here page for Crypto Basics. It gives the whole picture in plain terms and points to the pages that cover each part in depth.

How it works

A cryptocurrency is a digital asset whose ownership records are kept on a blockchain: a ledger that is copied across many computers and updated according to rules written into software. No single company or bank holds the master copy.

Three ideas do most of the work:

  • A shared ledger. Every computer on the network can hold the same record of who owns what, and each one checks new entries before accepting them.
  • Keys instead of accounts. Ownership is proved with a private key, a secret number that produces a digital signature. A wallet stores keys. The units themselves never leave the ledger.
  • Rules instead of an administrator. The software defines how transfers are checked and how new units come into existence. Changing those rules needs broad agreement among the people who run it.

The “crypto” in the name comes from cryptography, the mathematics used to sign transfers and to link the records together.

In plain words

Picture a notebook that lists everyone’s balance, with identical copies held by a large crowd of strangers. To move units you sign a note with a signature only you can produce. Everyone checks the signature and updates their copy in the same way. Nobody can quietly edit one copy, because it would stop matching all the others.

How it differs from money in a bank

With a bank, you trust an institution to keep the record correct, and the law gives you ways to complain when something goes wrong. With a cryptocurrency, the record is kept by software and many independent operators, and the holder of the key carries the responsibility. A transfer usually cannot be blocked by a single clerk, and it cannot be undone by one either.

The main kinds of crypto asset

  • Coins are the native asset of their own blockchain. Bitcoin is the coin of the Bitcoin network, and ether is the coin of Ethereum.
  • Tokens are issued by programs that run on an existing blockchain. The difference is covered in coins and tokens.
  • Stablecoins are tokens designed to track a reference value such as one U.S. dollar. See stablecoins.
  • Altcoins is a loose label for every cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin. See altcoins.

Going deeper

Where the price comes from. No central issuer sets the price of a cryptocurrency. The price is whatever buyers and sellers agree on in a market at that moment. Crypto markets never close, and their volatility has historically been high compared with most traditional assets.

Who holds it. A person can hold their own keys, or leave the asset with an exchange or another custodian that holds the keys for them. The two arrangements carry different risks, described in custodial and non-custodial wallets.

The legal picture. How crypto assets are classified, taxed and regulated differs between countries and keeps changing. Protections that apply to bank deposits or brokerage accounts often do not apply to crypto holdings.

A hypothetical transferHypothetical illustration

Ana controls an address holding 5 units of a coin. She sends 2 units to Ben and the network charges a fee of 0.01.

  • Ana’s wallet signs a message: “move 2 units from my address to Ben’s address”.
  • Computers on the network check the signature and check that her address really holds enough.
  • The transfer is written into the next block of the ledger.
  • Ana’s address now shows 5 minus 2 minus 0.01 = 2.99 units. Ben’s address shows 2 more than before.

No bank approved the payment, and nobody can reverse it if Ana typed the wrong address.

What it does NOT mean

  • Digital does not mean stored on your device. The record lives on the network. A wallet only holds the keys that control it.
  • Decentralised does not mean unregulated or anonymous. Laws apply to people and businesses that use crypto, and most blockchains are public records that anyone can read.
  • A market price is not a promise. Nothing in the technology says what a unit will be worth tomorrow.
  • Cryptocurrency is not one thing. Thousands of assets share the label, with very different designs, histories and risks.
  • Secured by cryptography does not mean safe from loss. Most losses come from stolen keys, scams, failed platforms and user error, not from broken mathematics.

Common misunderstandings

  1. “My coins are in my wallet app.” The app holds keys. Delete the app and the coins are still on the ledger, reachable by anyone who has the key or its backup.
  2. “Someone can fix a wrong transfer.” On most blockchains a confirmed transfer is final. There is no support desk for the network itself.
  3. “A cheap unit price means it is cheap.” The price of one unit says nothing without the number of units. See market cap.

Risks and limits

  • Prices can move sharply in either direction, and an asset can lose most or all of its market value.
  • Lost or stolen keys usually cannot be recovered.
  • Exchanges and other platforms that hold crypto for customers can fail or be hacked.
  • Scams are common, and the finality of transfers makes them hard to undo.
  • Rules, including tax treatment, vary by country and can change.

Key takeaway

A cryptocurrency is an entry on a shared ledger that only the holder of a private key can move, governed by software rules instead of a central administrator. That design removes some intermediaries and puts the responsibility, and the risk, on the holder.
