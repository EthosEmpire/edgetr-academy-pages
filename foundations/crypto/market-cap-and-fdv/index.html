Market Cap & Fully Diluted Valuation

Market cap is price multiplied by circulating supply. Fully diluted valuation (FDV) is price multiplied by max or total supply, showing the size implied if every unit existed today.

Why it matters

Market cap is the standard way to rank and compare crypto assets, and FDV is the figure that shows what the ranking leaves out. Together they answer two questions: how large is this asset at today’s price, and how much of its supply is not yet in the market. Neither is a measure of what an asset is worth.

How it is calculated

Market cap = price x circulating supplyCirculating supply is the number of units in public circulation.
Fully diluted valuation = price x max supplyIf the asset has no max supply, total supply is normally used instead.

The price is the same in both formulas. Only the supply figure differs. Market cap counts the units that can trade today. Fully diluted valuation (FDV) counts every unit that will ever exist, as though all of them were already in circulation at the current price.

The idea comes from share markets, where market capitalisation is share price multiplied by shares outstanding.

In plain words

One slice of pizza costs 2. A pizza cut into 8 slices is “worth” 16 on that basis. Cutting it into 16 smaller slices at 1 each changes the price per slice and nothing about the pizza. Market cap looks at the whole pizza. Unit price looks at one slice.

How to read the two together

A useful derived number is the ratio between them:

Market cap / FDV = circulating supply / max supplyThe share of the eventual supply that is circulating now.
Ratio What it describes
Near 100% Nearly all units are already circulating. Little new supply is still to come.
Around 50% Half the eventual supply is not yet in the market.
Low, such as 10% to 25% Most of the supply is still locked or uncreated.

A low ratio is a fact about supply, not a verdict. It says that the number of units is set to grow, and how and when is described by the release schedule. See token unlocks and emissions.

Going deeper

Unit price comparisons mislead. A token priced at a fraction of a cent can be larger than one priced in the hundreds, because the number of units differs by orders of magnitude. For one token to reach the unit price of another, its market cap would have to change by the ratio of their supplies.

Market cap is not money that went in. It multiplies the most recent trade price by all circulating units. A small trade at a higher price raises the market cap of the whole supply, even though only a little money changed hands.

Market cap is not money that can come out. If holders tried to sell a large share of the supply, they would move through the available buy orders and the price would drop along the way. How far depends on liquidity, not on market cap.

FDV is a hypothetical. It applies today’s price to units that are not in the market. There is no reason the price would be unchanged once they are. FDV is best read as a statement about the scale of future supply at the current price, nothing more.

Thin float. When only a small share of the supply circulates, a modest amount of trading can set a high price, and FDV then shows a very large figure resting on a small market. This pattern is one reason tokenomics pays attention to who holds the non-circulating part.

Data quality. Both figures inherit every uncertainty in the supply data. Different sites can show different market caps for the same asset at the same moment.

Two figures and a small tradeHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A token trades at 2.00. Its circulating supply is 250 million and its max supply is 1,000 million.

  • Market cap: 2.00 x 250M = 500M.
  • FDV: 2.00 x 1,000M = 2,000M.
  • Ratio: 500M / 2,000M = 25%.

Someone then buys 1,000 tokens at 2.20, paying 2,200 in total, and 2.20 becomes the latest price.

  • Market cap: 2.20 x 250M = 550M.
  • FDV: 2.20 x 1,000M = 2,200M.

The market cap figure rose by 50 million and the FDV by 200 million on a purchase of 2,200. No holder could sell 250 million tokens at 2.20, because nothing like that quantity of buy orders exists at that price.

What it does NOT mean

  • Market cap is not the value of a project. It is a price multiplied by a count.
  • A low market cap does not mean room to grow, and a high one does not mean safety. Size is not direction.
  • FDV is not a forecast of future market cap. It holds price fixed while supply changes, which is not how markets behave.
  • A low market cap to FDV ratio is not a prediction about price. It describes supply that has yet to be released.
  • Ranking by market cap is not ranking by quality. It orders assets by one arithmetic result.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Judging by unit price. “Only 0.01 each” says nothing until multiplied by supply.
  2. Treating market cap as cash invested. The total actually paid by holders can be far lower or higher.
  3. Ignoring FDV when float is small. The market cap then describes a minority of the eventual supply.
  4. Treating FDV as a target. It is a multiplication, not a destination.
  5. Comparing with other on-chain metrics carelessly. Figures such as TVL measure different things and are not interchangeable with market cap.

Risks and limits

  • Both figures depend on supply data that can be estimated, self-reported or out of date.
  • The price used may come from thin markets and may not be achievable in size.
  • FDV has limited meaning for assets with no max supply and ongoing issuance, because the total supply it uses keeps growing.
  • Neither figure says anything about usage, revenue, security or legal status.

Key takeaway

Market cap is price multiplied by circulating supply, and FDV is price multiplied by max or total supply. Read together, they show how large an asset is today and how much of its supply has yet to arrive.
