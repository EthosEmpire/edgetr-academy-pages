Governance & Utility Tokens

A governance token carries votes over a protocol's rules or treasury, and a utility token is needed to use a service. Many tokens combine both roles, and neither role is a claim on profits.

Why it matters

Many tokens are described by what they let the holder do: vote on a protocol, or use a service. Those descriptions sound like the rights of a shareholder or a customer, and they are neither. Knowing what a governance or utility token really grants, and what it does not, is basic to reading any project’s claims.

How it works

A governance token gives its holder a say in decisions about a protocol. The protocol is usually a set of smart contracts, such as a lending market or an exchange in DeFi. Decisions can include fee levels, which assets are supported, software upgrades and how a shared treasury is spent. The group of token holders making those decisions is often called a DAO, short for decentralised autonomous organisation.

A utility token is one that a service requires or rewards. It may be the unit in which fees are paid, a pass that unlocks features, or a deposit that participants must lock up to perform a role.

The labels describe intended function. They are not legal categories, and a single token frequently carries both roles. The native coins of blockchains are a related case: ether is needed to pay gas, which is a utility in the plainest sense.

In plain words

A governance token resembles a vote in a residents’ association: it lets you help decide the building’s rules, in proportion to how many flats you own. A utility token resembles a token for the building’s laundry machines: it is useful there and nowhere else.

How governance works

Most systems share a few features:

Feature What it means
Token-weighted voting Voting power is proportional to tokens held or delegated
Proposal threshold A minimum holding is needed to submit a proposal
Quorum A minimum number of votes must be cast for a result to count
Delegation Holders can hand their voting power to someone else without transferring the tokens
Timelock A fixed delay between a passed vote and its execution

Voting can take place on-chain, where the result automatically triggers the change in the contracts, or off-chain, where holders signal a preference and a team or a group of key-holders carries it out. The second form depends on those people acting as the vote directed.

Going deeper

Voting power follows ownership. With one token, one vote, control tracks the distribution of tokens. If the team, early investors and a few large holders own most of the supply, they decide most outcomes. This is the governance face of concentration risk, and the reason the allocation tables in tokenomics matter.

Turnout is usually low. In many protocols only a small share of tokens votes on a given proposal. Low turnout lowers the number of tokens needed to win.

Votes can be borrowed or bought. Because tokens can be lent, an actor may be able to gather voting power temporarily. Protocols have been attacked through their own governance in this way, with proposals that transferred funds to the attacker. Timelocks and snapshots of balances taken before a vote are common defences.

What governance usually cannot do. A vote can change only what the contracts allow to be changed. And a governance token does not normally give a legal claim on assets, revenue or the company that built the protocol. Some designs do direct fees to token holders or stakers, by a rule that governance itself can alter.

Legal status. Whether a given token is treated as a security, a commodity or something else depends on its features and on the jurisdiction. The label a project chooses does not decide that question, and regulators have said so publicly.

A vote decided by one holderHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A governance token has a supply of 100 million. A proposal needs a quorum of 10% of supply and a simple majority of votes cast.

  • Quorum required: 100M x 10% = 10M votes.
  • Votes cast: 8M for and 4M against, 12M in total. Quorum is met, and turnout is 12%.
  • Result: 8 / 12 = about 66.7% in favour. The proposal passes.

Of the 8M votes in favour, 6M came from one holder. That holder alone cast 6 / 12 = 50% of all votes. Without them, the count would have been 2M for and 4M against, and with only 6M votes cast the quorum would not have been met.

What it does NOT mean

  • A governance token is not a share. It normally carries no legal claim on profits, assets or a company.
  • Holding a vote does not mean having influence. A small holding among large ones rarely changes an outcome.
  • “Decentralised governance” does not mean nobody is in control. A few wallets, or a team holding upgrade keys, may hold decisive power.
  • “Utility” does not mean the token is needed or used. It describes an intended function.
  • Calling a token a utility token does not settle its legal treatment. That depends on facts and on local law.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Equating votes with ownership rights. Governance covers what the contracts expose, not everything about a project.
  2. Ignoring who holds the supply. The distribution of tokens is the distribution of power.
  3. Assuming a passed vote is always executed. Off-chain votes rely on people to implement them.
  4. Treating stated utility as demand. A use case on paper may see little real use.

Risks and limits

  • Concentrated holdings and low turnout can let a small group pass any proposal.
  • Governance processes can be attacked, including with borrowed tokens.
  • A vote can change fees, rewards and token supply in ways that affect existing holders.
  • Administrator keys may be able to override or bypass governance.
  • Legal and tax treatment differs by jurisdiction and is still developing.

Key takeaway

A governance token carries votes and a utility token unlocks a service, and many tokens do both. Each grants only what the underlying contracts and the distribution of holdings actually allow.
