Airdrops

An airdrop is a distribution of tokens to many wallet addresses, usually without payment, often to reward early users or spread ownership. The format is widely imitated by scams.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

Airdrops are a common way for crypto projects to hand out tokens, and “claim your airdrop” is one of the most common lures used to steal them. Anyone who uses a wallet will sooner or later meet both the real thing and the imitation. Understanding the mechanics makes it easier to tell which actions a real claim involves and which belong only to a scam.

How it works

An airdrop is a distribution of tokens to a large number of wallet addresses, usually without the recipients paying for them. A project typically sets aside a share of its supply for the purpose.

Common forms:

  • Retroactive. Tokens go to addresses that used a protocol before a certain date. Activity is recorded in a snapshot, a record of the blockchain’s state at one moment.
  • Holder-based. Tokens go to addresses that held a particular asset at the snapshot.
  • Task-based. Tokens are promised for actions such as testing a product.
  • Direct. Tokens are sent straight to addresses with no action needed.

Most large airdrops use a claim process. The project publishes a contract containing the list of eligible addresses and amounts. Each recipient sends a transaction to that contract, pays the network fee, and receives their allocation. Claims usually close after a set period.

In plain words

A genuine airdrop is like a shop mailing vouchers to past customers. A fake one is like a stranger at the door saying you have won a prize and asking for your house keys to deliver it. The first asks nothing secret of you. The second is only after the keys.

Why projects do it

Projects give several reasons, and more than one may apply:

  • to spread ownership of a governance token among people who use the protocol;
  • to reward early users and testers;
  • to attract attention and new users.

Because rewards go to past activity, some people perform activity only to qualify, often with many wallets at once. Projects try to filter out such duplicate participants. Criteria are normally announced after the fact, so activity carried out in the hope of an airdrop costs fees and may earn nothing.

Going deeper: how the format is abused

A fake airdrop does not need to break any cryptography. It needs the victim to sign something. The usual routes are:

  • Phishing sites. A copy of a real project’s claim page, promoted through search adverts, replies on social media, or compromised official accounts. See phishing.
  • Malicious approvals and signatures. The site asks the wallet to grant spending permission over tokens it already holds, or to sign a message that authorises a transfer. See token approvals and signing transactions.
  • Unsolicited tokens. An unknown token appears in a wallet, with a name or a note pointing to a website. The token is the advertisement. Receiving it is harmless by itself. Interacting with the site is where losses occur.
  • Advance fees. A message says tokens are waiting and a payment is needed to release them.
  • Seed phrase requests. Any page or person asking for a seed phrase or private key.

The dedicated page on fake airdrops and wallet drainers covers these attacks in detail.

Airdrops and scams look alike on the surface

A real claim sends tokens to the claimant and costs a network fee. It never requires a seed phrase or private key, never requires a payment to the project, and for a plain claim needs no permission to spend the claimant’s other assets. Fraudulent sites are often indistinguishable from real ones by appearance, and stolen assets generally cannot be recovered.

The arithmetic of one airdropHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A project has a total supply of 1,000 million tokens and assigns 10% to an airdrop.

  • Airdrop pool: 1,000M x 10% = 100M tokens.
  • Eligible addresses: 200,000.
  • Average allocation: 100M / 200,000 = 500 tokens.

The average hides the spread. Allocations are weighted by activity, so many addresses receive far less than 500 and a few receive far more.

Claiming costs a network fee of 0.002 of the chain’s native coin, whatever the allocation. By the end of the claim window, 150,000 addresses have claimed 80M tokens. The other 20M return to the project’s treasury. In the first days, 100M minus 20M = 80M newly received tokens are free to be sold, in a market that did not exist a week earlier.

What it does NOT mean

  • An airdrop is not free money. Claiming costs fees, the tokens may have little or no market, and receipt can have tax consequences that vary by country.
  • Tokens appearing in a wallet are not a gift to be trusted. Unsolicited tokens are a standard lure.
  • Using a protocol does not entitle anyone to an airdrop. Most protocols never run one, and criteria are set by the project.
  • An airdrop does not show that a project is sound. It is a distribution method, used by serious and fraudulent projects alike.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Following a link instead of checking the source. Fake claim pages are promoted more aggressively than real ones.
  2. Signing without reading. The wallet’s prompt shows what is being authorised. A claim has no reason to request spending rights.
  3. Treating urgency as normal. Pressure to act within minutes is a feature of scams.

Risks and limits

  • Wallet-draining scams built around airdrops are widespread, and losses are usually permanent.
  • Airdropped tokens often trade in thin, volatile markets, especially at first.
  • Claim contracts are smart contracts and can contain bugs.
  • Eligibility rules are discretionary and can exclude people without explanation.
  • Tax and legal treatment of received tokens differs between jurisdictions.

Key takeaway

An airdrop distributes tokens to many addresses, usually through a claim that costs only a network fee. Because the format is so easy to imitate, the request itself is the test: a real claim never asks for keys, payments or permission over other assets.

Defines an airdrop as a way for issuers to distribute crypto assets for no or nominal consideration, and explains when it does and does not involve a securities transaction
