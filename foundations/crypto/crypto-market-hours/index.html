A Market That Never Closes

Crypto markets trade 24 hours a day, every day of the year, with no opening bell, closing price or weekend break. That changes how candles, liquidity and risk behave.

Why it matters

Most markets a newcomer has heard of keep office hours. Crypto does not. There is no closing bell, no weekend pause and no moment when everyone agrees on “today’s price”. That single fact changes how charts are built, when liquidity is available and how risk behaves while you are asleep.

How it works

A stock exchange is a venue with opening hours. A blockchain is a network that produces blocks around the clock, and the exchanges that list crypto assets, both centralised and decentralised, operate continuously to match it. Trading takes place across many venues worldwide at once, each with its own order book or pool, kept roughly in line with one another by arbitrage.

Three consequences follow directly:

  • No official close. In share markets, the closing price is produced by a formal process and is used for valuations and index calculations. Crypto has no equivalent. Any “close” is a timestamp someone chose.
  • No overnight gap. Because trading never stops, the price at the start of one period is normally the price at the end of the last. Empty spaces between candles, described in gaps, are rare on crypto spot charts.
  • News is priced immediately. An event on a Saturday night is traded on a Saturday night.

In plain words

A stock exchange is like a shop with a shutter: prices on the shelf are frozen overnight and can jump when it reopens. A crypto market is like a vending machine in the street. It works at any hour, including when nobody sensible is awake, and whatever happens then counts just as much.

What a “daily candle” means here

A daily candle needs a start and an end. With no session to define them, charting tools pick a boundary. Midnight UTC is a widely used convention, but it is not universal, and a platform may let you change the time zone.

The boundary matters more than it seems. The same trades, cut at different times, give different opens, closes, highs and lows, and therefore different candlestick shapes and different indicator values. The worked example below shows this. The settings involved are covered in timeframes on a chart.

Weekly and monthly candles have the same property. A “weekly close” is the end of whichever seven-day block the chart uses.

Going deeper

Open is not the same as liquid. Activity rises and falls through the day as different regions come online, and weekends are often quieter. In quiet hours there are fewer resting orders, so liquidity is thinner, spreads can widen and a given order can move the price further.

Risk runs while you are away. A position cannot be parked at a closing price. Price can travel a long way overnight or over a weekend. For leveraged positions that includes liquidation, which is carried out automatically at any hour. Resting instructions such as stop orders exist partly for this reason, and they can fill at a worse price than expected in fast or thin conditions.

Venues can stop even though the market does not. Exchanges go offline for maintenance or under heavy load, sometimes during the busiest moments. Blockchains themselves have suffered congestion and, on some networks, outages. “Always open” describes the market as a whole, not any single platform.

Products tied to traditional hours. Crypto futures and funds listed on conventional exchanges trade only during those exchanges’ sessions. Their charts can show gaps after a weekend even though the underlying asset traded throughout. A broader comparison is in crypto markets and stock markets.

The same trades, two different daily candlesHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical prices for one asset over two days, in UTC:

  • Monday 00:00: 100
  • Monday 12:00: 104
  • Tuesday 00:00: 101
  • Tuesday 12:00: 97
  • Wednesday 00:00: 99

A chart that starts each day at 00:00 UTC shows two candles. Monday opens at 100 and closes at 101, a gain of 1. Tuesday opens at 101 and closes at 99, a loss of 2.

A chart that starts each day at 12:00 UTC shows one full candle across the same period. It opens at 104 on Monday noon and closes at 97 on Tuesday noon, a loss of 7.

The trades are identical. One chart shows a small up day and a small down day. The other shows a single large down day. Neither is wrong, and a pattern or indicator reading taken from one may not exist on the other.

What it does NOT mean

  • Always open does not mean always liquid. Depth varies by hour, by day and by venue.
  • A daily close is not an official price. It is the last trade before a boundary that a chart happens to use.
  • No gaps does not mean no sudden moves. Price can still change sharply within minutes. It simply does so while trading.
  • Continuous trading does not mean every exchange is available. Individual platforms can pause or fail.
  • Being able to trade at any hour is not a reason to. The clock offers no information about the market.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Assuming all charts show the same candles. Different boundaries and different venues give different data.
  2. Treating weekends as inactive. Large moves have occurred on weekends, often in thinner markets.
  3. Forgetting that leverage works at night. Automatic processes do not wait for anyone to wake up.
  4. Reading one venue’s price as “the” price. Each venue has its own, usually close and occasionally not.

Risks and limits

  • Thin periods can produce sharp moves and poor fills.
  • Positions are exposed at all hours, with no pause in which to reassess.
  • Venue outages can prevent action at the moment it is most wanted.
  • Candle-based analysis depends on a boundary that is a matter of convention.

Key takeaway

Crypto trades around the clock with no official open or close, so every daily candle rests on a chosen boundary and every position is exposed at every hour. Continuous access is a feature of the market’s structure, with costs as well as conveniences.
