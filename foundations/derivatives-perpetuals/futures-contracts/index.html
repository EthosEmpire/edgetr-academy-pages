Futures Contracts

A futures contract is a standardised agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a set future date, traded on an exchange and backed by margin.

Why it matters

Futures are among the oldest and most widely used exchange-traded derivatives. The crypto market’s perpetual contracts are a variation on them, and ideas such as margin, basis and funding only make sense once the plain futures contract is clear.

How it works

A futures contract is a standardised agreement to buy or sell a specified quantity of an asset at a price agreed today, on a specified date in the future. The side that agrees to buy is long. The side that agrees to sell is short. See long position and short position.

Nobody pays for the asset when the contract is opened. Instead, both sides deposit margin, a form of collateral that shows they can cover losses. The general idea of margin is covered in margin and collateral.

In plain words

Imagine agreeing in March to buy 100 litres of heating oil in November at a price fixed today. If oil is more expensive by November, the agreement turned out well for the buyer. If it is cheaper, it turned out well for the seller. Either way, both sides are bound by the price they agreed.

What a contract specifies

The exchange writes these terms, not the traders. Standard terms are what make a future tradable: anyone holding a contract can leave by taking the opposite trade with anyone else. In practice most positions are closed this way and never reach delivery.

Daily settlement and the clearing house

On an exchange, a clearing house stands in the middle of every trade. It becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, so neither side depends on the other directly.

At the end of each trading day the exchange publishes a settlement price. Gains and losses against that price are moved between margin accounts straight away. This is called marking to market. If losses push an account below the required minimum, the holder must add funds or the position is reduced. The derivatives-specific rules are explained in margin on derivatives.

How a contract ends

With cash settlement, the difference between the agreed price and the final settlement price is paid and nothing else happens. With physical delivery, the seller hands over the asset and the buyer pays for it. Many commodity futures allow delivery. Many crypto futures are cash-settled, and some are margined and settled in the cryptocurrency itself, which changes how gains and losses are calculated. Exact rules differ between exchanges.

Going deeper

Futures price and spot price

A future usually trades at a slightly different price from the asset’s current, or spot, price. The gap is called the basis, and it shrinks toward zero as expiry approaches. See basis, contango and backwardation.

Rolling

Someone who wants exposure beyond the expiry date has to close the expiring contract and open a later one. This is called rolling, and the price difference between the two contracts is a cost or a gain. Perpetual futures were designed to remove this step.

Three days of daily settlementHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A buyer and a seller open one cash-settled futures contract at 100. The contract covers 10 units, so its value is 1,000. Each side posts 100 of margin.

Day Settlement price Buyer’s margin Seller’s margin
Open 100 100 100
1 103 130 70
2 101 110 90
3 (expiry) 104 140 60

Each move of 1 in the price is worth 10. Over the life of the contract the price rose by 4, so the buyer gained 40 and the seller lost 40. The daily transfers (+30, -20, +30) add up to the same 40.

What it does NOT mean

  • A futures price is not a forecast of the future spot price. It reflects today’s spot price adjusted for financing, storage, income and demand for the contract.
  • Buying a future is not buying the asset. The holder owns an obligation under a contract, not the underlying.
  • An expiry date does not mean the position must be held until then. It can be closed at any time the market is open.
  • Margin is not the maximum loss. In many futures markets a loss can exceed the margin posted, and the holder owes the difference. Rules vary by venue.
  • Standardised does not mean identical everywhere. Each exchange sets its own contract size, expiry schedule and settlement method.

Common mistakes

  1. Overlooking the expiry date. A dated future ends whether or not the holder is ready.
  2. Misreading the contract size. One contract can cover far more than one unit of the underlying.
  3. Ignoring the settlement method. Cash settlement and delivery have very different consequences.
  4. Treating the gap to spot as a pricing error. A difference between futures and spot is normal and has known causes.

Risks and limitations

  • Futures are leveraged: gains and losses are calculated on the full contract value. See leverage.
  • Daily settlement means losses must be covered in cash immediately, not at expiry.
  • Contracts with distant expiry dates are often thinly traded.
  • Physically delivered contracts create real delivery obligations if held to the end.
  • Contract terms and customer protections differ between exchanges and jurisdictions.

Key takeaway

A futures contract fixes a price today for a trade on a set future date. It is standardised, backed by margin, settled daily and ends either in a cash payment or in delivery.
