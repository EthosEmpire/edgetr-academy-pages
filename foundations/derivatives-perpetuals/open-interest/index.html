Open Interest

Open interest is the total number, or value, of derivative contracts that are currently open and have not yet been closed or settled.

Why it matters

Open interest appears on almost every derivatives data page, usually next to price and volume. It answers a simple question that price cannot: how many contracts exist right now. It is also widely over-read, because a large number looks like it should mean something about direction.

How it works

Open interest (OI) is the number of derivative contracts that are open at a given moment: opened, and not yet closed, expired or liquidated. It applies to futures, perpetual futures and options.

Each contract is counted once, not once per side. Exchanges report the figure in contracts, in units of the underlying asset, or as a currency value. When it is shown as a currency value, it changes whenever the price changes, even if nobody opens or closes anything.

In plain words

Think of a hall where people play chess. Open interest is the number of games in progress. It goes up when two people sit down to start a new game and down when a game ends. If a player hands their seat to someone else, the number of games stays the same. And the count tells you nothing about who is winning any of them.

What makes it rise and fall

Open interest rises only when both sides of a trade are opening new positions. It falls only when both sides are closing. When a position simply passes from one holder to another, nothing changes. Liquidations and expiries also remove contracts.

Open interest and volume

Volume measures activity. Open interest measures what is left standing after that activity. A day can have very high volume and no change in open interest if traders mostly opened and closed within the day.

How it is commonly interpreted

Traders often read a change in open interest together with the change in price:

Price Open interest Common reading
Rising Rising New positions are being opened during the advance
Rising Falling Positions are being closed during the advance, often shorts buying back
Falling Rising New positions are being opened during the decline
Falling Falling Positions are being closed during the decline, often longs selling or being liquidated

These readings are conventions. They describe what kind of activity accompanied a move that has already happened.

Why it does not predict direction

  • Every open contract has one long and one short. Rising open interest means more longs and exactly as many more shorts. It cannot show which side is “winning”.
  • It does not show who started the trade. The data does not reveal whether buyers or sellers were the more urgent side.
  • It does not show intent. A hedger offsetting a spot holding and a speculator with no other position look identical.
  • It measures size, not direction. A high figure means a lot of leveraged exposure exists on both sides.

Going deeper

High open interest relative to a market’s liquidity means more positions could be forcibly closed if price moves sharply, which is one ingredient of a liquidation cascade. It describes how much fuel is present, not whether, when or in which direction anything happens.

Practitioners usually view open interest alongside the funding rate, which shows which side has been paying to hold. In dated futures, open interest in a contract typically shrinks as expiry approaches and positions are closed or rolled into later contracts.

Four trades, step by stepHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical market, starting with no open contracts.

  1. Ana buys 5 contracts from Ben. Both are new. OI = 5.
  2. Cal buys 3 contracts from Dee. Both are new. OI = 8.
  3. Ana sells 2 of her contracts to Eve. Ana is closing, Eve is new. OI = 8.
  4. Ben buys back 3 contracts from Cal. Both are closing. OI = 5.

At the end, the longs are Ana 3 and Eve 2, and the shorts are Ben 2 and Dee 3: five on each side. Volume across the four trades was 5 + 3 + 2 + 3 = 13 contracts, while open interest finished at 5.

What it does NOT mean

  • Rising open interest is not bullish, and it is not bearish. New longs and new shorts are created in equal numbers.
  • Open interest does not show which side is ahead. It counts contracts, not profits.
  • Open interest is not volume. One counts trades in a period; the other counts positions that remain.
  • A change in currency-valued open interest does not always mean trading. A price move alone changes the figure.
  • High open interest does not mean a cascade is coming. It describes exposure, not timing.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading open interest alone as a direction. The figure has no direction in it.
  2. Mixing units. Contracts, coins and currency value give different pictures of the same market.
  3. Comparing exchanges without care. Contract sizes and reporting methods differ.
  4. Treating the price and open interest table as rules. It lists common readings, each with frequent exceptions.

Risks and limitations

  • The data is reported by exchanges and cannot be verified from outside.
  • Aggregated figures cover only the venues a data site tracks, and definitions differ between them.
  • Open interest says nothing about who holds the positions or how much leverage each uses.
  • Like every market statistic, it describes the present and the past, not the future.

Key takeaway

Open interest counts the contracts that are currently open. Because every contract has one long and one short, it measures how much exposure exists, not which way the market is leaning.

Sources and further reading
