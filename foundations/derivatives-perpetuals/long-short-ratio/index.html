Long/Short Ratio

The long/short ratio compares long and short positioning in a derivatives market, usually by counting accounts, which is not the same as measuring position size.

Why it matters

The long/short ratio is shown on many exchange data pages and dashboards, and its name suggests something it cannot be: a measure of whether “more of the market” is long or short. Understanding what is really counted prevents one of the most common misreadings of derivatives data.

How it works

Start with a fact that holds in every derivatives market. Each open contract has one long side and one short side. The total number of contracts held long therefore always equals the total number held short. Measured in contracts, the ratio of long to short is always exactly 1.

A published long/short ratio that is not 1 must be measuring something else. Most often it counts accounts:

Long/short ratio = accounts that are net long / accounts that are net shortA value of 2.0 means two accounts are net long for every one that is net short. It is also shown as percentages, here 66.7% and 33.3%.

In plain words

Picture a see-saw with eighty small children on one end and twenty adults on the other, perfectly level. Counting heads gives 80 against 20. Weighing the two ends gives a tie. An account-based long/short ratio counts heads. It does not weigh anyone.

What the count hides

An account ratio above 1 means that the long side is held by more accounts, and so by smaller positions on average. The short side is held by fewer accounts with larger positions. Both sides hold the same number of contracts.

Three numbers, one name

  • The account ratio covers every account with an open position on one exchange.
  • A top trader ratio covers only the largest accounts. Within a subset of the market, long and short positions need not be equal, because the rest of the market holds the difference.
  • A taker buy/sell ratio measures trading flow in a period, not positions held. It belongs with volume data.

How it is commonly interpreted

A high account ratio is often described as “most traders are long” or “retail is long”. Some traders treat extreme readings as contrarian, on the idea that the crowd tends to be wrong at turning points, a theme related to herd behavior.

Why it does not predict direction

  • It counts accounts, not position size. The smaller number of accounts on the other side holds exactly as much.
  • It does not show intent. Large accounts on one side may be market makers or hedgers whose positions are offset elsewhere, for example by holding the asset on spot. Their net exposure may be close to zero.
  • It covers one exchange. A trader can be long on one venue and short on another.
  • One person can hold several accounts. The count is of accounts, not of people.
  • It can stay extreme for a long time. A lopsided ratio may persist through a long advance or a long decline.

Going deeper

Practitioners who look at this ratio usually set it beside open interest, which shows how much exposure exists, and the funding rate, which shows which side is paying to hold. The three describe positioning from different angles. None of them contains direction, and they are built from overlapping data, so agreement between them is weaker evidence than it looks.

A crowded side with high leverage is one ingredient of a liquidation cascade. The ratio cannot show leverage, so by itself it does not reveal that either.

A ratio that moves while positioning barely changesHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. On one exchange, 100 accounts hold positions in a perpetual contract.

  • 80 accounts are net long, with 5 contracts each: 400 contracts.
  • 20 accounts are net short, with 20 contracts each: 400 contracts.

The account ratio is 80 / 20 = 4.0, often shown as “80% long”. In contracts, the two sides are equal.

Now 20 new accounts each buy 1 contract, and one existing short account sells 20 more contracts to them, raising its short from 20 to 40.

  • Long: 100 accounts, 420 contracts.
  • Short: 20 accounts, 420 contracts.

The ratio jumps from 4.0 to 5.0. Open contracts rose by only 20, from 400 to 420, and they are still perfectly balanced.

What it does NOT mean

  • A ratio above 1 does not mean more money is long than short. In contracts, the two sides are always equal.
  • It is not a bullish or a bearish reading. It describes how positions are spread across accounts.
  • It is not a contrarian timing tool. Extreme readings can last, and nothing in the number marks a turning point.
  • One exchange’s ratio is not the whole market. Other venues and spot holdings are invisible to it.
  • “Top trader” does not mean skilled trader. It usually means an account with a large balance.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading an account ratio as a position ratio. The first counts holders, the second would always be 1.
  2. Comparing figures from different sources. Each exchange and data site uses its own definition.
  3. Confusing positioning with flow. A taker buy/sell ratio describes recent trades, not open positions.
  4. Treating an extreme as a trigger. The number carries no information about timing.

Risks and limitations

  • The data is reported by each exchange and cannot be checked from outside.
  • Methods can change without notice, which breaks comparisons over time.
  • Sub-accounts, hedged positions and activity on other venues distort the picture.
  • It shows nothing about leverage, entry prices or the size of individual positions.

Key takeaway

The long/short ratio usually counts accounts, not contracts. Since every contract has one long and one short, it shows how positions are spread among holders, not which way the market is leaning.

Sources and further reading

  • Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives, John C. Hull. Establishes that every futures contract has one long and one short side
  • Commitments of Traders: Explanatory Notes (opens in a new tab), U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission. Regulator statement that aggregate long open interest equals aggregate short open interest
