Liquidation Cascades

A liquidation cascade is a chain reaction in which forced closures of leveraged positions push the price further, which triggers more forced closures.

Why it matters

Some of the sharpest moves in leveraged markets are not caused by news or by a change of opinion. They are caused by the mechanics of liquidation feeding on itself. Understanding the mechanism explains why such moves are fast, why they overshoot and why they cannot be timed.

Risk

In a cascade, leveraged positions can lose their full margin within seconds, and stop orders may fill far from their trigger price. Regulators’ investor-education pages warn that leveraged trading amplifies losses and that traders may lose more than their initial investment.

How it works

A liquidation is an order that nobody chose to place. The exchange’s engine has to close the position, so it sells a liquidated long, or buys back a liquidated short, at whatever price the market offers.

One such order is small. The problem arises when many positions have liquidation prices close together. The first forced orders move the price into the next group of liquidation prices, whose forced orders move it into the group after that.

In plain words

Stand one domino on a table and knock it over: nothing else happens. Stand a hundred close together and the first one brings down the rest. A cascade needs the dominoes to be near each other. In a market, the dominoes are leveraged positions and the spacing is the distance between their liquidation prices.

What it looks like

On a chart, a cascade tends to appear as a few very large candles in one direction, often with a spike in volume. In derivatives data it often coincides with a sharp drop in open interest, as positions are closed, and a swing in funding. These are descriptions after the fact.

What makes a market vulnerable

The common thread is how much forced order flow could appear compared with how much the order book can absorb. Liquidity matters as much as leverage: the same liquidations move a thin market much further than a deep one.

Both directions

When shorts are forced to buy back in a rising market, the result overlaps with a short squeeze. The mechanism is the mirror image.

Going deeper

How a cascade ends

A cascade stops when the forced orders run out, or when voluntary orders on the other side absorb them. Insurance funds and auto-deleveraging handle losses that exceed the margin. Many traditional markets also pause trading after very large moves. Many crypto venues trade continuously with no such pause, although some apply price limits. Rules differ between exchanges.

Estimated liquidation levels

Some data sites publish maps of where liquidations are “expected”. These are models: they assume typical leverage and entry prices and estimate where liquidation prices might be. They are not a record of real positions, which only each exchange can see.

Spillover

Forced selling in perpetuals can pull the contract below the spot index, and arbitrage then carries part of the pressure into spot markets. Coin-margined collateral adds to the effect, because it loses value during the very move that is causing the losses.

Three clusters of liquidation pricesHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical and heavily simplified. Assume every 100 contracts of forced selling move the price down by 1. Leveraged longs have liquidation prices in three clusters: 200 contracts at 97, 300 at 95 and 400 at 92.

  1. Ordinary selling takes the price from 100 to 97.
  2. At 97, 200 contracts are liquidated. Price falls 2, to 95.
  3. At 95, 300 contracts are liquidated. Price falls 3, to 92.
  4. At 92, 400 contracts are liquidated. Price falls 4, to 88.
  5. No clusters remain below. Forced selling stops.

Ordinary selling moved the price by 3. Forced selling of 900 contracts moved it by a further 9. Had the clusters been spread far apart, step 2 would not have reached step 3.

What it does NOT mean

  • A cascade is not proof of manipulation. The loop can run on mechanics alone. It also does not rule manipulation out.
  • High leverage in a market does not mean a cascade is due. Vulnerable conditions can last for a long time without incident.
  • Liquidation maps are not maps of real orders. They are estimates built on assumptions.
  • A cascade says nothing certain about an asset’s long-term value. It reflects positioning and liquidity at one moment.
  • The end of a cascade is not a floor or a ceiling. Price may recover, pause or continue.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating estimated liquidation levels as known facts. Nobody outside the exchange sees the real positions.
  2. Assuming cascades only hit longs. Shorts are liquidated the same way in rising markets.
  3. Reading a cascade as news about the asset. The cause is often structural, not fundamental.
  4. Expecting stop orders to fill at their trigger. In a cascade, slippage can be severe.

Risks and limitations

  • Stop orders become market orders and may fill far from the trigger price.
  • Exchanges can slow down or go offline at the moments of heaviest activity.
  • Auto-deleveraging can reduce profitable positions on the other side.
  • Positions with modest leverage can still be affected when price gaps through several levels at once. See leverage risk.

Key takeaway

A liquidation cascade is forced closing that causes more forced closing. It depends on leverage, crowding and thin liquidity, and it describes how a move unfolds, not when one starts or where one ends.
