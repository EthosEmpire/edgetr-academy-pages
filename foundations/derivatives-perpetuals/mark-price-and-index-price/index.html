Mark Price & Index Price

The index price is an average of spot prices across several exchanges, and the mark price is the reference price built from it that is used for unrealized P&L and liquidation.

Why it matters

A perpetual contract shows more than one price, and they do different jobs. On most exchanges a position is liquidated according to the mark price, not according to the last trade shown on the chart. Knowing which price does what removes a common source of confusion about gains, losses and forced closures.

How it works

The last price is simply the most recent trade in the contract’s own order book. It is where orders are filled, and it is what most charts display.

The index price is an average, often a weighted one, of the asset’s spot price on several exchanges. It represents the wider market for the asset, not the trading in one contract.

The mark price is the exchange’s estimate of what the contract is fairly worth at this moment. It is usually derived from the index price, with a smoothed adjustment for how far the contract has been trading from the index. Unrealized gains and losses, margin checks and liquidation are all based on it.

In plain words

Imagine a heating system controlled by one thermometer that sits next to the oven. Every time someone cooks, the heating shuts off. A better system averages several thermometers around the house, so one hot spot cannot fool it. The index price is that average, and the mark price is the reading the system acts on.

Why exchanges do not use the last price

An order book can be thin. One large market order, or a deliberate attempt to move the price, can push the last price far from the rest of the market for a moment. If liquidations followed the last price, positions could be closed because of a move that existed on one venue for one second.

The mark price is harder to move, because it leans on spot prices from several exchanges. For widely traded assets, shifting it generally means shifting the wider market.

Which price is used for what

Price Where it comes from Typically used for
Last price Trades in the contract’s own order book Order fills, charts, realized P&L
Index price Spot prices on several exchanges Building the mark price, the funding rate
Mark price Index price plus an adjustment Unrealized P&L, margin checks, liquidation

Many exchanges let a stop order be triggered by either the last price or the mark price. Exact rules differ between exchanges.

Going deeper

How an index is built

Each exchange chooses which spot venues go into its index and how much weight each one gets. Most add safeguards: a venue whose price strays far from the others, or whose data feed stalls, may be given less weight or dropped for a while. The list of venues can change over time.

Unrealized and realized results

Unrealized P&L is shown at the mark price. When a position is closed, the result depends on the prices at which the closing orders really fill, which is the last-price world. The figure on screen and the final result can therefore differ, sometimes noticeably in fast markets. See P&L: realized and unrealized.

What the mark price cannot do

It filters out moves that happen in one order book. It does not filter out moves in the whole market. If the asset falls on every spot exchange, the index falls, the mark price follows and leveraged positions are liquidated as usual.

A dip that did not liquidate, and a fall that didHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. An index uses three spot exchanges with equal weight. Their prices are 100.2, 99.8 and 100.0, so the index is (100.2 + 99.8 + 100.0) / 3 = 100.0. The mark price is 100.1. A long position has a liquidation price of 95.

Case A. A large sell order hits the contract’s order book. The last price touches 94 and returns to 100 within moments. The spot exchanges barely move, the index stays near 100 and so does the mark price. The position is not liquidated.

Case B. The asset falls on all three spot exchanges to about 94.9. The index and the mark price follow. The mark price is now below 95 and the position is liquidated.

What it does NOT mean

  • The mark price is not the price orders fill at. Buying and selling happen at order book prices.
  • The mark price does not protect a position from real moves. It only ignores moves confined to one order book.
  • The index is not “the true price”. It is an average of venues chosen by the exchange, using the exchange’s weights.
  • Mark prices are not identical across exchanges. A liquidation price worked out on one venue does not carry over to another.
  • A chart wick through a liquidation price does not prove a liquidation happened. Most charts show the last price, not the mark price.

Common mistakes

  1. Judging the distance to liquidation on a last-price chart. The relevant line is the mark price.
  2. Assuming a stop order and a liquidation use the same price. A stop may be set to trigger on the last price while liquidation follows the mark price.
  3. Expecting unrealized P&L to equal the final result. Closing happens at fill prices.
  4. Assuming the index never changes. Venues and weights are revised by the exchange.

Risks and limitations

  • The formula and the list of index venues are set by the exchange and can be changed.
  • Assets that trade on few spot venues have weaker indexes that are easier to distort.
  • In extreme conditions the last price and the mark price can sit far apart, so a position may close at a much worse price than the mark price suggested.
  • Data outages at index venues can affect the mark price.

Key takeaway

The index price is an average of spot markets, and the mark price is the reference built on it. Orders fill at the last price, but unrealized P&L and liquidation follow the mark price.
