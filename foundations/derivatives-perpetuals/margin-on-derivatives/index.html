Margin on Derivatives

Margin on derivatives is the collateral posted to open and keep a position: initial margin opens it, and maintenance margin is the minimum needed to keep it open.

Why it matters

Margin decides how large a position can be, how far price can move against it and when the exchange steps in. The general idea is covered in margin and collateral. This page covers the layer that is specific to futures and perpetuals: two margin levels, a balance that changes every moment and rules set by each exchange.

Risk

Margin is what stands behind a leveraged derivative, and all of it can be lost quickly: a move of a few percent against a highly leveraged position is enough. Regulators’ investor-education pages warn that leveraged trading amplifies losses and that traders may lose more than their initial investment.

How it works

In spot margin trading, margin backs a loan. On a derivative nothing is borrowed. Margin is a deposit that shows the holder can cover losses on the contract.

Initial margin is the collateral required to open a position. It is the position value divided by the leverage: a position worth 1,000 at 10x needs 100.

Maintenance margin is the minimum that must remain behind the position to keep it open. It is lower than the initial margin and is usually set as a percentage of the position value, called the maintenance margin rate.

The margin balance is what currently stands behind the position. It starts at the margin posted and then moves with unrealized gains and losses, which are measured at the mark price.

In plain words

Think of the deposit paid when hiring a car. The deposit is not the price of the car. It is what the owner holds against damage. If the estimated damage creeps up toward the size of the deposit, the owner takes the car back before the deposit is used up. Initial margin is the deposit, and maintenance margin is the point where the owner steps in.

How the numbers relate

Margin balance = margin posted + unrealized P&L - funding and fees paidWhen the margin balance falls to the maintenance margin, liquidation begins. Exact definitions differ between exchanges.

In traditional futures markets, a balance that drops below the maintenance level leads to a margin call: a demand to add funds. On most crypto perpetual venues there is no call. The position is handed straight to the liquidation engine, as described in liquidation on perpetuals.

Margin tiers

Most exchanges raise the maintenance margin rate, and lower the maximum leverage, as a position gets larger. The numbers above are invented to show the shape. Real tables differ between exchanges and between contracts.

Going deeper

Isolated and cross

With isolated margin, a fixed amount is assigned to one position. With cross margin, the whole account balance stands behind all positions. The trade-offs are covered in isolated and cross margin.

The currency of the collateral

Some contracts take a stablecoin as margin. Others take the underlying coin itself. With coin margin, the collateral loses value at the same time as a long position loses money, so a falling price hurts twice. Some venues accept several assets as collateral and count volatile ones at less than their market value.

Unrealized gains as margin

In cross margin, unrealized gains on one position can support others. Those gains are not locked in. They can disappear with the next price move and take the support with them.

Following one margin balanceHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A trader opens a long of 10 contracts at 100, a position worth 1,000, at 10x. Initial margin is 100. The maintenance margin rate is 0.5%, so maintenance margin is 5. To keep things simple, it stays at 5 as price moves.

Event Change Margin balance
Position opened 100.00
Mark price falls to 97 -30.00 70.00
Funding paid over one day -0.30 69.70
Mark price falls to 91 -60.00 9.70
Mark price falls to 90.53 -4.70 5.00

At about 90.5 the balance equals the maintenance margin and liquidation begins. That is a fall of roughly 9.5%, not the 10% that “10x” might suggest, and funding brought the point slightly closer.

What it does NOT mean

  • Margin is not a fee. It is collateral, and whatever is not lost remains the holder’s.
  • Margin is not the size of the position. Gains and losses are calculated on the full position value.
  • Reaching maintenance margin does not mean a polite request for more funds. On most crypto venues it starts an automatic liquidation.
  • The leverage setting does not change what a price move is worth. A 1% move on a 1,000 position is 10 at any leverage. The setting changes how much margin stands behind it.
  • Margin requirements are not the same everywhere. They vary by exchange, by contract and by position size.

Common mistakes

  1. Looking at the margin instead of the position value. The exposure is the full position.
  2. Forgetting that funding and fees come out of the margin balance. They bring the liquidation point closer over time.
  3. Assuming liquidation needs a move of 100 divided by the leverage. Maintenance margin makes the real distance shorter.
  4. Overlooking the collateral currency. Coin-margined collateral shrinks as price falls.
  5. Counting unrealized gains as secure collateral. They can reverse.

Risks and limitations

  • The full margin behind a position can be lost. See leverage risk.
  • In cross margin, one losing position can draw on the balance that supports every other position.
  • Exchanges can change margin tiers and leverage limits, sometimes at short notice.
  • In some futures markets, losses can exceed the margin posted. Rules vary by venue and jurisdiction.

Key takeaway

Initial margin opens a derivative position and maintenance margin is the floor that keeps it open. The margin balance between them moves with every price change, and when it reaches the floor the exchange takes over.
