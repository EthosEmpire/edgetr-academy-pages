Options Basics

An option is a contract that gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a set strike price by a set date.

Why it matters

Options are the second large family of derivatives after futures, and they behave differently in one crucial way: the two sides of the contract do not carry the same risk. Knowing who has a right and who has an obligation is the foundation for everything else about them.

Risk

Options can be leveraged derivatives. A bought option can lose its entire premium, and a sold option can lose many times the premium received, with margin that can be wiped out quickly. Regulators’ investor-education pages warn that leveraged trading amplifies losses and that traders may lose more than their initial investment.

How it works

An option is a contract between a buyer and a seller. The buyer pays a price, called the premium, and receives a right:

  • A call gives the right, not the obligation, to buy the underlying asset at a fixed price.
  • A put gives the right, not the obligation, to sell the underlying asset at a fixed price.

The fixed price is the strike price. The right lasts until the expiry date. Using the right is called exercising the option. If exercising makes no sense, the buyer lets the option expire and loses only the premium.

This is the key difference from a futures contract, which binds both sides to the trade.

In plain words

A buyer pays a small fee to reserve a house at an agreed price for three months. If house prices rise, the reservation is valuable and the buyer uses it. If prices fall, the buyer walks away and loses only the fee. That is a call. A put works like an insurance policy: a premium is paid, and it pays out if the insured thing loses value.

The vocabulary

Term Meaning
Premium The price of the option, paid by the buyer to the seller
Strike price The price at which the underlying can be bought or sold
Expiry The date the right ends
In the money Exercising now would be worth something: a call above its strike, a put below it
Out of the money Exercising now would be worth nothing
American style Can be exercised at any time up to expiry
European style Can be exercised only at expiry

Options may be settled by delivering the asset or in cash. Contract size, exercise style and settlement differ between exchanges.

Buyer and seller

The buyer’s position is simple: the premium is the most that can be lost.

The seller takes the other side. In return for the premium, the seller must honour the contract if the buyer exercises. The seller of a call who does not own the asset faces a loss with no upper limit, because a price has no ceiling. The seller of a put must buy at the strike even if the asset has collapsed. Sellers post margin and can be liquidated.

What the buyer’s result looks like

Call result at expiry = max(price - strike, 0) - premiumPut result at expiry = max(strike - price, 0) - premium. The seller's result is the exact opposite.

Going deeper

What an option’s price is made of

A premium has two parts. Intrinsic value is what the option would be worth if exercised now. Time value is everything else: what buyers pay for the chance that the option becomes more valuable before expiry.

Time value depends on the time remaining and on how much the underlying is expected to move, its volatility. All else equal, time value shrinks as expiry approaches. This is called time decay.

Implied volatility

The level of volatility that would explain current option prices is called implied volatility. It describes how much movement the market is pricing in. It carries no information about the direction of that movement.

What options are used for

A put can act as protection for an asset already held, a form of hedging. Options are also used to take a view with a known maximum loss for the buyer. Sellers receive premiums for carrying the risk that buyers pay to remove.

A call and a put at the same strikeHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. An asset trades at 100. A one-month call with a strike of 100 costs 5 per unit.

Price at expiry Value of exercising Buyer’s result Seller’s result
120 20 +15 -15
105 5 0 0
102 2 -3 +3
90 0 (not exercised) -5 +5

A one-month put with a strike of 100 costs 4. If the price is 85 at expiry, exercising is worth 15 and the buyer’s result is 15 - 4 = +11. At any price of 100 or more, the put expires unused and the buyer loses 4.

What it does NOT mean

  • An option is not an obligation for its buyer. Only the seller is bound.
  • A limited loss is not a small risk. Losing 100% of the premium is an ordinary outcome for a bought option.
  • Selling options is not collecting free premiums. The premium is payment for taking a risk that can be many times larger.
  • A cheap option is not a bargain. A low premium usually reflects a low chance that the option ends up worth anything.
  • Implied volatility is not a forecast of direction. It prices the size of possible moves, not which way they go.

Common mistakes

  1. Forgetting time decay. A bought option loses time value every day, even if price does not move.
  2. Leaving the premium out of the break-even. A call needs the price above the strike plus the premium.
  3. Overlooking exercise style and settlement. They decide when and how the contract can end.
  4. Underestimating the seller’s exposure. The loss on a sold option is not capped by the premium.

Risks and limitations

  • A sold option can produce losses far larger than the premium, with margin calls or liquidation on the way.
  • Being right about direction is not enough: the move has to be large enough and arrive before expiry.
  • Many option contracts are thinly traded, with a wide bid-ask spread.
  • Availability to retail customers is restricted in some jurisdictions.

Key takeaway

A call is the right to buy at the strike price and a put is the right to sell. The buyer risks only the premium, while the seller takes on an obligation whose possible loss can be far larger.
