Liquidation on Perpetuals

Liquidation on perpetuals is the forced closing of a leveraged position by the exchange when its margin balance, measured at the mark price, falls to the maintenance margin.

Why it matters

Liquidation is the event that turns a leveraged loss into a closed position. The general concept is explained in liquidation. This page covers what is specific to perpetuals: the price that triggers it, what the exchange’s engine does next and who covers the loss if the close goes badly.

Risk

A leveraged perpetual position can lose its full margin in minutes, and in cross margin a liquidation can consume the whole account balance. Regulators’ investor-education pages warn that leveraged trading amplifies losses and that traders may lose more than their initial investment.

What it looks like

A long is liquidated when price falls far enough. A short is liquidated when price rises far enough. In both cases the trigger is measured at the mark price, not at the last trade on the chart.

How it works

Liquidation starts when the position’s margin balance falls to its maintenance margin. Both terms are explained in margin on derivatives. From that moment the exchange’s liquidation engine owns the decision. Many exchanges close large positions in steps, called partial liquidation, and many charge a liquidation fee.

In plain words

A pawnbroker lends against a watch and watches its market value. If the value drops close to the size of the loan, the pawnbroker sells the watch at once, at whatever price buyers offer that day. The owner is not asked. Liquidation works the same way: the exchange closes the position while the collateral still roughly covers the loss.

The key prices

The liquidation price is the mark price at which the margin balance equals the maintenance margin. The bankruptcy price is the price at which the margin would be completely used up.

Long: liquidation price = entry x (1 - 1 / leverage + maintenance margin rate)Short: entry x (1 + 1 / leverage - maintenance margin rate). A simplified version for isolated margin that ignores fees and funding.

For a 10x long from 100 with a 0.5% maintenance rate: 100 x (1 - 0.10 + 0.005) = 90.5. For a 10x short: 100 x (1 + 0.10 - 0.005) = 109.5.

When the close goes badly

If the engine closes the position at a better price than the bankruptcy price, some margin is left over. Depending on the exchange, it goes to an insurance fund, is partly returned to the holder, or both.

If the market is moving fast and the close happens beyond the bankruptcy price, the loss is larger than the margin. The insurance fund normally covers the difference. If the fund is not enough, many exchanges use auto-deleveraging (ADL): profitable positions on the other side, usually ranked by profit and leverage, are reduced at the bankruptcy price. Exact rules differ between exchanges.

Going deeper

Liquidation and stop losses

A stop loss is an exit chosen in advance by the holder. Liquidation is an exit forced by the exchange at the edge of the collateral. They are compared in stop loss vs liquidation.

The liquidation price moves

Funding payments, fees, added or removed margin and changes in margin tier all shift the liquidation price. In cross margin it also moves whenever the account balance or any other position changes.

One liquidation, two outcomesHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers on a hypothetical exchange. A long of 10 contracts is opened at 100 with isolated margin of 100. Maintenance margin is 5, so the liquidation price is 90.5 and the bankruptcy price is 90.

The mark price falls to 90.5. The loss is 95, the margin balance is 5 and the engine takes over.

  • Orderly market. The position is closed at 90.3. The loss is 9.7 x 10 = 97. The remaining 3 goes to fees and the insurance fund.
  • Fast market. The position is closed at 89.5. The loss is 10.5 x 10 = 105, which is 5 more than the margin. The insurance fund covers the 5.

In both cases the holder’s 100 of margin is gone.

What it does NOT mean

  • Liquidation is not a stop loss. It happens at the edge of the collateral, not at a level chosen as part of a plan.
  • The liquidation price is not fixed. Funding, fees and margin changes move it.
  • A 10x position does not need a 10% move to be liquidated. Maintenance margin and costs make the distance shorter.
  • Liquidation is not a verdict on the idea behind the trade. Price can recover straight afterwards. Liquidation is about margin, not about direction.
  • An insurance fund is not insurance for the liquidated holder. It protects the other side of the market from unpaid losses.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating the liquidation price as the planned exit. It is the point where control is lost.
  2. Measuring the distance with the last price. The trigger is the mark price.
  3. Forgetting what cross margin shares. One position can drain the collateral behind all the others.
  4. Ignoring the slow drift. Funding moves the liquidation price a little at every interval.

Risks and limitations

  • In fast markets the engine may close at prices far from the liquidation price.
  • On some venues a trader can owe more than the margin posted. Check whether the venue offers negative-balance protection.
  • Auto-deleveraging can reduce a profitable position without the holder’s consent.
  • Exchange outages and delays tend to occur at the busiest moments.
  • Many liquidations at once can feed a liquidation cascade.
  • Procedures, fees and what happens to leftover margin differ between exchanges. See leverage risk.

Key takeaway

On a perpetual, liquidation is triggered when the margin balance, measured at the mark price, falls to the maintenance margin. From that point the exchange closes the position on its own terms.
