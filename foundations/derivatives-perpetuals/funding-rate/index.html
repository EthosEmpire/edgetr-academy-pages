Funding Rate

The funding rate is a periodic payment exchanged between long and short holders of a perpetual future that pulls the contract price toward the spot index.

Why it matters

Funding is the mechanism that keeps a perpetual future near the spot price, and it is a running cost or receipt for everyone holding one. It is also one of the most quoted numbers in crypto market data, and one of the most often mistaken for a forecast.

How it works

The funding rate is a percentage applied at regular intervals to every open perpetual position. Its sign decides who pays:

  • Positive rate: holders of longs pay holders of shorts. This is typical when the contract trades above the spot index.
  • Negative rate: holders of shorts pay holders of longs. This is typical when the contract trades below the index.

The payment passes between traders. It is not paid to the exchange, which earns trading fees separately.

In plain words

Think of a see-saw with a rule: whichever side has become heavier pays a small sum to the lighter side every few hours. The payment makes the heavy side slightly less comfortable and the light side slightly more attractive, which nudges the see-saw back toward level.

How it is calculated

Funding payment = position value x funding ratePosition value is normally measured at the mark price. A positive result is paid by longs and received by shorts.

The rate itself usually has two ingredients:

  • a premium component, which measures how far the contract has traded from the index price over the interval;
  • an interest component, a small fixed amount reflecting the difference in borrowing cost between the two currencies in the pair.

Many exchanges also cap the rate. Funding is often exchanged every 8 hours, sometimes every 4 hours or every hour, and on some venues it accrues continuously. On many exchanges only positions open at the funding time pay or receive. Formulas, caps and intervals differ between exchanges.

What it measures, and how it is read

Reading What it measures Common interpretation
Positive The contract has traded above the index Demand for leveraged long exposure has outweighed demand for short exposure
Near zero The contract has traded close to the index Positioning is roughly balanced
Negative The contract has traded below the index Demand for leveraged short exposure has outweighed demand for long exposure

Traders often watch funding as a gauge of crowding. A very high positive rate is commonly described as “crowded longs”, and a very negative one as “crowded shorts”.

Why it does not predict direction

  • It looks backward. The rate is computed from prices that have already traded.
  • It can stay one-sided for a long time. Funding may remain positive through a long advance and negative through a long decline.
  • Every contract has two sides. Funding shows which side is paying to hold its position, not which side is right.
  • Payers and receivers have mixed motives. A trader who is short the perpetual and holds the same amount of the asset on spot has no view on direction at all.

Going deeper

Funding and arbitrage

When funding is positive, some participants hold the asset on spot and short the perpetual in equal size, so that price moves cancel out and the funding is received. Their selling of the contract is part of what pulls it back toward the index. This is a form of arbitrage, and it is not free of risk: the short leg needs margin and can be liquidated, funding can change sign, and both legs depend on the venue. The same idea for dated futures is covered in basis, contango and backwardation.

Annualised figures

Data sites often convert a rate to a yearly figure. A rate of 0.01% every 8 hours is 0.03% a day, or about 10.95% a year (0.03% x 365). The conversion assumes the rate never changes, which is not how funding behaves.

Funding on one positionHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A trader holds a long of 10 contracts at a mark price of 100, so the position value is 1,000. The margin is 100. The funding rate is +0.01% every 8 hours.

  • Each interval, the long pays 1,000 x 0.01% = 0.10 to the shorts.
  • If nothing changed for 30 days, that would be 90 intervals x 0.10 = 9.00.
  • 9.00 is 0.9% of the position value, but 9% of the margin.

Had the rate been -0.01%, the long would have received 0.10 per interval instead. In both cases the position still gains or loses 10 for every move of 1 in the price, which dwarfs the funding.

What it does NOT mean

  • Positive funding is not a bullish reading, and it is not a bearish one. It records that the contract traded above the index. It does not say what happens next.
  • Funding is not a fee collected by the exchange. It moves from one group of traders to the other.
  • An extreme rate is not a timing tool. Crowded positioning can become more crowded before anything changes.
  • Receiving funding is not income without risk. The position that receives it still carries full price risk and liquidation risk.
  • The displayed rate is not fixed. Many exchanges show an estimate that keeps changing until the funding time.

Common mistakes

  1. Working out funding on the margin. It is charged on the position value, which is the margin multiplied by the leverage.
  2. Comparing rates without checking the interval. 0.01% per hour and 0.01% per 8 hours are very different costs.
  3. Reading funding in isolation. It is one description of positioning, alongside open interest and the long/short ratio, none of which shows direction.
  4. Forgetting it on long holds. Small payments repeat for as long as the position is open.

Risks and limitations

  • Rates can jump in volatile markets, up to the exchange’s cap.
  • Funding paid is taken from the margin balance, which moves a position closer to liquidation.
  • The sign can flip, turning a receipt into a cost.
  • Aggregated figures from data sites mix exchanges with different formulas and intervals.

Key takeaway

The funding rate shows which side of a perpetual market is paying the other to hold its position, and how much. It is a cost and a balancing mechanism, not a signal about where price goes next.
