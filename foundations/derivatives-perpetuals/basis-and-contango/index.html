Basis, Contango & Backwardation

Basis is the futures price minus the spot price: contango means futures trade above spot, and backwardation means futures trade below spot.

Why it matters

A futures contract almost never trades at exactly the same price as the asset it tracks. The gap has a name, known causes and a predictable end point at expiry. Understanding it explains why holding a future is not the same as holding the asset, and why a futures price is not a prediction.

How it works

Basis = futures price - spot priceThis site uses futures minus spot, the usual convention in crypto markets. Some textbooks define basis the other way round, as spot minus futures, so always check the sign.

The spot price is the price for immediate delivery. The basis is how far a futures price sits above or below it.

  • Contango: the futures price is above spot. Basis is positive.
  • Backwardation: the futures price is below spot. Basis is negative.

In plain words

Suppose you want a tonne of grain in six months. You could buy it today and store it, paying for the warehouse, the insurance and the money tied up. Or you could agree a price today for delivery in six months. The seller of that agreement has to carry those costs for you, so the agreed price is normally a little higher than today’s price. That built-in difference is contango.

Why futures differ from spot

The main driver is the cost of carry: what it costs to hold the asset until the expiry date.

Ingredient Effect on the futures price
Financing: interest on the money tied up Raises it
Storage and insurance, for physical goods Raises it
Income from holding the asset, such as dividends Lowers it
The benefit of having a scarce physical good on hand Lowers it
Futures price ≈ spot price x (1 + net cost of carry until expiry)A simplified form of the standard relationship. It ignores compounding.

Cryptocurrencies have no warehouse costs, so the basis on crypto futures mostly reflects interest rates and the demand for leveraged exposure. For commodities, storage and scarcity play a much larger part.

Convergence

At expiry a future settles at the spot price, so the basis must reach zero. Before then, arbitrage limits how wide it can get. If the future is far above spot, some participants buy the asset and sell the future, which pushes the two prices together. That trade is not free of risk: the short future needs margin throughout, money is tied up and both legs depend on the venues involved.

Going deeper

Annualised basis

To compare contracts with different expiry dates, basis is often expressed as a yearly percentage. With spot at 100 and a contract expiring in 90 days at 102, the basis is 2, or 2% of spot. Annualised, that is 2% x 365 / 90, or about 8.1%.

Perpetuals

A perpetual future has no expiry, so nothing forces its gap to spot to close on a set date. The funding rate does that job instead, and it plays a role similar to the basis on a dated contract.

How it is commonly interpreted

Contango is the ordinary state for an asset that costs something to hold and pays no income. A steep contango is commonly read as strong demand for leveraged long exposure, and backwardation as scarcity of the asset for immediate delivery or strong demand for short exposure. These readings describe current positioning and costs. The basis can widen, narrow or change sign without the spot price following any particular path.

A second meaning

Some academic texts use contango and backwardation to compare the futures price with the expected future spot price instead of today’s spot price. Market commentary almost always means the simpler comparison used on this page.

One basis, seen from three positionsHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. Spot is 100 and a six-month future trades at 103. The basis is +3: contango.

Spot is still 100 at expiry. The future settles at 100. The buyer of the future at 103 loses 3. The seller gains 3. A holder of spot is unchanged.

A holder who bought spot at 100 and sold the future at 103 ends with 3 whatever the final price is:

Final price Spot leg Futures leg Total
120 +20 -17 +3
80 -20 +23 +3

That 3 is the cost of carry being paid, before fees and financing. On the way to 120, the short future showed a loss of 17 that had to be covered by margin.

What it does NOT mean

  • Contango is not a bullish forecast. A higher futures price mostly reflects the cost of holding the asset until expiry.
  • Backwardation is not a bearish forecast. It reflects conditions today, such as scarcity or hedging demand.
  • A positive basis is not income without risk. Capturing it involves margin, financing, fees and dependence on venues.
  • Basis does not shrink smoothly. It can widen sharply before it converges.
  • The terms are not used identically everywhere. Sign conventions and definitions differ between sources.

Common mistakes

  1. Reading the futures price as the market’s prediction. It is today’s spot price adjusted for carry and positioning.
  2. Ignoring the sign convention. Futures minus spot and spot minus futures give opposite signs.
  3. Comparing raw basis across expiry dates. A gap of 2 over one month and over one year are very different.
  4. Forgetting convergence. In contango, a long future loses the basis by expiry if spot does not move.

Risks and limitations

  • Basis can move against a position before expiry, creating losses and margin demands along the way.
  • Settlement rules and reference prices differ between exchanges.
  • The cost-of-carry relationship is an approximation. It holds less tightly when arbitrage is difficult or a contract is thinly traded.

Key takeaway

Basis is the gap between a futures price and the spot price. Contango means the future is above spot, backwardation means it is below, and at expiry the gap closes whatever the price does.
