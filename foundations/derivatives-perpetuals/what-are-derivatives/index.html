What Are Derivatives?

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from the price of something else, called the underlying asset, rather than from owning that asset directly.

Why it matters

A large share of activity in both traditional and crypto markets takes place in contracts about assets, not in the assets themselves. Words such as futures, perpetuals, options, funding and open interest all belong to this world.

Knowing what a derivative is, and what it is not, removes much of the confusion around these products. It also explains why they behave so differently from simply owning something.

How it works

A derivative is a contract between two parties whose value is derived from the price of something else. That something is called the underlying asset, or simply the underlying. It can be a commodity such as wheat or oil, a share, a stock index, an interest rate, a currency or a cryptocurrency.

The holder of a derivative does not own the underlying. The holder owns a claim defined by the contract: which price it refers to, how large it is, when it ends and how it is settled.

In plain words

A farmer and a baker agree today on the price of wheat to be handed over in six months. No wheat changes hands today. Yet the agreement itself now has a value: if wheat becomes more expensive, the baker’s side of the deal looks better, and if wheat becomes cheaper, the farmer’s side does. That agreement is a derivative, and wheat is its underlying.

The main types

Type What the contract says Typical venue
Forward Buy or sell at a set price on a set future date, on custom terms Directly between two parties
Future The same idea with standard terms and daily settlement An exchange
Perpetual future A future that never expires and uses funding payments Mostly crypto exchanges
Option The buyer may trade at a set price but does not have to Exchanges and private deals
Swap Two parties exchange payment streams, for example fixed for floating interest Mostly private deals

What derivatives are used for

Hedging means reducing a risk that already exists. The farmer above fears falling prices and the baker fears rising ones, so the contract gives both a known price. See hedging basics.

Speculation means taking a view on price without owning the asset. The contract gains or loses value with the underlying, and nothing else offsets it.

Arbitrage means trading the gap between two related prices, such as a future and its underlying. This activity tends to keep derivative prices tied to the asset. See arbitrage.

Going deeper

One side’s gain is the other side’s loss

Before fees, the two sides of a derivative mirror each other: whatever one side gains on the contract, the other loses. This does not make the contract pointless. A hedger may be content with a loss on the contract because it is matched by a gain on what they own.

The other party matters

A contract is only as good as the ability of the other side to pay. On an exchange, a clearing house stands between buyers and sellers and collects margin from both. In private deals each party depends on the other directly. This is counterparty risk.

Leverage is usually built in

Most derivatives require only a fraction of the contract’s value as collateral. A small deposit therefore controls a much larger exposure, which is leverage. Gains and losses are calculated on the full contract value, not on the deposit.

A forward between a farmer and a bakerHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. Wheat trades at 100 per unit today. A farmer and a baker agree that in six months the baker will buy 10 units at 100 each.

  • Wheat is at 120 in six months. The baker pays 100 for something worth 120: a gain of 20 per unit, 200 in total. The farmer sells 200 below the market.
  • Wheat is at 80 in six months. The farmer receives 100 for something worth 80: a gain of 20 per unit, 200 in total. The baker pays 200 above the market.

In both cases the two results cancel out, and both parties got the certainty they wanted. A speculator holding either side with no farm and no bakery would simply have gained or lost 200.

What it does NOT mean

  • A derivative is not ownership of the underlying. A contract on a coin cannot be withdrawn to a wallet, and a contract on a share carries no vote.
  • Derivatives are not only tools for speculation. They grew out of the need of producers and buyers to fix prices in advance.
  • “Derivative” does not describe one product. A simple forward and a complex option structure share the name and little else.
  • A derivative’s price is not a forecast. It reflects today’s price of the underlying plus contract-specific costs, not knowledge of the future.
  • Hedging does not remove risk for free. It swaps one risk for another and gives up possible gains in exchange for certainty.

Common mistakes

  1. Confusing the contract with the asset. The contract has its own price, its own rules and its own way of ending.
  2. Skipping the contract terms. Size, expiry, settlement method and margin rules decide what the holder is really exposed to.
  3. Underestimating built-in leverage. A small deposit can stand behind a large exposure.
  4. Assuming every venue works the same way. Exact rules differ between exchanges and between countries.

Risks and limitations

  • Leverage means losses can be large relative to the money deposited, and can arrive quickly.
  • Every contract carries the risk that the other party, or the venue, fails.
  • Some derivatives are complex enough that their behaviour is hard to anticipate.
  • Rules vary by jurisdiction. Some derivatives are restricted or unavailable to retail customers in some countries.

Key takeaway

A derivative is a contract about an asset’s price, not the asset itself. Its value follows the underlying, its terms decide the risks, and it can be used to reduce risk or to take more of it.
