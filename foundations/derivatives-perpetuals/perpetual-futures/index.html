Perpetual Futures

A perpetual future is a futures contract with no expiry date that uses funding payments between longs and shorts to keep its price close to the spot index.

Why it matters

Perpetual futures are among the most heavily traded products in crypto. Funding rates, open interest, mark price and liquidations are all features of this one type of contract. Understanding how it is built explains why it behaves so differently from holding a coin.

Risk

Perpetuals are leveraged derivatives. A small price move in the wrong direction can wipe out the full margin of a position within minutes. Regulators’ investor-education pages warn that leveraged trading amplifies losses and that traders may lose more than their initial investment. This page explains the mechanism only.

How it works

A perpetual future, also called a perpetual swap or “perp”, is a derivative contract that tracks the price of an underlying asset and has no expiry date. A position can stay open for as long as its margin is sufficient. Nothing is ever delivered: gains and losses are settled in the margin currency.

A trader posts margin and opens a long or a short position. The contract has its own order book and its own price, set by its own buyers and sellers. Nothing forces that price to equal the spot price.

A dated future is tied to spot by its expiry: on the final day it settles at the spot price. A perpetual never expires, so it needs a different anchor.

The funding anchor

That anchor is funding: a payment exchanged at regular intervals between the holders of long positions and the holders of short positions. When the contract trades above the spot index, funding is typically positive and longs pay shorts. When it trades below, funding is typically negative and shorts pay longs.

Funding passes between traders. It is not a fee paid to the exchange, which charges trading fees separately. The details are in funding rate.

In plain words

Picture a dog on an elastic lead walking beside its owner. The dog can wander ahead or fall behind, but the further it strays, the harder the lead pulls. The perpetual price is the dog, the spot index is the owner and funding is the lead. It pulls; it does not lock the two together.

Going deeper

Perpetuals and dated futures

Margin and settlement currency

Some perpetuals are margined and settled in a stablecoin. Others are margined and settled in the underlying coin itself. The choice changes which currency gains and losses arrive in and how they are calculated.

Where they trade

Perpetuals are offered by centralised exchanges and by some decentralised protocols. Funding intervals, leverage limits, margin tiers and liquidation procedures differ between venues. Availability also depends on jurisdiction: in some countries these products are not offered to retail customers.

One position, three eventsHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. The index is at 100. A trader opens a long of 10 contracts, each tracking one unit, so the position value is 1,000. The margin posted is 100, which is 10x leverage.

  • Price rises to 103. Unrealized gain: 3 x 10 = 30, which is 30% of the margin.
  • Price falls to 97 instead. Unrealized loss: 30, which is 30% of the margin.
  • Funding is +0.01% at the next interval. The long pays 0.01% of 1,000 = 0.10 to the shorts.

A 3% move in price became a 30% change in margin. The position has no end date: it continues until it is closed or liquidated.

What it does NOT mean

  • A perpetual is not the asset. It cannot be withdrawn to a wallet, staked or spent. It is a contract on an exchange.
  • No expiry does not mean no cost of time. Funding is charged or received at every interval for as long as the position is open.
  • Funding does not force the contract price to equal spot. It creates an incentive. Gaps can persist, especially in fast markets.
  • Easy shorting does not mean low-risk shorting. A short on a perpetual can be liquidated just as fast as a long.
  • The maximum leverage on offer is not a suggestion. It is a product setting chosen by the exchange and says nothing about what is prudent.

Common mistakes

  1. Treating a perpetual like a spot holding. A coin held on spot and bought without borrowing cannot be liquidated; a leveraged contract can.
  2. Overlooking funding on long holding periods. Small payments every few hours add up over weeks.
  3. Watching the last traded price for liquidation. Most exchanges use the mark price instead.
  4. Assuming every exchange uses the same rules. Formulas, intervals and limits differ.

Risks and limitations

  • Leverage shortens the distance to liquidation. See leverage risk.
  • Funding varies, can spike in volatile markets and can change sign.
  • Forced closures can feed on each other in a liquidation cascade.
  • The position depends on the exchange or protocol staying solvent and online.
  • Rules can be changed by the venue, sometimes at short notice.

Key takeaway

A perpetual future is a leveraged contract that tracks an asset’s price without ever expiring. Funding payments between longs and shorts, not an expiry date, keep it near the spot index.
