Genesis Block & Early History

The genesis block is the first block of the Bitcoin blockchain, dated 3 January 2009. This page follows the network from that block through its first few years.

Why it matters

Every blockchain has to start somewhere. Bitcoin’s starting point is a single block created on 3 January 2009, and every transaction since then rests on top of it.

The early years also show something that is easy to forget: Bitcoin began as a small experiment, with bugs, hobbyist users and no market at all. Seeing how it actually started is a useful correction to stories that make its history sound either inevitable or mysterious.

The first block

A blockchain is a sequence in which each block points back to the one before it. The first block has nothing to point to, so it is defined directly in the software. It is called the genesis block, or block 0.

The genesis block contains one transaction, which creates the first 50 bitcoin. That transaction also carries a line of text:

“The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks”

This is a front-page headline from the London newspaper The Times of that day. It has one clear technical function: it shows the block could not have been prepared before 3 January 2009. Many people also read it as a comment on the banking crisis of the time. That reading is plausible, but Satoshi Nakamoto never explained the choice, so it remains an interpretation.

In plain words

Think of the foundation stone of a building with the day’s newspaper sealed inside it. The newspaper proves the stone was not laid earlier than that date. Everything else in the building is stacked on that stone, and nobody can swap it out without taking the whole structure down.

One oddity is well known. Because of the way the original software handled the first block, its 50 bitcoin can never be spent. Whether this was intended is not known.

The first weeks

The software was released publicly a few days after the genesis block. For some time the network consisted of very few machines. Mining was done on ordinary computer processors, and the difficulty of finding a block stayed at its minimum because so little computing power was involved.

On 12 January 2009, Satoshi sent 10 bitcoin to Hal Finney, a cryptographer who had been one of the first people to run the software. This is the first known transfer between two people. It worked as a test: the system described in the white paper could move value from one participant to another.

At this stage bitcoin had no market price. Coins were mined, sent back and forth and discussed on mailing lists by people interested in the idea.

From experiment to payment

On 22 May 2010, a programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 bitcoin for two pizzas. He had posted the offer on a forum, and another user ordered the pizzas and received the coins. It is generally regarded as the first documented purchase of a physical good with bitcoin, and the date is still marked informally by enthusiasts.

The significance is not the size of the number. It is that two strangers agreed the coins could be exchanged for something outside the network.

Other changes arrived in the years that followed:

  • Mining became competitive. Miners moved from ordinary processors to graphics cards, and began to combine their efforts in pools.
  • A serious bug was found and fixed. In August 2010, a flaw allowed a transaction to create a huge number of bitcoin out of nothing. Developers released a corrected version within hours, and the network moved to a chain without the faulty transaction.
  • The founder left. Satoshi’s last known public communications date from around 2010 to 2011. Other contributors took over maintenance of the software.
  • The first halving occurred. In November 2012 the block subsidy fell from 50 to 25 bitcoin, as scheduled. See the halving.

Going deeper

Why the genesis block is fixed in the software

A new node needs a trusted starting point that it does not have to take from a stranger. By carrying block 0 inside the program, every node begins from the same place and can check everything after it independently.

A hypothetical new node joins the networkHypothetical illustration

Suppose someone installs Bitcoin software on a new computer today.

  1. The program already contains the genesis block.
  2. It asks other nodes for block 1 and checks that block 1 refers to the hash of block 0.
  3. It verifies the proof of work and every transaction in block 1, then repeats the process for block 2, and so on.
  4. After working through every block, it arrives at the current state of the ledger.

The node has not relied on anyone’s word. It has re-checked the entire history, starting from a block it already had.

What the 2010 bug shows

The August 2010 incident is sometimes left out of simplified histories. It is worth knowing for two reasons. It shows that the software has contained serious errors. It also shows how a rule change happens in practice: a fix is published, and it takes effect only because the people running nodes and miners choose to adopt it.

What it does NOT mean

  • The headline is not an official mission statement. Its presence is a fact. What it was meant to say is interpretation.
  • Creating the genesis block gave its author no lasting power. Block 0 follows the same rules as every other block and grants no control over the network.
  • Early history is not a guide to the future. How a system behaved with a few dozen users says little about how it or its market will behave later.
  • The pizza purchase is not a lesson about what anyone ought to have done. Judging a 2010 decision by later prices is hindsight, not analysis.
  • A long record of operation is not proof of flawlessness. The early software had bugs, and the network depended on quick human coordination to fix them.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Mixing up the milestones. The white paper (October 2008), the genesis block (January 2009), the first transfer (January 2009) and the first purchase (May 2010) are four separate events.
  2. Assuming early mining was the same activity as today. It ran on home computers. Modern mining uses specialised hardware at industrial scale.
  3. Believing the genesis coins belong to someone who could spend them. That output is unspendable under the rules.
  4. Treating block dates as exact. Timestamps are set by miners and are only required to be approximately right.

Limits and open questions

  • Much of what is known about 2009 and 2010 comes from forum posts and from private emails published later, which cannot all be verified.
  • Several days separate the genesis block’s timestamp from the next block. The reason has never been confirmed.
  • It is unknown how many early coins are permanently lost, because an unused key and a destroyed key look the same on the blockchain.
  • Early participants were few and loosely organised, so some firsts may simply be undocumented.

Key takeaway

The genesis block of 3 January 2009 is the fixed starting point that every Bitcoin node shares. The years that followed were a small, imperfect experiment that gradually became a network with no founder in charge.
