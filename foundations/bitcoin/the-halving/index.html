The Halving

The halving is the scheduled event, every 210,000 blocks, at which the number of new bitcoin created with each block is cut in half.

Why it matters

New bitcoin enter circulation only as a reward for mining a block. The size of that reward is not constant. It steps down on a fixed schedule, and each step is called a halving.

The halving is the mechanism behind the 21 million limit. It is also one of the most talked-about events in crypto, and much of what is said about it goes beyond what the mechanism supports.

How it works

A miner’s reward has two parts: the block subsidy, which is newly created bitcoin, and the fees paid by the transactions in the block. The halving affects only the subsidy.

The rule is short. The subsidy began at 50 bitcoin. After every 210,000 blocks it is divided by two.

subsidy = 50 BTC / 2^(number of halvings so far) where number of halvings = block height / 210,000, rounded downThe calculation is done in satoshis and rounded down, so the subsidy eventually reaches zero.

Nobody triggers the event. Every node works out the permitted subsidy from the height of each block. A block that pays its miner too much is rejected.

In plain words

Picture a tap that fills a tank. A counter on the tap closes the valve halfway after every 210,000 drips. The water keeps flowing, only more slowly each time, until the tap is effectively shut. Nobody turns the valve: the counter does it.

The schedule so far

Halving Block height Date Subsidy after
Start 0 January 2009 50 BTC
1st 210,000 November 2012 25 BTC
2nd 420,000 July 2016 12.5 BTC
3rd 630,000 May 2020 6.25 BTC
4th 840,000 April 2024 3.125 BTC
5th 1,050,000 Expected around 2028 1.5625 BTC

Why “about four years”

The halving is counted in blocks, not in days. With a target of ten minutes per block, 210,000 blocks take 2,100,000 minutes, which is roughly 1,458 days or just under four years.

The actual dates drift. When computing power is growing, blocks arrive slightly faster than every ten minutes until the next difficulty adjustment catches up, so past halvings have come a little sooner than exactly four years apart. A future halving date is therefore always an estimate.

What changes at a halving

The fourth halving in numbersWorked calculation

This uses the protocol’s constants and an idealised day of 144 blocks.

  • Before block 840,000: 144 x 6.25 = 900 new bitcoin per day.
  • From block 840,000: 144 x 3.125 = 450 new bitcoin per day.

The change applied from one block to the next. No existing coin was altered. Only the rate at which new coins appear was reduced.

For a hypothetical miner whose machines find one block per week on average, the subsidy part of that weekly income fell from 6.25 to 3.125 bitcoin overnight, while the electricity bill stayed the same.

For miners

A halving cuts the subsidy part of a miner’s revenue by half, measured in bitcoin, while costs are unchanged. Miners with expensive electricity or older machines may no longer cover their costs and may switch off. If enough of them do, blocks slow down until the difficulty adjusts downward. The system is built to absorb this.

For users

Nothing changes in how transactions work. Balances, addresses and fees follow the same rules as the day before.

For the long term

Each halving shifts a little more of the miners’ income from subsidy to fees. The white paper anticipated this, saying the incentive can move entirely to transaction fees once issuance ends. Whether fee income will be enough to pay for strong security is an open question that each halving makes more relevant.

Going deeper: the halving and markets

The halving is discussed heavily in trading circles, usually as a claim about price. Three points are worth keeping apart:

  • The fact. The rate of new issuance falls by half.
  • The reasoning. If demand stays the same and new supply shrinks, there is less new supply to absorb. That is a statement about one side of a market, as described in supply and demand.
  • The limits of the reasoning. Demand does not stay the same. The schedule has been public since 2009, so the event is not new information. Only four halvings have happened, each in different economic conditions, which is far too few cases to establish a pattern.

Other tokens have very different issuance rules. See token unlocks and emissions for comparison.

What it does NOT mean

  • The halving is not a forecast. It is a change in issuance. It carries no information about what the market price does afterwards.
  • It does not halve anyone’s coins. Existing balances are untouched. This is nothing like a stock split.
  • It does not reduce the supply. The total keeps growing, only more slowly.
  • It does not halve fees or change transaction speed. Block timing and fee rules are separate mechanisms.
  • It is not tied to a calendar date. It happens at a block height, and the date can only be estimated in advance.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Reading a schedule as a cycle. A fixed issuance timetable is a fact. A repeating market cycle built on it is a theory with very little data behind it.
  2. Confusing subsidy with total reward. Miners also earn fees, which vary from block to block and are not halved.
  3. Assuming halvings threaten the network’s operation. Difficulty adjusts to whatever computing power remains.
  4. Treating countdown dates as exact. Published countdowns are projections from average block times.

Limits and open questions

  • The long-term security budget is uncertain: lower subsidies must be offset by fees or by a higher value of each coin, and neither is assured.
  • Halvings can concentrate mining among operators with the cheapest power, which matters for decentralisation.
  • Because the sample is so small, statistical claims about what follows a halving cannot be tested rigorously.
  • The schedule could in theory be altered by a rule change, though this would need broad adoption by nodes and faces strong opposition.

Key takeaway

The halving cuts the block subsidy in half every 210,000 blocks, which slows new issuance and leads to the 21 million limit. It is a scheduled rule about supply, not a statement about price.
