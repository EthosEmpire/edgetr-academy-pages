The 21 Million Limit

Bitcoin's rules allow just under 21 million bitcoin to be created. The limit is the result of a block subsidy that halves every 210,000 blocks until it reaches zero.

Why it matters

Most currencies have no fixed upper limit: a central bank can issue more. Bitcoin’s issuance follows a schedule written into its software, and that schedule ends. The figure usually quoted is 21 million.

The limit is one of the most repeated facts about Bitcoin and one of the most loosely explained. This page covers where the number comes from, who enforces it and what it does not imply.

How it works

There is no single line in the design that says “stop at 21 million”. The limit is a consequence of two rules:

  1. Each new block may create a fixed amount of new bitcoin, the block subsidy. It started at 50.
  2. Every 210,000 blocks, the subsidy is cut in half. This event is the halving.

Add up 210,000 blocks at 50, then 210,000 at 25, then 12.5, and so on, and the total settles toward a ceiling.

210,000 x 50 x (1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ...) = 210,000 x 50 x 2 = 21,000,000The series in brackets gets ever closer to 2. In practice the subsidy is counted in whole satoshis and rounded down, so the true total is slightly below 21 million.

In plain words

Imagine walking toward a wall, covering half of the remaining distance with every step. The first step is huge and each later step is smaller. You get extremely close to the wall, but your steps never carry you past it. Bitcoin’s supply moves toward 21 million in the same way.

Units and divisibility

A limit of 21 million does not mean only 21 million payments or holders are possible. Each bitcoin divides into 100,000,000 satoshis, so the ledger actually counts about 2.1 quadrillion indivisible units. On the base layer, amounts cannot be smaller than one satoshi.

Who enforces it

No treasury or company controls issuance. Enforcement happens at every node.

When a miner publishes a block, each node calculates the subsidy allowed at that block height and adds the fees paid by the block’s transactions. If the miner’s reward exceeds that sum, the node rejects the entire block. The miner’s proof of work is wasted.

Worked arithmetic: the schedule at the fourth halvingWorked calculation

This example uses the protocol’s own constants, not market data.

By block 840,000, reached in April 2024, four eras of 210,000 blocks had been completed:

  • 210,000 x 50 = 10,500,000
  • 210,000 x 25 = 5,250,000
  • 210,000 x 12.5 = 2,625,000
  • 210,000 x 6.25 = 1,312,500

Total scheduled: 19,687,500 bitcoin, which is 93.75% of 21 million.

So the first 15 years issued almost 94% of the eventual supply. The remaining 6.25% is scheduled to appear over more than a century, in ever smaller amounts.

Going deeper

When issuance ends

The subsidy keeps halving until it falls below one satoshi, at which point it becomes zero. At ten minutes per block, that is expected around the year 2140. From then on, miners would be paid only by transaction fees.

Scheduled supply and usable supply

The number of coins that can actually move is lower than the scheduled total, for several reasons:

  • The 50 bitcoin of the genesis block cannot be spent.
  • A few miners have claimed less than the reward they were entitled to. That difference is gone for good.
  • Coins whose keys are lost stay on the ledger but can never be spent. Nobody knows how many there are, and estimates vary widely.

Could the limit be changed?

The limit is a rule in software, and software can be edited. A change would count only if the people running nodes adopted the new version. Anyone who did not would keep following the old rules and treat the new coins as invalid.

In practice, the limit is regarded as one of the hardest rules to change, because holders, miners and node operators all have reasons to resist. That is an observation about incentives and social agreement. It is not a law of physics, and it is fair to describe the cap as very firmly established without calling it impossible to alter.

Supply is only half of a market

A fixed schedule describes how many units exist. The price of anything also depends on how much people want it, as explained in supply and demand. The cap makes future supply predictable. It tells you nothing about future demand.

What it does NOT mean

  • A capped supply does not mean the price must go up. Scarcity alone does not create value. Plenty of scarce things are worth very little.
  • 21 million is not the amount available today. Part of it is not yet issued, and an unknown share of what exists is permanently lost.
  • The cap does not restrict small payments. Divisibility into satoshis is a separate property from the total.
  • A predictable supply does not mean stable purchasing power. The number of coins follows a schedule. What a coin buys does not, and it has changed sharply in both directions.
  • The limit is not maintained by a promise. It holds because independent nodes each check it. It would stop holding only if they chose different rules.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Saying exactly 21 million will exist. The schedule ends slightly below that figure, and usable supply is lower still.
  2. Believing the last coin arrives soon. Most coins are already issued, but the tail of the schedule runs to around 2140.
  3. Thinking a company or developer decides the supply. Each node enforces the rule independently.
  4. Comparing “price per coin” across cryptocurrencies. Unit counts differ enormously between assets, so the price of one unit says little by itself. See token supply.

Limits and open questions

  • Miner incentives after the subsidy. Whether fees alone can pay for enough mining to keep the network secure is unresolved and cannot be tested in advance.
  • Lost coins. Their number is unknown and cannot be measured from the blockchain.
  • Economic debate. Economists disagree about whether a fixed supply is a desirable property for money. Some see protection from arbitrary issuance. Others see a system that cannot respond to changes in demand.
  • Governance. The cap depends on continued agreement among participants, which is strong today but is a social fact.

Key takeaway

The 21 million limit is the sum of a subsidy that halves every 210,000 blocks, enforced separately by every node. It makes supply predictable and says nothing about demand or price.
