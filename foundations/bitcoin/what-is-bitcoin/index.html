What Is Bitcoin?

Bitcoin is a digital currency and payment network that runs without a central operator, using a public ledger that thousands of independent computers verify.

Why it matters

Bitcoin was the first cryptocurrency, and almost every idea in the crypto world either copies it, extends it or argues with it. Understanding how it works makes the rest of the field far easier to follow: blockchains, mining, wallets and altcoins all start here.

It is also a subject surrounded by strong opinions. This page describes what Bitcoin is and how it functions. It does not say whether anyone should own it.

How it works

Bitcoin is two things with one name. Bitcoin (capital B, by convention) is a network of computers running the same open-source software. bitcoin (lower case, ticker BTC) is the unit that the network keeps track of.

The network maintains one shared record called the blockchain: a list of every transaction ever accepted, grouped into blocks. Each block refers to the one before it, which makes old entries very hard to change. No bank or company holds a master copy. Thousands of independent computers, called nodes, each keep their own copy and check every new entry against the same rules.

In plain words

Picture a village notice board where every payment is pinned up in public: “Ana pays Ben 5”. Anyone can read the board and anyone can keep a photocopy. A new note is accepted only if the copy-holders can see that Ana really had 5 to give. Bitcoin is that notice board, kept by computers instead of villagers.

Issuance Creates new units at a rate that halves about every four years The 21 million limit

Three properties follow from this arrangement:

  • No central operator. There is no company, server or administrator that can approve or reverse a payment.
  • Rule-based issuance. New bitcoin appear only as a reward to miners, on a schedule written into the software.
  • Open participation. Anyone can run the software, create keys and broadcast a transaction without asking permission.

Going deeper: trade-offs in the design

Bitcoin was described by its pseudonymous creator, Satoshi Nakamoto, as “a peer-to-peer electronic cash system” in a white paper published on 31 October 2008. The network began running in January 2009.

The design makes deliberate trade-offs. Having every node verify everything keeps the system independent of any single operator, but it limits capacity. The base network handles only a handful of transactions per second, and fees rise when many people compete for space in a block.

Payments are also final. Once a transaction is buried under several blocks, it is practically impossible to cancel, whether by the sender, a miner or a developer. That removes the need for a trusted intermediary. It also removes the protection an intermediary can offer when something goes wrong.

A hypothetical payment, start to finishHypothetical illustration

Ana wants to send 0.01 bitcoin to Ben. This is a made-up illustration.

  1. Ben gives Ana an address, a string of letters and numbers derived from his public key.
  2. Ana’s wallet software builds a transaction and signs it with her private key.
  3. The transaction is broadcast and nodes check that the signature is valid and that the coins have not already been spent.
  4. It waits in the mempool until a miner includes it in a block.
  5. Each later block adds one confirmation, making the payment harder to undo.

No institution approved the payment at any step, and no institution can undo it afterwards.

What it does NOT mean

  • Bitcoin is not a company. There is no chief executive, head office or support desk. Businesses build services around it, but none of them owns the network.
  • Bitcoin is not anonymous. Every transaction is public forever. Addresses are pseudonyms, and they can often be linked to real identities.
  • Bitcoin is not stored “in” a wallet. A wallet holds keys. The coins are entries in the shared ledger.
  • A limited supply does not say anything about price. The rules cap how many units can exist. They do not determine what anyone will pay for one, and the market price has been very volatile throughout its history.
  • Bitcoin is not the same thing as “blockchain” or “crypto”. It is one specific network. Other systems reuse the idea with different rules.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Thinking the unit cannot be divided. One bitcoin equals 100,000,000 satoshis, and transactions routinely move small fractions.
  2. Confusing the network with exchanges. An exchange is a company that holds coins for customers. An exchange failing or being hacked is a different event from the Bitcoin protocol failing.
  3. Assuming mistakes can be fixed. A payment to a wrong address, or a lost key, has no helpline and no appeal process.
  4. Treating the technology and the market as one subject. How the system works and how its price behaves are separate questions. This section covers the first.

Limits and open questions

  • Throughput. Base-layer capacity is small by design. Systems such as the Lightning Network try to add capacity on top, with their own trade-offs.
  • Fee volatility. The cost of a transaction can change sharply within hours.
  • Energy use. Proof of work consumes a large amount of electricity. Whether that cost is justified is an ongoing public debate.
  • Irreversible errors. Self-custody places the whole burden of security on the user.
  • Long-term incentives. As new issuance shrinks, miners must rely more on fees. How well that works over decades is not yet known.

Key takeaway

Bitcoin is a public ledger of transactions maintained by a network of computers that share rules instead of an operator. Its strengths and its limits come from that same choice.
