Nodes, Validators & Miners

Nodes store and verify a blockchain's ledger. Miners, under proof of work, and validators, under proof of stake, are the participants that propose new blocks for the nodes to check.

Why it matters

When people say a blockchain is “run by a network”, these are the participants they mean. The split of duties between them answers some of the most common questions about crypto: who checks transactions, who could cheat, and what “decentralised” refers to in practice.

How it works

A node is a computer running a blockchain’s software and connected to other nodes. A full node downloads every block, checks every transaction against the rules, and keeps its own copy of the ledger. It trusts nothing it is told and verifies everything itself.

Some participants do an extra job: proposing new blocks.

  • Under proof of work, they are miners. They compete to solve a computational puzzle, and the winner’s block is the next one.
  • Under proof of stake, they are validators. They lock up the network’s coin as a stake, and the protocol selects one to propose each block while others vote on it.

The two jobs, verifying and proposing, are separate.

In plain words

Think of a football match. The players who score are like miners or validators: they make things happen and collect the rewards. The referees and every spectator with a rulebook are like full nodes. A player can put the ball in the net by hand, but the goal does not count, because everyone watching applies the same rules.

Who has which power

A block producer decides which pending transactions go into its block and in what order. That is real influence: it can delay a transaction or arrange the order to its own benefit.

It cannot break the rules. A block that creates coins out of nothing, spends from an address without a valid signature or exceeds the size limit is rejected by every honest full node, however much mining power or stake stands behind it. The invalid block is ignored, and its producer has spent effort for no reward.

Participant Can do Cannot do
Full node Verify everything, reject invalid blocks, relay transactions Add blocks to the chain
Miner or validator Choose and order transactions, propose blocks, earn rewards Make nodes accept an invalid block
Light client Check headers, verify that a transaction is in a block Verify every rule independently

Going deeper

Types of node. A full node may prune old data to save space while still having verified it. An archive node keeps every historical state and needs far more storage. A light client stores only block headers and relies on full nodes for details, with some cryptographic checks.

Most wallets rely on someone else’s node. A typical wallet app does not run a node. It asks a provider’s node for balances and hands it transactions to broadcast. That is convenient, and it means the user is trusting the provider to report accurately and stay available.

Pools. Winning a block is rare for a small miner, so miners combine into mining pools and share rewards. Stakers similarly join staking pools or use services that run validators for them. See staking. Pools smooth out income, and they also concentrate block production in a few operators.

What decentralisation measures. There is no single number. It depends on how many independent full nodes exist, how block production is spread among miners or validators, how many separate software implementations are in use, and where all of these are located. A network can be widely spread on one measure and concentrated on another. Bitcoin’s case is covered in nodes and decentralization.

Agreement between nodes. How thousands of nodes settle on the same chain when they see different blocks is the subject of consensus.

An invalid block goes nowhereHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers. A network’s rules allow a block producer to pay itself a reward of 5 coins per block. A large miner controlling 30% of the mining power produces a block that pays itself 50.

  • The block’s proof of work is genuine, and the miner broadcasts it.
  • Each full node checks the reward: 50 is greater than 5. The block breaks a rule.
  • Every honest node discards it and continues to wait for a valid block at that height.
  • Another miner produces a valid block paying 5. Nodes accept it and the chain moves on.

The large miner has spent the electricity and earned nothing. Its 30% share gave it a better chance of producing blocks, and no ability to change what a valid block is.

What it does NOT mean

  • Miners and validators do not control the rules. They choose among valid transactions. Validity is enforced by the nodes.
  • Running a node does not earn rewards by itself. On most networks, only block production and related duties are paid.
  • A validator is not a person approving transactions. It is software signing blocks according to the protocol.
  • Many nodes does not mean many independent operators. Large numbers can run in a few data centres or under a few providers.
  • Using a wallet is not the same as verifying. Most wallets trust a third party’s node.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Using “node”, “miner” and “validator” as synonyms. All block producers run nodes, but verifying and proposing are different jobs.
  2. Assuming the largest miner or staker is in charge. Size increases the chance of proposing blocks, not the power to rewrite rules.
  3. Counting nodes as the only measure of decentralisation. The spread of block production and of software matters as much.

Risks and limits

  • Block producers can censor or reorder transactions within the rules.
  • Concentration in a few pools or providers weakens the protection that independence is meant to give.
  • Rising hardware needs can reduce the number of people able to run full nodes.
  • Light clients and hosted nodes involve trust in others.
  • A bug in widely used node software can affect a large part of a network at once.

Key takeaway

Nodes hold the ledger and enforce the rules, while miners or validators propose the blocks that nodes then check. Proposers choose what goes in. Verifiers decide whether it counts.
