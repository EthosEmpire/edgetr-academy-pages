Public & Private Blockchains

A public blockchain lets anyone read, transact and help validate. A private or permissioned blockchain restricts those roles to approved participants chosen by an operator or consortium.

Why it matters

“Blockchain” is used for two quite different things. One is an open network such as Bitcoin, which anyone can join and nobody controls. The other is a shared database run by a company or a group of companies, which uses a similar data structure but admits only approved participants. News about “banks adopting blockchain” usually concerns the second kind. Telling them apart prevents the properties of one being assumed for the other.

How it works

A public blockchain, also called permissionless, is open in three ways. Anyone can read the full ledger. Anyone can submit a transaction. Anyone can run a node, and anyone who commits the required work or stake can help produce blocks. Because participants are strangers, security comes from a consensus mechanism that makes influence costly.

A private blockchain, also called permissioned, has a gatekeeper. An operator, or a group of organisations, decides who may read the ledger, who may submit transactions and who may validate. Participants are identified and usually bound by legal agreements.

In plain words

A public blockchain is like a town noticeboard in the square. Anyone can read it and anyone can pin something up, so long as they follow the posted rules. A private blockchain is like the noticeboard in a company’s staff room. It may look the same, but you need a pass to get in, and management decides who gets a pass.

A spectrum, not two boxes

Real systems sit along a range. The two questions that locate one are:

Question Open answer Closed answer
Who can read the ledger? Anyone Members only
Who can write and validate? Anyone who meets the protocol’s conditions Approved parties only

A consortium blockchain is run jointly by several organisations, such as a group of banks or companies in a supply chain. None of them fully trusts the others to keep the record alone, so they share it.

What changes when access is restricted

Consensus becomes simpler. With a small set of known validators, there is no need for mining or for large stakes. Members can simply vote, using protocols that tolerate a minority of faulty participants. Blocks are fast and final at once.

Capacity rises. A few powerful, well-connected servers can process far more than thousands of independent nodes. The trade-off is described in finality and throughput.

Privacy becomes possible. Transactions can be hidden from outsiders, and even between members.

Trust returns. The ledger is only as hard to alter as its validators are unwilling to collude. If the operators agree to rewrite or reverse entries, they can. Users of the system rely on those organisations, which is a form of counterparty risk.

Going deeper

Is a private blockchain just a database? Critics argue that a ledger controlled by one organisation offers little beyond what an ordinary database with good audit logs provides. The case is stronger for consortia, where several parties that do not fully trust each other need a common record and no single member should be able to change it alone.

Is a public blockchain really open? Openness on paper does not ensure wide participation in practice. If mining power, stake or node hosting is concentrated, a public chain may depend on a small group too. See nodes and decentralization.

No coin required. Public chains need a native asset to pay fees and reward those who secure the network. A private chain’s validators are paid by their employers, so it often has no coin. A private blockchain is therefore not, in itself, a cryptocurrency.

A consortium of fiveHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical ledger is run by five companies. Each operates one validator. A block is accepted when at least 4 of the 5 sign it.

  • Normal operation. All five are online. Blocks are final within a second or two, and the system handles thousands of transactions per second on ordinary servers.
  • One validator fails. Four remain, which meets the threshold of 4. The ledger keeps running.
  • Two validators fail. Three remain, which is below 4. No new blocks can be accepted until one returns.
  • Four validators collude. They hold 4 of 5 signatures, enough to accept a block that reverses an earlier entry. The fifth member can see it and object, but cannot prevent it within the system.

On a large public chain, the equivalent of the last case would require controlling a majority of the mining power or a large share of all stake, bought on the open market. Here it requires an agreement between four known organisations, who are in turn answerable to courts and contracts.

What it does NOT mean

  • “Blockchain” in a headline does not mean a public, decentralised network. Many projects described this way are permissioned systems.
  • Private does not mean insecure. It means security rests on identified operators and legal agreements instead of open competition.
  • Public does not mean decentralised in practice. That depends on how participation is actually distributed.
  • Immutable does not carry over automatically. A permissioned ledger can be changed whenever enough of its operators agree.
  • A private blockchain is not a cryptocurrency. Many have no tradable asset at all.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Assuming every blockchain has the properties of Bitcoin. Openness, censorship resistance and fixed rules belong to particular designs.
  2. Believing public chains hide activity. Every transaction on them is visible to everyone, permanently.
  3. Confusing a company’s use of blockchain with exposure to a crypto asset. The two are unrelated unless a token is involved.

Risks and limits

  • Permissioned ledgers depend on the honesty and continued cooperation of their operators.
  • A small validator set is a small target for failure, coercion or collusion.
  • Public chains expose all transaction data and have limited capacity.
  • Rules on a private chain can be changed by its operators without users’ consent.
  • Systems built on different ledgers may not work with one another.

Key takeaway

A public blockchain is open to anyone and secured by costly work or stake. A private one is restricted to approved participants and secured by their identity and agreements. They share a data structure and differ in whom you have to trust.
