Finality & Throughput

Finality is the point after which a transaction is practically irreversible. Throughput is how many transactions a chain processes per second. Both trade off against decentralisation and security.

Why it matters

Two questions decide how usable a blockchain is: how long until a payment cannot be undone, and how many payments the network can handle. The first is finality and the second is throughput. Both are routinely quoted in marketing, often as a single flattering number. Reading them properly requires knowing what is being measured and what was given up to achieve it.

Finality

Finality is the point after which a transaction is practically irreversible. Before that point, the block containing it could still be replaced by a competing block in a reorganisation, covered in consensus.

Probabilistic finality

On a proof-of-work chain, nothing ever declares a block final. Each new block built on top makes a reversal harder, because an attacker would have to redo all of that work and overtake the honest chain. The blocks on top are called confirmations.

The chance of reversal shrinks rapidly with each confirmation but is never exactly zero. Recipients choose how many confirmations to wait for, depending on the value at stake. Six confirmations, about an hour, is a long-standing convention on Bitcoin, not a rule of the protocol. See mempool and confirmations.

Explicit finality

Many proof-of-stake chains finalise blocks by vote. When validators holding a set share of the stake, commonly two thirds, have signed, the block is final. Reversing it would require a large share of validators to sign a conflicting history, for which their stake would be destroyed.

This gives a clear moment of finality. The cost is that if too many validators are offline, the vote cannot complete and finality stalls, even though blocks may still be produced.

In plain words

Probabilistic finality is like concrete setting: it is firmer every minute, and at some point you stop worrying, though no bell rings. Explicit finality is like a notary’s stamp: one moment the document is not official, the next it is, provided enough notaries are in the office.

Throughput

Throughput is the amount of activity a chain can process in a given time, usually expressed as transactions per second (TPS).

Throughput (TPS) = transactions per block / block time in secondsA simplification. Transactions vary in size and complexity, so real capacity is set by a block's data or gas limit.

Three different times are often confused:

Measure Question it answers
Block time How long until the next block?
Time to finality How long until a transaction is irreversible?
Throughput How many transactions fit per second?

A chain can have fast blocks and slow finality. The base layers of Bitcoin and Ethereum carry far fewer transactions per second than chains that report figures in the thousands, which achieve them through larger or faster blocks, fewer validators or more demanding hardware.

The trade-off

Raising throughput on a base chain means bigger blocks, more frequent blocks, or both. Every full node must download, verify and store all of it.

As requirements grow, fewer participants can afford to run a node, and verification concentrates in data centres. Blocks that are large and frequent also take longer to spread, which increases accidental forks. A chain can be made faster by using a small, fixed set of powerful validators, at the cost of relying on that set.

This is why capacity is increasingly added in layers. A Layer 2 executes transactions elsewhere and uses the base chain mainly for settlement.

Going deeper

Economic finality. In both models, “irreversible” means that reversal would cost more than an attacker could plausibly gain. The strength of finality therefore depends on the total work or stake protecting the chain. The same number of confirmations means much less on a small chain than on a large one.

Reading TPS claims. A headline figure may come from a test network, count only the simplest kind of transfer, or include validators’ own voting messages. Theoretical maximum, tested maximum and actual daily usage are three different numbers.

Throughput and what it costs a nodeHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical chains, assuming an average transaction of 250 bytes.

  • Chain A: 3,000 transactions per block, one block every 600 seconds. Throughput: 3,000 / 600 = 5 TPS.
  • Chain B: 4,000 transactions per block, one block every 2 seconds. Throughput: 4,000 / 2 = 2,000 TPS.

Data per second is TPS x 250 bytes, and a day has 86,400 seconds.

  • Chain A: 5 x 250 = 1,250 bytes per second, or 108 megabytes a day.
  • Chain B: 2,000 x 250 = 500,000 bytes per second, or 43,200 megabytes a day: about 43 gigabytes.

Chain B handles 400 times the traffic of Chain A and asks each node to download, verify and store 400 times the data.

What it does NOT mean

  • Final does not mean impossible to reverse. It means reversal is prohibitively costly under the chain’s assumptions.
  • “Confirmed” does not mean final. One confirmation is the start of the process on a probabilistic chain.
  • A fast block time is not fast finality. The two are separate measures.
  • A high TPS figure does not mean a better chain. It shows a different set of trade-offs.
  • A quoted maximum is not typical performance. Real throughput depends on demand and on the mix of transactions.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Comparing TPS numbers without their conditions. Figures measured differently are not comparable.
  2. Treating a Layer 2 confirmation as settlement. A rollup operator’s quick confirmation is a promise. The transaction is final only when its batch is final on the base chain.
  3. Ignoring what speed required. High throughput usually reflects heavier nodes or fewer validators.

Risks and limits

  • Recent transactions can be reorganised, especially on chains with little work or stake behind them.
  • Explicit finality can stall when many validators are offline.
  • High hardware requirements reduce the number of independent verifiers.
  • Congestion raises fees and delays inclusion whatever the theoretical capacity.
  • Performance claims are hard to verify independently and are often made by interested parties.

Key takeaway

Finality tells you when a transaction can be relied on, and throughput tells you how much a chain can carry. Each can be improved, usually by spending some decentralisation or security, so both numbers only make sense alongside what was traded for them.
