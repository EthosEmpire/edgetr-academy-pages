Commodities

Commodities are standardised raw materials such as oil, gold, copper and wheat. They trade in physical markets and, more visibly, through futures contracts.

Why it matters

Commodities are the oldest traded goods. Organised markets for grain existed long before stock exchanges, and much of the language of trading, including the futures contract, was developed for them.

They also show supply and demand in its plainest form. A commodity has no profits, no management and no code. Its price depends on how much is produced, how much is used and how much is stored.

How it works

A commodity is a raw material that is fungible: one unit is treated as interchangeable with any other unit of the same grade. A barrel of a given grade of crude oil is the same whoever pumped it. This standardisation is what allows commodities to trade in large, anonymous markets.

Commodities differ from the financial assets on the neighbouring pages in one basic way. A share can pay dividends and a bond pays interest. A commodity pays nothing. Holding it actually costs money, because it must be stored, insured and sometimes kept from spoiling.

In plain words

A bag of sugar from one shop is as good as a bag from another, so you simply buy the cheaper one. That is fungibility. A painting is the opposite: every one is different, so there is no single “painting price”. Commodity markets work because their goods are like the sugar.

Physical markets and futures markets

The spot price is the price for delivery now. Physical trade is done between producers, traders and industrial users, and depends on the exact grade and where the goods are located.

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell a standard quantity of a commodity at a fixed price on a future date. Futures were created so that a farmer could lock in a selling price before harvest and a miller could lock in a buying price. Using a contract to reduce an existing price risk is called hedging.

Most people who trade commodity futures never touch the goods. They close their contracts before the delivery date. Some contracts are settled in cash and involve no delivery at all.

Going deeper

Spot and futures prices differ

A futures price is usually not the same as the spot price. Storing a commodity costs money, so a contract for later delivery often trades above spot. When it does, the market is said to be in contango. When near-term supply is tight, futures can trade below spot, called backwardation. Both are explained in Basis, Contango & Backwardation.

Because futures expire, a position held for a long time must be moved from an expiring contract to a later one. This is called rolling, and the price difference between the two contracts affects the result.

What moves commodity prices

Typical forces include weather and harvests, discoveries and mine output, decisions by producing countries, transport disruptions, industrial activity and the value of the currency the commodity is priced in. Many commodities are quoted in US dollars worldwide.

Gold, and the “digital gold” comparison

Gold is used in jewellery and industry and is also held as a store of value. Bitcoin is sometimes called “digital gold” because it has a limited supply and no issuer. That is an analogy about scarcity, not a statement that the two behave alike. Their price histories, uses and risks are different.

In the United States, the derivatives regulator has treated Bitcoin as a commodity for regulatory purposes. Classification of crypto assets differs between countries and between assets.

A hypothetical futures contractHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical oil futures contract covers 1,000 barrels. The futures price is 80 per barrel.

  • The value of goods the contract represents is 1,000 x 80 = 80,000.
  • If the futures price rises by 1, to 81, the contract’s value changes by 1,000 x 1 = 1,000. The buyer of the contract gains 1,000 and the seller loses 1,000.
  • If the price falls by 1, to 79, the buyer loses 1,000 and the seller gains 1,000.

A producer who expects to sell 1,000 barrels in three months could sell one contract today. If the price then falls, the loss on the physical oil is offset by the gain on the contract. If the price rises, the gain on the oil is offset by a loss on the contract. The producer has swapped an uncertain price for a known one.

What it does NOT mean

  • Holding a commodity future is not the same as owning the commodity. It is a contract with an expiry date and its own costs.
  • A commodity is not a business. It produces no earnings or interest. Any return comes only from a change in price.
  • A widely quoted price is not the price everywhere. Grade, location and delivery date all change what a real buyer pays.
  • Scarcity does not mean a rising price. Higher prices encourage more production and less use, which can reverse a shortage.
  • “Digital gold” does not mean Bitcoin behaves like gold. It is a comparison of one feature, limited supply.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Confusing spot and futures. A headline price is usually for a specific futures contract, not for goods today.
  2. Forgetting storage and rolling. The result of holding futures over time can differ from the change in the spot price.
  3. Thinking futures markets are only speculation. They exist so that producers and users can hedge. Speculators take the other side of those hedges.
  4. Treating all commodities as one market. Wheat, copper and natural gas respond to completely different events.

Limits

  • Contract sizes, delivery rules and trading hours differ for every commodity and exchange.
  • Futures are usually traded with leverage, which magnifies both gains and losses. A leveraged futures position can lose more than the money first deposited.
  • Commodity prices can move sharply on events that cannot be anticipated, such as weather or political decisions.

Key takeaway

Commodities are interchangeable raw materials whose prices depend on production, use and storage. Most trading happens through futures contracts, which are agreements about the goods, not the goods themselves.
