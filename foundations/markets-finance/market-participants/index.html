Market Participants

Market participants are the people and organisations that trade: investors, hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs and market makers, each with different goals and time horizons.

Why it matters

A chart shows prices and volume. It does not show who traded or why. It is tempting to imagine every buyer as an optimist and every seller as a pessimist, and that picture is wrong most of the time.

Markets work because participants want different things. Knowing the main types helps you read commentary critically, especially claims about what “institutions”, “whales” or “smart money” are supposedly doing.

How it works

A useful way to sort participants is by motive.

Investors buy assets to hold, often for years. Pension funds, insurers and individuals saving for the future belong here. They trade when money comes in or goes out, or when they adjust their holdings.

Hedgers already carry a risk and trade to reduce it. A farmer who sells wheat futures before harvest, or an importer who locks in an exchange rate, is hedging. See Hedging Basics.

Speculators accept price risk deliberately, hoping to gain from a price change. Most short-term traders are speculators in this technical sense. The word is descriptive, not an insult: speculators often take the other side of hedgers’ trades.

Arbitrageurs look for the same or closely related assets trading at different prices and trade the gap. See Arbitrage.

Market makers continuously quote a price at which they will buy and a price at which they will sell. See Market Makers & Liquidity Providers.

In plain words

Think of a busy railway station. Commuters, tourists, staff, delivery drivers and people meeting friends all pass through the same doors. A count of people entering tells you the station is busy. It does not tell you why any of them came. A market is a station for orders.

Retail and institutional

Participants are also sorted by scale. Retail participants are individuals trading their own money. Institutional participants are organisations managing large pools of money.

Institutions face a problem individuals rarely have: their orders are large enough to move the price. They often split a large order into many small ones executed over hours or days, or trade away from public order books. A large participant’s activity is therefore rarely obvious on a chart.

Going deeper

Participants specific to certain markets

  • Central banks and governments are major participants in currency and bond markets, where they act for policy reasons, not for profit. See Interest Rates & Inflation.
  • Companies buy back or issue their own shares and exchange currencies to run their businesses.
  • Miners and validators in crypto receive newly issued coins and fees, and may sell some to cover costs. See Nodes, Validators & Miners.
  • Project teams, foundations and early backers often hold large allocations of a token, sometimes subject to release schedules.
  • Whales is crypto slang for holders of very large amounts. It describes size only.
  • Automated programs place a large share of orders in modern markets on behalf of all the groups above.

Different horizons, same price

A long-term investor and a short-term trader can look at the same price and both act sensibly in opposite directions. One is selling something held for ten years. The other is buying for ten minutes. Time horizons are covered in Overview of Trading Styles.

“Smart money”

Commentary often credits moves to “smart money”, meaning large or professional participants assumed to know more. Large participants do have better tools and information. They also disagree with each other, face constraints individuals do not, and make losses. Their intentions cannot be read reliably from a price chart.

Reading the hypothetical block aboveHypothetical illustration

A pension fund sells 10,000 shares because shares have grown beyond their planned portion of its holdings. A market maker buys them, then sells 6,000 to an index fund that has received new deposits and 4,000 to individuals.

  • Units sold by the pension fund: 10,000.
  • Units finally bought: 6,000 + 4,000 = 10,000.
  • Volume recorded: 10,000 for the first trade and 10,000 for the resales, 20,000 in total.

A chart shows 20,000 units of volume and perhaps a small dip and recovery. Nothing in that record reveals that the seller had no opinion on the company and that the largest buyer had no choice.

What it does NOT mean

  • A seller is not necessarily bearish. People sell to rebalance, to pay expenses, to hedge or to close an earlier trade.
  • “Institutional” does not mean “right”. Professional participants are frequently wrong and frequently on opposite sides of each other.
  • A large trade is not a message. Its size says nothing certain about its purpose.
  • “Whale” does not mean insider. It describes the size of a holding.
  • Speculators are not the opposite of “real” participants. They supply much of the trading that lets hedgers and investors transact.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Assuming everyone trades for the same reason. Motives differ, which is why trades happen at all.
  2. Reading intent from price. A chart records that a trade happened, not what either side was thinking.
  3. Following stories about what large players are doing. Such claims are usually guesses, and copying a crowd is covered in Herd Behavior.
  4. Imagining one coordinated opponent. “They” in “they pushed the price down” is nearly always many unrelated participants.

Limits

  • The categories overlap. A bank may hedge, speculate and make markets on the same day.
  • Public data rarely identifies who traded. Statements about participant behaviour are mostly estimates.
  • The mix of participants differs between markets and changes over time.

Key takeaway

Markets bring together participants with different goals, sizes and time horizons. A trade needs a buyer and a seller, and it does not need them to disagree about the future.

Sources and further reading

  • Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners, Larry Harris (2003). Classifies traders by why they trade: investors, borrowers, hedgers, speculators, dealers and arbitrageurs
  • Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives, John C. Hull. Introduces hedgers, speculators and arbitrageurs as the three broad types of derivatives trader
  • Market Centers: Buying and Selling Stock (opens in a new tab), U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. Describes exchanges, electronic communications networks and market makers as participants in U.S. stock trading
