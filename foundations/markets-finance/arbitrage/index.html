Arbitrage

Arbitrage is buying an asset where it is cheaper and selling the same asset where it is dearer. The trading itself tends to remove the price difference.

  • 5 min read
  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

The same asset trades in many places at once. Something has to keep its price roughly the same everywhere, and that something is arbitrage.

It matters for learners less as a way of trading and more as an explanation. Arbitrage is why a coin costs nearly the same on every large exchange, why a futures price stays tied to the spot price, and why an ETF trades close to the value of what it holds.

How it works

Arbitrage means buying an asset in one place and selling the same asset in another place where the price is higher, as close to simultaneously as possible. The person or firm doing it is an arbitrageur.

The important part is the side effect. Buying on the cheaper venue pushes that price up. Selling on the dearer venue pushes that price down. As described in Buyers, Sellers & Price, each order uses up the waiting orders it trades against. The gap narrows until it is no longer worth trading.

The idea that one asset should have one price everywhere is called the law of one price. Arbitrage is the mechanism that enforces it, approximately.

In plain words

Two petrol stations stand on opposite sides of a road. If one is much cheaper, drivers queue there and the other stands empty, until the cheap one raises its price or the dear one cuts it. Small differences survive, because crossing the road is a nuisance. Arbitrageurs are the drivers, and their costs are the nuisance.

Why gaps never fully close

Arbitrage has costs: trading fees on both sides, the spread, the cost and time of moving assets or cash between venues, and the capital that has to sit ready on both. A price difference smaller than those costs is not worth trading, so it can remain.

Prices on different venues therefore do not match exactly. They stay inside a band whose width depends on how costly and slow it is to move between them. Where transfers are restricted, for example by withdrawal limits or by rules on moving money across borders, the band can be wide and long-lasting.

Going deeper

Triangular arbitrage involves three pairs. If the rates for A/B, B/C and A/C are inconsistent, exchanging around the triangle ends with more than the starting amount. The same logic applies to currency pairs and to crypto pairs on one exchange.

Spot against futures. A futures contract and its underlying asset must meet at the same price when the contract expires. If the future trades far above spot, an arbitrageur can buy the asset and sell the future. See Basis, Contango & Backwardation. On perpetual contracts, the funding rate plays a related role.

Decentralized exchanges. A liquidity pool does not look at outside prices. When the wider market moves, the pool’s price is left behind until someone trades against it to bring it back in line. Arbitrageurs do this routinely, as described in AMMs & Liquidity Pools.

Statistical arbitrage is a different thing despite the name. It bets that two related but different assets will return to a typical relationship. Nothing forces them to, so it is a form of speculation.

Who does it

Arbitrage is dominated by specialised firms, including many market makers, with automated systems, low fees and capital already placed on many venues. Gaps large enough to cover an ordinary participant’s costs are usually gone within moments.

What can go wrong

Textbooks describe arbitrage as free of risk. In practice it is not.

  • Execution risk. One trade fills and the other does not, or fills at a worse price. The arbitrageur is left holding a position.
  • Transfer risk. Moving an asset between venues takes time, and the gap may close before it arrives.
  • Venue risk. A venue may suspend withdrawals or fail. See Counterparty Risk.

A hypothetical gap between two venuesHypothetical illustration

A coin trades at 100 on venue A and 102 on venue B. An arbitrageur with funds on both buys 50 units on A and sells 50 units on B.

  • Bought on A: 50 x 100 = 5,000.
  • Sold on B: 50 x 102 = 5,100.
  • Gross difference: 100.
  • Trading fees of 0.20 per unit on each trade: 50 x 0.20 x 2 = 20.
  • Transfer cost to rebalance afterwards: 10.
  • Result: 100 - 20 - 10 = 70, or 1.40 per unit.

Now suppose the sale on B is delayed and the price there has fallen to 100.50 by the time it fills.

  • Sold on B: 50 x 100.50 = 5,025. Gross difference: 25.
  • Result: 25 - 20 - 10 = a loss of 5.

The same plan produced a gain in one case and a loss in the other. The only difference was timing.

What it does NOT mean

  • Arbitrage is not a risk-free trade in practice. Fees, delays and failed fills can turn an apparent gap into a loss.
  • A visible price difference is not automatically an opportunity. It may be smaller than the costs, or it may exist because the asset cannot be moved between the venues.
  • Arbitrage does not make prices identical. It keeps them within a band set by costs.
  • A lasting gap does not prove the market is broken. It usually signals a barrier: a restriction, a risk or a cost that arbitrageurs are unwilling to bear.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Comparing last prices. What counts is the ask on the cheaper venue and the bid on the dearer one, for the size being traded.
  2. Forgetting transfer time. A gap seen on screen may be gone before an asset can be moved.
  3. Ignoring why a gap exists. A coin trading well below the wider market on one exchange may reflect suspended withdrawals there.

Limits

  • The example is simplified. Real arbitrage involves order book depth, partial fills and changing fees.
  • Tools and services advertised as effortless arbitrage are a common theme in scams. The wallets and security shelf covers how such schemes work.
  • How quickly gaps close depends on the market. It is fast between large venues and can be slow where barriers exist.

Key takeaway

Arbitrage buys an asset where it is cheaper and sells it where it is dearer, and in doing so pulls the two prices together. It links markets to each other, within a band set by the costs and risks of doing it.

Sources and further reading

  • Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives, John C. Hull. Introduces arbitrageurs and uses no-arbitrage arguments to price forwards and futures
  • Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners, Larry Harris (2003). Chapter on arbitrageurs covers the types of arbitrage and the risks involved
