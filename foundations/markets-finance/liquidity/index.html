Liquidity

Liquidity is how easily an asset can be bought or sold, in the size you want, without the trade itself moving the price.

  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A price on a screen is only useful if you can actually trade near it. Liquidity describes how true that is. In a liquid market, the quoted price is close to what a real order gets. In an illiquid one, the quoted price can be far from it.

Liquidity sits behind many other topics: slippage, the spread, sudden price spikes and why the same asset can behave calmly on one venue and wildly on another.

How it works

Liquidity is the ease of trading an asset without moving its price. It comes from other people’s willingness to trade: the buy and sell orders waiting in the order book.

  • The spread is the gap between the best bid and the best ask. A narrow spread means the cost of trading immediately is small.
  • Depth is how many units are waiting near the current price. With depth, a large order fills without reaching distant prices.

A market can have a tight spread and little depth. A small order then fills well, and a large one does not. Liquidity always has to be judged against the size of the trade.

In plain words

Selling a common used car in a big city takes days, and the price is close to what similar cars fetch. Selling a rare vintage tractor in a small village may take months, or require a big discount to the one interested buyer. The tractor may be valuable. It is just not liquid.

What illiquidity looks like

In the thin book above, the best ask is 100.10, and only 100 units are offered there. A buyer who needs more must pay the next prices up. The more they buy, the worse the average price.

The same order placed in a deep market fills at one price. The difference between the two outcomes is the cost of illiquidity, and it is paid by whoever demands to trade immediately.

Going deeper

Liquidity changes

Liquidity is not a fixed property of an asset. It varies by time of day, by venue and with market conditions. It tends to be lowest when it is most wanted: in fast, stressed markets, those who normally post orders widen their prices or step away. A book that looks deep in calm conditions can empty in seconds.

Where liquidity comes from

Much of the standing liquidity in an order book is posted by market makers, firms that quote both a bid and an ask. On decentralized exchanges, liquidity usually comes from pools of tokens supplied by users, explained in AMMs & Liquidity Pools.

Liquidity and volume

Volume counts what has already traded. Liquidity describes what could trade now. The two usually go together, since active markets attract standing orders, but they are different things. A market can show high volume during a panic while its order book is thin.

Other uses of the word

  • Asset liquidity in personal finance means how quickly something can be turned into cash. Cash is the most liquid asset. Property is among the least.
  • In chart commentary, “liquidity” is sometimes used for areas where many stop orders are thought to be resting. That is an interpretation, not something visible in the data.
  • Economists also speak of liquidity in the financial system, meaning how easy it is to borrow money.

The same order in two hypothetical marketsHypothetical illustration

A trader sends a market order to buy 500 units. The best ask in both markets is 100.10.

Deep market. 1,000 units are offered at 100.10. All 500 fill there. Cost: 500 x 100.10 = 50,050.

Thin market. The book is the one shown above.

  • 100 units at 100.10 = 10,010
  • 200 units at 100.50 = 20,100
  • 200 units at 101.00 = 20,200
  • Total: 50,310 for 500 units, an average of 50,310 / 500 = 100.62.

The thin market cost 50,310 - 50,050 = 260 more, or 0.52 per unit, about 0.52% above the best ask. The last price printed in the thin market is now 101.00, even though nothing about the asset changed.

What it does NOT mean

  • Liquid does not mean low-risk. A liquid asset can fall sharply. Liquidity describes the cost of trading, not the direction of price.
  • High volume does not prove deep liquidity. Volume is past trades. Liquidity is the orders waiting now.
  • A quoted price is not available for any size. The best bid and ask each hold a limited number of units.
  • Liquidity today is not liquidity tomorrow. It can shrink quickly when markets are stressed.
  • A large market cap does not mean a liquid asset. Market capitalization can be large while very few units are actually offered for sale.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Judging liquidity by the spread alone. A tight spread with few units behind it only helps small orders.
  2. Assuming an exit is always available at the screen price. In thin markets, selling a large amount can move the price well below the last trade.
  3. Using market orders in thin books without checking depth. The order fills at whatever prices are available, however far away.
  4. Treating one venue’s liquidity as the asset’s liquidity. The same asset can be deep on one exchange and thin on another.

Limits

  • Displayed depth is not the full picture. Orders can be hidden, and displayed orders can be cancelled before they trade.
  • There is no single number for liquidity. Spread, depth and volume each capture part of it.
  • Depth data differs between venues and is not comparable without care.

Key takeaway

Liquidity is how easily you can trade a given size without moving the price. It lives in the orders waiting near the current price, it varies by venue and moment, and it tends to shrink when markets are under stress.
