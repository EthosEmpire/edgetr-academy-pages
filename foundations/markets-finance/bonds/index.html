Bonds

A bond is a loan to a government or company that pays interest and returns the amount borrowed on a set date. Its market price moves in the opposite direction to interest rates.

Why it matters

Bond markets are larger than stock markets in many countries, and they are where interest rates become visible. When news reports say “yields rose”, they are describing bond prices falling.

Bonds matter to crypto learners too. The interest rates set in bond markets influence the price of borrowing everywhere, and some stablecoin issuers report holding short-term government debt as part of their reserves.

How it works

A bond is a loan cut into tradable pieces. The borrower is called the issuer, usually a government or a company. Whoever holds the bond is the lender.

  • Face value (or par value) is the amount the issuer repays at the end.
  • The coupon is the interest, stated as a yearly percentage of the face value.
  • Maturity is the date on which the face value is repaid.
  • The price is what the bond trades for in the market today. It can be above or below the face value.

A bondholder is a lender, not an owner. Compare this with stocks, where the holder owns part of the company and is promised nothing.

In plain words

You lend a friend 1,000 for ten years. They agree to pay you 50 every year and return the 1,000 at the end. You write that promise on a piece of paper. A bond is that piece of paper, and the bond market is where such papers are sold on to other people before the ten years are up.

Price and yield move in opposite directions

A bond’s coupon is fixed when it is issued. Market interest rates are not. That mismatch is why bond prices move.

Suppose the bond above pays 50 a year, and new, similar bonds start paying 60 a year. Nobody will pay 1,000 for the old bond when 1,000 buys a new one that pays more. The old bond’s price has to fall until its overall return matches the new bonds.

The reverse also holds. If new bonds pay only 40 a year, the old bond paying 50 becomes more attractive, and buyers bid its price above 1,000.

Yield is the return a buyer receives at the current price. The fullest measure, yield to maturity, counts both the coupons and the gain or loss between the purchase price and the face value. Because the payments are fixed, a lower price means a higher yield and a higher price means a lower yield. This is arithmetic, not a market opinion.

Going deeper

Who issues bonds

  • Government bonds are issued by national governments. Each country has its own names for them.
  • Corporate bonds are issued by companies.
  • Municipal bonds are issued by cities and regional authorities.

Credit risk

The promise to pay is only as good as the issuer. If an issuer fails to pay interest or principal, it is in default. Rating agencies publish credit ratings, which are opinions about how likely an issuer is to pay. Bonds from issuers seen as more likely to default usually offer higher yields, as compensation for the extra risk.

Longer bonds react more

The further away the repayment date, the more a bond’s price changes for a given change in yields. The measure of this sensitivity is called duration. A bond with one year left moves little when rates change. A bond with thirty years left can move a great deal.

Not every bond pays a coupon

A zero-coupon bond pays no interest. It is sold below its face value and repays the full face value at maturity. The difference is the lender’s return.

How bonds trade

Most bonds trade over the counter between dealers and large investors, not on a central order book. Many individuals hold them through funds, including the bond funds described in ETFs & Indexes.

A hypothetical rate riseHypothetical illustration

A 10-year bond has a face value of 1,000 and a 5% coupon. It pays 50 a year and was issued at 1,000.

Soon after, market yields for similar bonds rise from 5% to 6%.

  • New bonds issued at 1,000 now pay 60 a year.
  • The old bond still pays 50 a year, and that cannot change.
  • Its price falls to about 926. At that price a buyer receives 50 a year, plus a gain of 1,000 - 926 = 74 when the bond matures. Together those add up to a yield of about 6% a year.

A holder who keeps the bond to maturity still receives every coupon and the full 1,000, provided the issuer pays. A holder who sells today receives about 926.

What it does NOT mean

  • A bond is not free of risk. Its price falls when rates rise, the issuer can default, and inflation can erode what the fixed payments buy.
  • A higher yield is not a better deal by itself. A high yield is usually compensation for higher risk or a longer wait.
  • “Fixed income” does not mean a fixed price. The payments are fixed. The market value of the bond changes every day.
  • Rising yields are not good news for existing holders. A rise in yields means the price of bonds already issued has fallen.
  • A credit rating is not a promise. It is an opinion, and rated issuers have defaulted.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Confusing coupon and yield. The coupon is set at issue. The yield depends on the price paid today.
  2. Reading “yields up” as “bonds up”. Yield and price move in opposite directions.
  3. Ignoring maturity. Two bonds from the same issuer can react very differently to the same rate change.
  4. Comparing a bond yield with a crypto yield as if they were alike. The source of the payment, and the risks behind it, are completely different. See APR & APY.

Limits

  • The numbers here use a simplified bond with one payment a year. Real bonds often pay twice a year and follow detailed pricing conventions.
  • Some bonds can be repaid early by the issuer, have floating coupons or are linked to inflation. They behave differently.
  • Why market yields change is a large topic. Interest Rates & Inflation introduces it.

Key takeaway

A bond is a tradable loan with fixed payments. Because the payments are fixed, its price has to fall when market interest rates rise and rise when they fall.
