Market Makers & Liquidity Providers

A market maker continuously quotes a price to buy and a price to sell an asset, earning the spread between them in exchange for carrying the risk of holding inventory.

Why it matters

When you send an order and it fills instantly, someone was already waiting on the other side. Very often that someone is a market maker: a firm whose business is to stand ready to trade with whoever arrives.

Market makers are also a favourite character in trading folklore, credited with secretly steering prices. Understanding how they actually earn and lose money makes those stories easier to judge.

How it works

A market maker posts two limit orders at once: a bid to buy and an ask to sell at a slightly higher price. The gap between them is the spread.

When a seller in a hurry arrives, the market maker buys at its bid. When a buyer in a hurry arrives, it sells at its ask. If both happen, it has bought low and sold slightly higher without taking a view on where the price is heading.

In plain words

A currency exchange booth at an airport buys euros at one rate and sells them at a slightly higher rate. It does not care whether the euro rises or falls next year. It wants a steady stream of travellers going both ways, and it worries about being stuck with a drawer full of one currency when the rate moves.

In return for the spread, the market maker provides liquidity: other participants can trade immediately instead of waiting for a matching order to appear.

The risks a market maker carries

Inventory risk. After buying, the market maker holds the asset until someone buys it back. If the price falls in the meantime, it loses. One adverse move can cost many times the spread.

Adverse selection. Some of the people trading against a market maker know more than it does, for example because news has just come out. The market maker’s stale quote gets hit, and the price then moves against the position it has just taken on.

These risks explain behaviour you can observe. When volatility rises, market makers widen their spreads or reduce the size they quote, because the chance of a loss on inventory is higher. Around major news, displayed liquidity often thins out for the same reason.

Going deeper

Designated and voluntary market makers

Some exchanges appoint designated market makers with formal obligations, such as quoting a minimum size within a maximum spread for a set part of the day, usually in return for fee benefits. Other firms make markets voluntarily wherever they find it worthwhile. Arrangements differ by exchange.

In crypto, exchanges commonly run programmes that give lower fees or rebates to firms providing liquidity, and token projects sometimes engage market-making firms so that their token has a usable order book.

“Maker” in fee schedules

Exchange fee tables use “maker” for any order that rests in the book and “taker” for any order that trades against it, as explained in Trading Fees: Maker & Taker. Anyone placing a resting limit order is a maker in that sense. A market maker is a participant that does this systematically on both sides.

Liquidity providers on decentralized exchanges

On many decentralized exchanges there is no order book. Users called liquidity providers deposit tokens into a pool, and a formula sets the price, as described in AMMs & Liquidity Pools. They receive a share of trading fees and carry a risk comparable to inventory risk, known as impermanent loss.

Staying neutral

Market makers generally try to keep their inventory small. They adjust their quotes to attract trades that reduce it, and they hedge on other venues or with related instruments. Keeping prices consistent across venues overlaps with arbitrage.

A hypothetical good trip and bad tripHypothetical illustration

A market maker quotes a bid of 99.95 and an ask of 100.05, each for 100 units.

Good trip. A seller hits the bid: the market maker pays 100 x 99.95 = 9,995. A buyer then lifts the ask: it receives 100 x 100.05 = 10,005. Result: 10,005 - 9,995 = 10.

Bad trip. A seller hits the bid: the market maker pays 9,995. Before any buyer appears, news arrives and the market drops. The market maker lowers its quotes to 99.45 bid and 99.55 ask, and sells its 100 units at 99.55 for 9,955. Result: 9,955 - 9,995 = a loss of 40.

It takes four good trips to make up for one bad one in this example. The spread is the fee for a service, and it has to cover the bad trips.

What it does NOT mean

  • A market maker does not set the price. It quotes prices that others may accept or ignore. Competing quotes and incoming orders decide where trades happen.
  • The spread is not a certain income. It is earned only when both sides trade before the price moves away, and losses on inventory can exceed it.
  • A market maker is not betting on direction. Its business depends on turnover and on staying close to neutral.
  • Market makers are not obliged to absorb every sell-off. Most can widen or withdraw quotes, and they do so in stressed markets.
  • “The market maker” is not one hidden actor. In active markets many firms compete with each other. Manipulation by any participant is prohibited in regulated markets, and enforcement varies between venues.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Blaming every sharp move on market makers. Thin liquidity and clustered orders explain most sudden wicks without any coordinated plan.
  2. Assuming displayed liquidity is permanent. Quotes can be cancelled in milliseconds.
  3. Confusing a market maker with a broker. A broker acts for a customer. A market maker trades for its own account.
  4. Thinking liquidity provision is effortless income. Both order-book market making and pool deposits carry real risk of loss.

Limits

  • Obligations, incentives and rules for market makers differ between exchanges and jurisdictions.
  • The example ignores fees, hedging and the many quote updates a real firm makes each second.
  • On lightly regulated venues, practices that would be prohibited elsewhere may go unpoliced.

Key takeaway

A market maker quotes both a bid and an ask and earns the spread for letting others trade immediately. The price of that business is inventory risk: holding the asset when the market moves.
