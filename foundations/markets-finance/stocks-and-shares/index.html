Stocks & Shares

A share is one unit of ownership in a company. Stocks trade on exchanges, and their prices reflect what buyers and sellers currently agree the company's shares are worth.

Why it matters

The stock market is the market most people hear about first, and many ideas used in crypto trading were borrowed from it: charts, market capitalization, exchanges and order types.

Understanding what a share actually is also makes one distinction clear. A share is a legal claim on a real business. A chart of its price is a record of trading. The two are related, and they are not the same thing.

How it works

A company can divide its ownership into equal units called shares. All the shares together are the company’s stock, and in everyday speech the two words are used interchangeably. Stocks are also called equities.

If a company has issued 1,000,000 shares and you hold 1,000, you own one thousandth of it.

A company usually becomes publicly traded through an initial public offering (IPO), in which shares are sold to outside investors for the first time. That sale raises money for the company. After the listing, investors buy and sell the shares among themselves on a stock exchange, through brokers. The company is not a party to those trades.

In plain words

Imagine a bakery owned by four friends, each with a quarter. If one friend sells her quarter to a neighbour, the bakery’s ovens, recipes and bank balance do not change. Only the owner of that quarter does. A stock exchange does this for large companies, with millions of small slices changing owners every day.

What a shareholder has

Ordinary shares, known as common stock, typically carry:

  • A residual claim. Shareholders are entitled to what remains of the company’s profits and assets after employees, suppliers, tax authorities and lenders have been paid.
  • Dividends, if declared. A dividend is a payment from the company to shareholders. Companies are not obliged to pay one, and many do not.
  • Voting rights. Shareholders usually vote on matters such as electing the board of directors.
  • Limited liability. A shareholder can lose the amount paid for the shares, but is not personally responsible for the company’s debts.

Some companies also issue preferred shares, which usually have a fixed dividend and priority over common shares, and often no vote. Exact rights depend on the company and the country.

Compare this with bonds, where the holder is a lender, not an owner.

Going deeper

Why the price moves

A share’s price is set the same way as any other market price: by the latest trade between a buyer and a seller. Over time, buyers and sellers react to the company’s results, its prospects, the wider economy, interest rates and each other’s behaviour.

People who study the company itself are doing fundamental analysis. People who study the price history are doing technical analysis. The two are compared in Technical & Fundamental Analysis.

Shares outstanding and float

Shares outstanding is the total number of shares held by all shareholders. The float is the part that is available for public trading, excluding shares held by insiders or locked up. Price multiplied by shares outstanding gives the market capitalization.

Companies can change the share count. Issuing new shares increases it and spreads ownership across more units, an effect called dilution. Buying back shares reduces it.

Stock splits

In a stock split, a company replaces each existing share with several new ones. The price per share falls in proportion, and the value of each holding stays the same. A split changes the unit, not the company.

Trading hours

Stock exchanges have set opening hours and are closed at weekends and on holidays. Prices can therefore open at a different level from the previous close, which appears on a chart as a gap. This is one of several differences from crypto described in Crypto Markets and Stock Markets.

A hypothetical companyHypothetical illustration

A company has 2,000,000 shares outstanding, and the share price is 50.

  • Market capitalization: 2,000,000 x 50 = 100,000,000.
  • An investor holding 4,000 shares owns 4,000 / 2,000,000 = 0.2% of the company. The holding is priced at 4,000 x 50 = 200,000.
  • The company declares a dividend of 1 per share. The investor receives 4,000 x 1 = 4,000.
  • Later the company issues 500,000 new shares. There are now 2,500,000 shares, and the same 4,000 shares represent 4,000 / 2,500,000 = 0.16% of the company.

The investor’s share count never changed. The fraction of the company it represents did.

What it does NOT mean

  • A share price does not tell you how big a company is. A share at 500 can belong to a smaller company than a share at 5. Size depends on the price multiplied by the number of shares.
  • Buying a share on an exchange does not give money to the company. The payment goes to the investor who sold it.
  • Owning shares does not mean a promised income. Dividends are optional and can be reduced or stopped.
  • A stock split does not create value. It cuts the same ownership into more pieces.
  • A rising share price does not prove the business improved. It shows that buyers were willing to pay more.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Calling a low-priced share “cheap”. Price per share means little without the share count and the company’s results.
  2. Ignoring dilution. New shares can shrink each existing holder’s fraction of the company.
  3. Equating the stock with the company. A good business and a rising share price do not always go together, in either direction.
  4. Assuming a token is a share. Most crypto tokens carry no ownership of a company and no legal claim on profits. See Coins & Tokens.

Limits

  • Shareholder rights, taxes and disclosure rules differ between countries and between share classes.
  • Shareholders are last in line if a company fails, and a share can fall to zero.
  • This page covers ordinary listed shares. Private companies, depositary receipts and other structures follow their own rules.

Key takeaway

A share is a unit of ownership in a company, with a claim on what is left after everyone else is paid. Its market price is what the latest buyer and seller agreed, which is a separate thing from the business itself.
