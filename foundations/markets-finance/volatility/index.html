Volatility

Volatility is a measure of how large an asset's price moves are over a period. It describes the size of the swings, not their direction.

Why it matters

Two assets can end a month at the same price and have given their holders completely different experiences. One drifted. The other lurched up and down every day. Volatility is the word for that difference.

It matters for practical reasons. The size of typical price moves affects how far away a stop loss can reasonably sit, how large a position is in relation to an account, and how dangerous leverage is.

What it looks like

Volatility describes how much a price moves, up or down, over a period. In a low-volatility market, candles are small and one day looks much like the next. In a high-volatility market, candles are long, wicks are large and prices cover a lot of ground.

In plain words

Compare a motorway and a mountain road between the same two towns. Both get you there. On the motorway the car barely changes speed. On the mountain road you brake, climb, drop and turn all the way. Volatility describes the road, not the destination.

How it is measured

There are several common ways to put a number on it.

Measure What it does
Range The distance between a period’s high and low
ATR The average of recent ranges, including gaps
Standard deviation of returns How widely the percentage changes scatter around their average
Bollinger Bands Bands placed a number of standard deviations around a moving average, which widen and narrow with volatility

In finance textbooks, “volatility” without qualification usually means the standard deviation of returns, stated as a yearly percentage. Daily figures are converted to yearly ones by multiplying by the square root of the number of trading periods in a year. You do not need the formula to use the idea: a higher figure means larger typical moves.

Going deeper

Historical and implied

Historical volatility is calculated from prices that have already printed. Implied volatility is derived from the prices of options: it is the volatility figure that would make an option pricing model match the option’s market price. It reflects what option traders are paying for, and it can turn out to be too high or too low.

Volatility changes, and it clusters

Volatility is not constant. A well-documented feature of markets is clustering: large moves tend to be followed by more large moves, in either direction, and quiet periods tend to be followed by more quiet. Quiet periods do end, often abruptly.

Volatility is separate from direction

Any of these states can be calm or wild. An uptrend can climb in small, steady steps or in violent swings, and a range can be narrow or wide. Direction and volatility are two different descriptions of the same chart.

Volatility and liquidity

Thin markets are usually more volatile, because each order moves the price further. See Liquidity. This is one reason smaller crypto assets tend to show larger percentage swings than large, heavily traded ones.

Volatility and position size

A position of the same size carries more price risk in a volatile asset than in a calm one. Many practitioners therefore relate the size of a position to the asset’s typical move, an idea covered in Position Sizing. With leverage, a move that is ordinary for a volatile asset can be enough to cause a liquidation.

Two hypothetical assets over one weekHypothetical illustration

This example uses a simplified measure, the average absolute daily move, rather than the standard deviation described above.

Asset A has daily changes of +1%, -1%, +2%, -1% and +1%.

  • Size of the moves, ignoring direction: 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 6.
  • Average absolute daily move: 6 / 5 = 1.2%.

Asset B has daily changes of +5%, -6%, +7%, -4% and +3%.

  • Size of the moves, ignoring direction: 5 + 6 + 7 + 4 + 3 = 25.
  • Average absolute daily move: 25 / 5 = 5%.

Both assets ended the week higher than they started. The direction was the same. The volatility was not: B’s typical day was about four times as large as A’s. A holder of B saw a single-day fall of 6%, while A’s worst day was a fall of 1%.

What it does NOT mean

  • Volatility does not mean falling prices. It measures the size of moves in both directions. A sharply rising market is volatile too.
  • Low volatility does not mean low risk. Quiet markets can change character quickly, and a calm asset can still fall steadily.
  • High volatility does not mean high returns. Large swings make large gains and large losses both possible.
  • Past volatility does not fix future volatility. It is a description of one period and shifts over time.
  • Implied volatility is not a forecast of direction. It is a price-based estimate of the size of moves, and it is often wrong.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Using “volatile” to mean “going down”. The word says nothing about direction.
  2. Comparing price moves in units. A move of 100 means little without the price level. Compare percentages.
  3. Ignoring the timeframe. Volatility measured on one-minute candles and on weekly candles are different quantities.
  4. Assuming calm will last. Narrow ranges are commonly followed by wider ones, with no indication of which way.
  5. Sizing positions the same way in every asset. The same amount carries very different price risk in a calm market and a wild one.

Limits

  • Every measure depends on the lookback period and the timeframe chosen. There is no single “true” volatility.
  • Standard deviation treats upward and downward moves alike and understates the effect of rare, extreme moves.
  • Volatility describes price behaviour only. It does not capture other risks, such as an exchange failing or an asset becoming impossible to sell.

Key takeaway

Volatility measures how big price moves are, not which way they go. It changes over time, tends to come in clusters, and sets the scale for every decision about distance and size.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999)
