Why it matters

Every chart, candle and indicator on this site is a record of something that happened in a market. A price on a screen is not a fact of nature. It is the result of people agreeing to trade with each other.

This is the starting page of the Markets & Finance shelf. It gives you the basic picture that the later pages fill in: who trades, what they trade, where they meet and how a price comes out of it.

How it works

A market is any place or system where buyers and sellers meet to exchange something. It needs three ingredients:

  • Something to trade. In finance this is called an asset: a share, a bond, a currency, a barrel of oil, a coin.
  • People on both sides. Someone willing to buy and someone willing to sell.
  • A way to meet and agree. A physical stall, a trading floor, or today almost always a computer system.

When a buyer and a seller agree, a trade happens. The amount paid per unit is the price. The next page, Buyers, Sellers & Price, looks at that moment in detail.

In plain words

Picture a weekend fruit market. Nobody in charge announces what apples cost. Each stall holder names a price, shoppers accept it or walk on, and by mid-morning most stalls charge roughly the same. A financial market is the same process, done by computers, thousands of times a second.

What a market does

Price discovery is the process of finding the price at which trades actually happen. No single person knows what an asset is “worth”. The market collects many different opinions and needs and turns them into one number that keeps updating.

Liquidity means being able to trade when you want to without moving the price much. A busy market has many participants, so a buyer rarely waits long for a seller. Liquidity has its own page.

Transfer is the practical result. After a trade, the buyer owns the asset and carries its risk, and the seller holds cash instead.

Kinds of markets

Markets are usually named after what trades in them.

Market What is traded Covered in
Stock market Shares of companies Stocks & Shares
Bond market Loans to governments and companies Bonds
Foreign exchange One currency for another Forex & Currency Pairs
Commodity markets Raw materials such as oil, gold and wheat Commodities
Crypto markets Coins and tokens recorded on blockchains What Is Cryptocurrency?

Two other distinctions come up often:

  • In a primary market, an asset is sold for the first time, and the money goes to the issuer. In a secondary market, existing holders trade with each other, and the issuer receives nothing. Almost all the trading you see on a chart is secondary.
  • An exchange is an organised venue with published rules where orders are matched. Over-the-counter (OTC) trading happens directly between two parties, often through dealers, without a central venue.

Going deeper

Most modern markets run on an order book: a live list of the prices at which people are willing to buy and sell, and how much.

The book makes a market’s two sides visible. Buyers queue below the current price, sellers queue above it, and a trade happens when someone accepts the other side’s price.

A market is also rarely one single place. The same share, currency or coin can trade on several venues at once, each with its own order book and a slightly different price. Traders who buy on the cheaper venue and sell on the dearer one, a practice called arbitrage, keep those prices close together.

Finally, markets have rules and operators. Exchanges and brokers run the systems, and regulators in most countries set rules for how they must behave. How strict those rules are differs between asset types and jurisdictions.

One hypothetical trade, start to finishHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset last traded at 100.

  1. Ana wants to sell 10 units and offers them at 100.
  2. Ben wants to buy 10 units and is willing to pay up to 101.
  3. Ben accepts Ana’s offer. Ten units change hands at 100. Ben pays 10 x 100 = 1,000.
  4. The screen now shows a last price of 100 and a volume of 10 units.

Nothing here tells you what happens next. If the next seller only accepts 102 and a buyer agrees, the price becomes 102. If the next buyer only pays 98 and a seller agrees, it becomes 98.

What it does NOT mean

  • A market is not a single building or website. It is the whole set of buyers, sellers and venues trading the same thing.
  • A market price is not set by an authority. It is the price of the most recent trade that two parties agreed to.
  • A market price is not a statement of true value. It records what one buyer and one seller accepted at one moment.
  • A market is not a machine that pays people for taking part. Every trade has a buyer and a seller, and they cannot both turn out to be right about what follows.
  • “The market” does not think or want anything. Phrases such as “the market expects” are shorthand for the combined actions of many participants with different goals.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Thinking someone decides the price. Exchanges publish prices. They do not choose them.
  2. Treating the last price as available to everyone. It is the price of the last trade, for the size that traded. A larger order may fill at a different price.
  3. Assuming all markets work alike. Trading hours, rules, protections and settlement differ a great deal between stocks, currencies and crypto.
  4. Confusing the asset with the market for it. A sound asset can trade in a thin, erratic market, and a busy market can exist for a poor asset.

Limits

  • This page describes how markets work in general. Real venues add many details: fees, order types, auctions and trading halts.
  • Markets can fail to do their jobs. In stressed conditions buyers or sellers may step away, and prices can jump.
  • Rules and investor protections vary by country and by asset type. A description of one market does not automatically apply to another.

Key takeaway

A market is buyers and sellers meeting to exchange an asset, and a price is simply the level at which the latest pair agreed. Everything else on this shelf builds on that picture.
