Buyers, Sellers & Price

A price is the level of the most recent trade between a buyer and a seller. It moves when one side becomes more willing to accept the other side's terms.

Why it matters

“Why did the price go up?” is the first question most people ask about markets. Common answers such as “there were more buyers than sellers” sound right and are not literally true.

Understanding what actually happens when a price changes makes every other topic easier: candles, volume, slippage and the order book all follow from it.

How it works

At any moment a market holds two queues:

  • Bids are prices at which buyers are waiting to buy. The highest is the best bid.
  • Asks (also called offers) are prices at which sellers are waiting to sell. The lowest is the best ask.

The gap between them is the spread. While everyone waits, nothing trades and the price does not change.

A trade happens when someone stops waiting and accepts the other side’s price. A buyer who wants to buy immediately takes the best ask. A seller who wants to sell immediately takes the best bid. The price you see on a chart is the level of the most recent of these trades.

In plain words

Think of a second-hand car. The seller asks 10,000. You offer 9,000. Nothing happens until one of you moves. If you are in a hurry, you pay 10,000. If the seller is in a hurry, they accept 9,000. The sale price depends on who needed the deal more.

How a price moves

A buyer in a hurry uses a market order, which takes whatever offers are available. If the order is bigger than the amount offered at the best ask, it uses up that level and continues to the next, higher one. The last trade is now at a higher price.

The mirror image applies to sellers. A large market sell order uses up the best bid and continues down to lower bids.

So a price rises when buyers are willing to pay up to reach the sellers, and falls when sellers are willing to accept less to reach the buyers. The side that crosses the spread is often called the aggressor.

Waiting orders, called limit orders, also matter. If sellers cancel their offers or move them higher, the price can rise on very little trading, because the next available offer is further away.

Going deeper

Every trade has both sides

Each trade pairs exactly one buyer with one seller for the same number of units. The amount bought always equals the amount sold. “More buyers than sellers” is a figure of speech. What it really describes is buyers being more urgent: more of the trading happened because buyers crossed the spread.

Several prices at once

A screen may show several different numbers:

Term Meaning
Last price The level of the most recent trade
Best bid The highest price a waiting buyer will pay
Best ask The lowest price a waiting seller will accept
Mid price Halfway between the best bid and the best ask

The last price can be stale if nothing has traded for a while. For that reason, professionals often watch the bid and ask more closely than the last trade.

Different venues, slightly different prices

The same asset usually trades on several venues, and each has its own book. Their prices stay close because of arbitrage, but they are rarely identical to the last decimal.

Opening and closing prices

On many stock exchanges the first and last prices of the day are set by an auction: orders are collected for a period and then matched at the single price that allows the most units to trade. This is still buyers and sellers agreeing. It just happens in one batch.

A hypothetical price move with no newsHypothetical illustration

The book shows 50 units offered at 100.10 and 80 units offered at 100.20. The last trade was at 100.00.

A buyer sends a market order for 100 units.

  • 50 units fill at 100.10, costing 50 x 100.10 = 5,005.
  • 50 units fill at 100.20, costing 50 x 100.20 = 5,010.
  • Total cost: 10,015 for 100 units, an average of 100.15.

The last price is now 100.20, up 0.20 from 100.00. Thirty units remain offered at 100.20. One hundred units were bought and one hundred units were sold, by the sellers who had been waiting.

What it does NOT mean

  • A rising price does not mean there were more buyers than sellers. Units bought always equal units sold. It means buyers were willing to pay higher prices.
  • A price is not set by the exchange, a regulator or a committee. It is the level of the latest trade between two participants.
  • The last price is not a promise. It is where the previous trade happened. The next order may fill higher or lower.
  • A price change does not need news. One large order, or a few cancelled offers, can move the price in a thin market.
  • A higher price does not show that the asset became more valuable. It shows what the most recent pair of traders agreed to.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Believing price follows opinion polls. Opinions only matter when they turn into orders.
  2. Ignoring size. The best ask may hold only a few units. A larger order reaches deeper, less favourable prices.
  3. Reading a small trade as a market verdict. In a quiet market a single small trade can print a new last price.
  4. Assuming buyers are “bullish” and sellers “bearish”. People sell to pay bills, rebalance or close an earlier trade. A seller’s reason is not visible in the price.
  5. Expecting one global price. Each venue reports its own trades.

Limits

  • This page describes order-book markets. Some markets work through dealers who quote prices directly, and some crypto venues use pools and formulas instead, as described in AMMs & Liquidity Pools.
  • A book shows visible orders only. Hidden orders and orders that have not yet been sent are not displayed.
  • Knowing how a price moved says nothing certain about where it moves next.

Key takeaway

A price is the level of the latest trade, and every trade has one buyer and one seller. Prices move when one side is willing to cross the spread and accept the other side’s terms.
