Interest Rates & Inflation

Inflation is a general rise in prices that reduces what money can buy. Interest rates are the price of borrowing money, and central banks adjust their policy rates largely in response to inflation.

Why it matters

Interest rates and inflation are the background against which every asset is priced. They decide what cash earns, what loans cost and how much future money is worth today. Financial news treats central bank meetings as major events for this reason.

The two ideas are also widely confused with each other, and with the “inflation” of a crypto asset’s supply. This page separates them.

Inflation

Inflation is a general, sustained rise in the prices of goods and services. The other side of the same fact is that each unit of money buys less. That is a fall in purchasing power.

Inflation is usually measured with a consumer price index (CPI): statisticians price a fixed basket of typical household purchases and track how the total changes. An inflation rate of 5% means the basket costs 5% more than a year earlier.

Related terms:

Term Meaning
Inflation Prices in general are rising
Disinflation Prices are still rising, but more slowly than before
Deflation Prices in general are falling
Hyperinflation Extremely rapid, out-of-control inflation

In plain words

Imagine your weekly shop costs 100. A year later the same trolley of goods costs 105. Your banknotes have not changed, but each one covers a little less of the trolley. Inflation is the trolley getting more expensive. Purchasing power is how much trolley your money covers.

Interest rates

An interest rate is the price of borrowing money, stated as a percentage per year. A borrower pays it. A lender or saver receives it.

There are many interest rates in an economy. Most of them take their lead from the policy rate set by the central bank, the public institution responsible for a currency, such as the U.S. Federal Reserve or the European Central Bank. The policy rate applies to very short-term funds that commercial banks borrow or deposit, and it influences the rates those banks offer to everyone else.

Central banks typically raise rates when inflation is above their goal and lower them when the economy is weak and inflation is low. Many, including the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank, aim for inflation of about 2% a year. Higher rates generally make borrowing more expensive and saving more attractive, which tends to cool spending. Lower rates tend to do the opposite.

Putting them together: real rates

The quoted rate is the nominal rate. The real rate is what remains after inflation, and a common approximation is:

Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate - inflation rateIf savings earn 4% while prices rise 3%, purchasing power grows by about 1%. If prices rise 6%, it shrinks by about 2%.

A positive nominal rate can therefore still mean a loss of purchasing power.

Going deeper

Why rates matter for asset prices

Money received in the future is worth less than money today, because money today can earn interest. Working out today’s value of a future payment is called discounting. At an interest rate of 5%, a payment of 100 due in one year is worth 100 / 1.05 = about 95.24 today. At 10%, it is worth 100 / 1.10 = about 90.91.

The higher the rate, the lower the present value of the same future payment. This is the arithmetic behind the inverse relationship between bond prices and yields. It is also one reason commentators link rates to exchange rates and to the prices of shares and other assets. For assets without fixed payments the link is looser, and it has not been stable through history.

Expectations

Markets react to the difference between what happens and what was expected. A rate rise that everyone anticipated may cause little movement, which is why the same kind of announcement can be followed by opposite price reactions on different occasions.

Inflation of a token’s supply

In crypto, “inflation” often means something else: the rate at which new units of a coin are created. A token whose supply grows 5% a year is said to have 5% supply inflation. That is a statement about the number of units, not about consumer prices. See Token Unlocks & Emissions and The 21 Million Limit. A fixed or slowly growing supply does not by itself determine an asset’s price, because demand matters equally, as explained in Supply & Demand.

A hypothetical year of savingsHypothetical illustration

A basket of goods costs 100 today. A saver holds 100 in an account paying 3% interest. Inflation over the year is 5%.

  • After one year the account holds 100 x 1.03 = 103.
  • The basket now costs 100 x 1.05 = 105.
  • The savings buy 103 / 105 = about 98.1% of the basket.

The balance rose by 3. Its purchasing power fell by about 1.9%, close to the approximation of 3% - 5% = -2%.

What it does NOT mean

  • Inflation does not mean every price rises. It is an average. Some prices fall while the general level rises.
  • Lower inflation does not mean prices are falling. It means they are rising more slowly. Falling prices are deflation.
  • A central bank does not set all interest rates. It sets a short-term policy rate. Other rates are set in markets and by lenders, and can move differently.
  • A rate change does not have a fixed effect on any asset. The reaction depends on what was expected and on everything else happening at the time.
  • A token’s supply inflation is not consumer price inflation. One counts new units of an asset. The other measures the cost of living.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Looking only at the nominal rate. A 4% return with 6% inflation is a loss of purchasing power.
  2. Treating a crypto yield like a bank interest rate. The source of the payment and the risks are different. See APR & APY.
  3. Assuming scarcity protects against inflation. Whether any asset keeps its purchasing power depends on its future price, which nobody knows.

Limits

  • Inflation measures depend on the basket chosen. An individual’s own cost of living can differ from the official figure.
  • The chain from policy rate to prices is a simplification. Economists disagree about its strength and timing.
  • This page describes how the concepts work. It says nothing about where rates or inflation are heading.

Key takeaway

Inflation is the general rise in prices that erodes purchasing power, and interest rates are the price of borrowing that central banks adjust in response. What matters to a saver or lender is the real rate: interest minus inflation.
