Trading Volume Explained

Trading volume is the number of units of an asset that changed hands in a period. Every unit counted was bought by one party and sold by another.

Why it matters

Price tells you where trades happened. Volume tells you how much traded there. A move from 100 to 105 on ten units and the same move on ten million units are very different events, and only volume separates them.

Volume is also described in misleading ways. Phrases such as “buying volume”, “more buyers than sellers” and “money flowing in” suggest things the number does not measure.

How it works

Volume is the total number of units traded during a period: a minute, a day, or the last 24 hours. Each trade adds its size to the total once.

Every trade has a buyer and a seller for the same quantity. If volume for the day is 1,000,000 units, then 1,000,000 units were bought and 1,000,000 units were sold. There is no such thing as a trade with only a buyer.

On most charts, a volume bar is coloured to match its candle: one colour if the price closed higher than it opened, another if it closed lower. The colour describes the candle’s direction. It does not split the volume into “buys” and “sells”.

In plain words

Think of a ticket barrier counting people entering a stadium. The counter tells you how busy the match is. It does not tell you which team the crowd supports or who will win. Volume is the counter.

How to read it

Volume is almost always read relative to what is normal for that asset and time of day. Conventional interpretations, as described in standard technical analysis references, include:

Situation Commonly interpreted as What it cannot tell you
Large price move, high volume Many participants took part in the move Whether the move continues
Large price move, low volume Few units moved the price, often in a thin market Whether the move is “fake”
Small price move, high volume Heavy trading in which neither side gained ground Which side gives way next
Small price move, low volume A quiet market How long the quiet lasts

The Volume indicator page covers chart reading in more detail, and breakouts have their own page.

Going deeper

What “more buyers than sellers” really means

Since units bought equal units sold, the phrase cannot be literally true. It is a figure of speech for buyers being more aggressive: more of the trades happened because a buyer accepted a seller’s asking price than the other way round. The mechanics are in Buyers, Sellers & Price.

Some data feeds label each trade by which side was the aggressor and report “buy volume” and “sell volume” on that basis. Even then, each of those trades had both a buyer and a seller.

Units or money

Volume can be reported in units of the asset or in the money paid. For a pair such as BTC/USDT, exchanges often show both: volume in BTC and volume in USDT. The second is sometimes called quote volume or turnover. The two rank assets very differently, so check which one a site is showing.

Volume differs by venue

A stock’s volume is usually consolidated across the venues where it trades. Crypto assets trade on many separate exchanges, each reporting its own figure. Aggregator sites add them up, using their own choices about which exchanges to include.

Reported volume is not always reliable. Wash trading, in which the same party trades with itself to create the appearance of activity, inflates volume and is prohibited in regulated markets. It has been documented on some crypto venues.

Volume, liquidity and open interest

  • Liquidity describes orders waiting to trade. Volume counts trades already done.
  • Open interest counts derivative contracts that currently exist. Volume counts how many traded. A day can have high volume and unchanged open interest.

Forex has no central exchange, so volume on a forex chart is usually one platform’s figure or a count of price updates.

Counting a hypothetical minuteHypothetical illustration

Three trades happen in one minute:

  • 100 units at 20.00, worth 100 x 20.00 = 2,000
  • 250 units at 20.10, worth 250 x 20.10 = 5,025
  • 150 units at 20.20, worth 150 x 20.20 = 3,030

Volume in units: 100 + 250 + 150 = 500.

Volume in money: 2,000 + 5,025 + 3,030 = 10,055.

The candle for that minute opened at 20.00 and closed at 20.20, so its volume bar is drawn in the “up” colour. All 500 units are shown in that colour, although each one was sold by someone as well as bought by someone.

What it does NOT mean

  • High volume does not mean more buyers than sellers. It means many units changed hands, with a buyer and a seller on every trade.
  • Volume is not money entering the market. The buyer’s money goes to the seller. The total is a count of activity.
  • A green volume bar is not “buy volume”. The colour follows the candle’s direction.
  • High volume does not confirm that a move will continue. It shows participation in what already happened.
  • Reported volume is not always real. On lightly regulated venues it can be inflated.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Comparing raw volume across assets. A million units of one asset and a million of another are unrelated quantities. Compare each asset with its own average.
  2. Reading every volume spike as meaningful. Scheduled events, index changes and contract expiries cause spikes that say little about direction.
  3. Mixing unit volume with money volume. Rankings change completely depending on which is used.
  4. Treating volume as a signal by itself. It is context for price, not a replacement for it.
  5. Ignoring the venue. A chart shows the volume of one exchange unless stated otherwise.

Limits

  • Volume says how much traded, not who traded or why.
  • Trades done away from public venues may be reported late or not appear on a chart at all.
  • Volume-based indicators such as OBV and VWAP inherit every weakness of the underlying data.

Key takeaway

Volume is a count of units that changed hands, each one both bought and sold. It measures how much activity stood behind a price move, and nothing about which way price goes next.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999). Chapter on volume and open interest gives the conventional interpretation of volume
  • Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners, Larry Harris (2003). Explains how trades are reported and counted
  • Understanding Market Liquidity and Your Investments (opens in a new tab), FINRA. Notes that securities with higher trading volume are generally more liquid
