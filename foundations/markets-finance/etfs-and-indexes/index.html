ETFs & Indexes

An index is a calculated number that tracks a defined group of assets. An ETF is a fund whose shares trade on an exchange, and most ETFs are built to follow an index.

Why it matters

When a headline says “the market rose 1% today”, it is quoting an index. When people talk about “buying the market”, they usually mean a fund that follows one. The two words are often used as if they were the same thing, and they are not.

Indexes are also the standard yardstick. A fund, a portfolio or a single stock is commonly compared with an index to judge how it did.

How it works

An index is a number calculated from the prices of a defined group of assets, according to published rules. An index provider decides which assets are included and how much each one counts. An index cannot be bought. It is a measurement.

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is an investment fund whose shares trade on an exchange throughout the day, the same way stocks do. Most ETFs are designed to follow a specific index by holding the same assets in the same proportions. The investor holds shares of the fund, and the fund holds the assets.

In plain words

An index is a recipe: “six parts of company A, three parts of B, one part of C”. Nobody can eat a recipe. An ETF is a kitchen that cooks the recipe in bulk and sells it by the slice. The slice changes hands at the market, and its price follows the cost of the ingredients.

How an index is weighted

The weighting rule decides how much each member moves the index.

Method How weights are set Effect
Market-cap weighted By each company’s market capitalization The largest companies dominate
Price weighted By share price High-priced shares dominate, whatever the company’s size
Equal weighted Every member the same Small members count as much as large ones

Most widely followed stock indexes are market-cap weighted. A few older ones are price weighted. Two indexes covering similar companies can therefore show different results on the same day.

How an ETF stays close to its holdings

The value of everything a fund owns, divided by the number of fund shares, is its net asset value (NAV) per share. An ETF’s market price is set by buyers and sellers, so it could in principle drift away from NAV.

In practice, large firms called authorized participants can exchange baskets of the underlying assets for new ETF shares, and the reverse. If the ETF trades above the value of its holdings, they can deliver the assets, receive ETF shares and sell them. If it trades below, they can do the opposite. This is a form of arbitrage, and it usually keeps the price close to NAV. The gap can widen when the underlying assets are hard to trade or their own market is closed.

Going deeper

Costs. A fund charges a yearly fee, called the expense ratio, taken from the fund’s assets. Trading the shares also involves the bid-ask spread and any broker commission.

Tracking difference. A fund rarely matches its index exactly. Fees, trading costs and the practical difficulty of holding every member cause small gaps.

Not every ETF follows a broad index. Some follow a single sector, a commodity or a bond index. Some are actively managed and follow no index. Leveraged and inverse ETFs aim to deliver a multiple, or the opposite, of an index’s move for a single day. Over longer periods their results can differ substantially from that multiple.

Crypto. In some jurisdictions, exchange-traded products hold crypto assets or futures on them. Their shares trade on stock exchanges during stock market hours, while the underlying crypto market trades around the clock. Crypto data sites also publish indexes of coins, built with the same weighting ideas.

A hypothetical index moveHypothetical illustration

An index contains three companies with market capitalizations of 600, 300 and 100, a total of 1,000. Their weights are 60%, 30% and 10%.

Day 1. Company A rises 10%, and the others are unchanged. A’s capitalization becomes 660 and the total becomes 1,060. The index is up 60 / 1,000 = 6%.

Day 2, starting again from the original values. Company C rises 10%, and the others are unchanged. C becomes 110 and the total becomes 1,010. The index is up 10 / 1,000 = 1%.

The same 10% move changed the index by 6% in one case and 1% in the other. In an equal-weighted version, either move would have changed the index by about 3.3%.

What it does NOT mean

  • An index is not something you can own. It is a calculation. Only products that follow it can be bought.
  • An ETF is not automatically diversified. Some hold thousands of assets and some hold a handful, or one.
  • “The index went up” does not mean most of its members went up. In a cap-weighted index, a few large members can lift the number while most members fall.
  • An ETF does not remove risk. A fund that follows an index falls when the index falls.
  • An ETF’s price is not exactly its NAV. The two are usually close, and the gap can widen in stressed or closed markets.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Using “index” and “ETF” as synonyms. One is a number. The other is a fund with fees, a manager and shares.
  2. Assuming all indexes are built alike. The weighting rule changes what the number means.
  3. Treating a leveraged ETF as a long-term multiple of the index. These funds target a daily multiple, and compounding makes longer periods behave differently.
  4. Overlooking what is inside. Two funds with similar names can hold quite different assets.

Limits

  • Fund structures, investor protections and tax treatment differ by country.
  • Index providers change their membership and rules over time, so an index is not a fixed list.
  • This page describes typical ETFs. Complex products carry extra risks described in their own documents.

Key takeaway

An index is a rule-based measurement of a group of assets, and an ETF is a fund that trades on an exchange and usually follows one. Knowing how the index is weighted tells you what its number is really measuring.
