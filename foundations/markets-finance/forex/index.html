Forex & Currency Pairs

Forex is the market where currencies are exchanged for one another. A quote such as EUR/USD gives the price of one unit of the first currency in terms of the second.

Why it matters

Every time someone pays for imports, travels abroad or buys a foreign asset, one currency is exchanged for another. The market where this happens is foreign exchange, shortened to forex or FX. By the value traded each day, it is the largest financial market in the world.

Forex also teaches a habit that crypto trading depends on. Every crypto price is a pair: BTC/USDT is read exactly like EUR/USD.

How it works

A currency has no price by itself. It only has a price in terms of another currency, called an exchange rate. That is why currencies are always quoted in pairs.

In a pair written as EUR/USD:

  • the first currency, EUR, is the base currency;
  • the second currency, USD, is the quote currency;
  • the number is how many units of the quote currency one unit of the base currency costs.

If EUR/USD rises, one euro buys more dollars: the euro has strengthened against the dollar, and the dollar has weakened against the euro. Both statements describe the same move.

In plain words

A price tag in a shop says “bread: 3 dollars”. Bread is the thing, dollars are the money. A currency pair is the same tag with a currency in place of the bread: “one euro: 1.25 dollars”. The only twist is that the “thing” is also money, so you can turn the tag around and read it as “one dollar: 0.80 euros”.

Reading a quote

A currency dealer or trading platform shows two prices, as described in Bid, Ask & Spread:

Term Meaning Hypothetical value
Bid Price at which you can sell the base currency 1.2500
Ask Price at which you can buy the base currency 1.2502
Spread The difference between them 0.0002

Small moves are measured in pips. For most pairs a pip is 0.0001, the fourth decimal place. For pairs quoted in Japanese yen it is conventionally 0.01. The spread above is 2 pips.

Going deeper

A market without a central exchange

Forex has no single exchange. It is an over-the-counter market: banks, dealers and electronic platforms quote prices to each other and to their clients. There is therefore no single official price or official volume figure. The volume shown on a forex chart usually comes from one platform, or counts price updates instead of units traded.

Trading hours

Because trading passes from one time zone to the next, forex runs 24 hours a day from Monday to Friday and pauses at the weekend. Activity is heaviest when two sessions overlap. Crypto spot markets go one step further and do not close at all, as described in A Market That Never Closes.

What moves exchange rates

Exchange rates respond to differences between two economies: interest rates and inflation, trade flows, growth and political events. Since a pair compares two currencies, a move can come from either side.

Who trades

Banks, companies paying for foreign goods, investment funds, central banks and individual traders all take part. Retail forex trading is often offered with high leverage, which magnifies losses as well as gains, and a leveraged position can lose more than the money deposited. Regulators have published warnings: the U.S. CFTC reports that, over the year from the second quarter of 2021 to the first quarter of 2022, about one third of customers at registered retail forex dealers made a profit and about two thirds lost money. Most beginners should treat leveraged currency trading as high risk and read Leverage Risk first.

The link to crypto

Crypto pairs follow the same convention. In BTC/USDT, BTC is the base and USDT, a stablecoin, is the quote. In ETH/BTC, the price of one ether is given in bitcoin.

A hypothetical exchange rate moveHypothetical illustration

EUR/USD is 1.2500.

  • Exchanging 1,000 euros gives 1,000 x 1.25 = 1,250 dollars.
  • Reading the pair the other way: one dollar costs 1 / 1.25 = 0.80 euros.

Later, EUR/USD is 1.3000.

  • Exchanging 1,000 euros now gives 1,000 x 1.30 = 1,300 dollars.
  • The rate moved by 1.3000 - 1.2500 = 0.0500, which is 500 pips.
  • In percentage terms the euro rose 0.05 / 1.25 = 4% against the dollar.

Someone holding euros can now buy more dollars. Someone holding dollars can buy fewer euros. Whether that is good or bad depends entirely on which currency a person holds and which one they need.

What it does NOT mean

  • A rising pair does not mean a “good” currency. It means the base currency gained against that one quote currency. It may be falling against others.
  • A higher number does not mean a more valuable currency. One unit of a currency being worth 0.01 of another says nothing about the strength of either economy. Only changes in the rate carry information.
  • There is no single official forex price. Each dealer and platform quotes its own, and they are kept close by competition and arbitrage.
  • Forex volume on a chart is not total market volume. It reflects one data source.
  • A 24-hour market is not equally active all day. Quiet hours have thinner trading and often wider spreads.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Reading the pair backwards. The price belongs to the first currency. EUR/USD rising means the euro strengthened.
  2. Forgetting there are two sides. A pair can rise because the base strengthened, because the quote weakened, or both.
  3. Treating small pip moves as small risks. With leverage, a move of a fraction of one percent can produce a large loss. See Leverage Risk.
  4. Comparing rates across pairs. A rate of 150 in one pair and 1.25 in another are not comparable numbers.

Limits

  • Quoting conventions, pip sizes and the way pairs are grouped vary between sources and platforms.
  • Some currencies are managed or pegged by their governments and do not float freely.
  • This page covers spot exchange. Forwards, swaps and other currency contracts add further detail.

Key takeaway

A currency pair gives the price of one unit of the base currency in the quote currency. Every move in a pair is one currency strengthening and the other weakening at the same time.
