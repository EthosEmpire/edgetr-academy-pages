Technical & Fundamental Analysis

Technical analysis studies price and volume history. Fundamental analysis studies the underlying business or asset. They ask different questions, and neither produces certainty.

Why it matters

People who study markets tend to fall into two camps, and beginners are often told to pick one. Technical analysis looks at charts. Fundamental analysis looks at what the asset is. Arguments between the two can be heated.

Most of this site’s library covers technical concepts, so it is worth being clear at the start about what that approach is, what the alternative is and what neither can do.

How they differ

Technical analysis (TA) studies the history of an asset’s price and volume. Its tools are market structure, support and resistance, chart patterns and indicators. It does not ask what the asset is worth. It asks how the price has behaved and how participants have reacted at particular levels.

Fundamental analysis (FA) studies the thing being traded and the conditions around it, in order to estimate what it might be worth. For a company that means reading its financial statements and judging its prospects. The estimate is then compared with the market price.

In plain words

Two people are deciding whether a restaurant is any good. One reads the menu, checks the kitchen and looks at the accounts. The other stands outside and watches the queue: how long it is, whether it is growing, when people leave. The first is studying the restaurant. The second is studying how the public behaves toward it. Both learn something real, and either can be wrong.

Technical analysis in brief

Standard references describe three working assumptions behind technical analysis:

  1. Market action reflects known information. Whatever is publicly known is assumed to be already expressed in the price.
  2. Prices move in trends. A trend in place is treated as more likely to continue than to reverse, until evidence shows otherwise.
  3. Patterns of behaviour recur. Because people react in similar ways to similar situations, chart shapes repeat often enough to be studied.

These are assumptions, not proven laws. They are the reason a technician feels able to work from the chart alone.

Fundamental analysis in brief

For shares, fundamental analysis draws on a company’s published accounts: what it sells, what it earns, what it owns and what it owes. Analysts often summarise these with ratios. A common one is the price-to-earnings ratio (P/E): the share price divided by the profit per share.

Crypto assets usually have no earnings in the company sense. Fundamental work there looks at a token’s supply and release schedule, how the network is used, what fees it generates and how the token is designed, topics covered in Tokenomics. The methods are less settled than for shares, and analysts disagree more.

Going deeper

Time horizon

Fundamentals change slowly. A company reports results a few times a year. For this reason fundamental analysis is mostly associated with longer holding periods, and technical analysis is used on every timeframe down to minutes. The contrast is discussed in Trading and Investing.

Using both

The approaches are not mutually exclusive. Many practitioners use fundamentals to decide what to study and charts to describe how it is currently trading. Others use only one. No combination removes uncertainty.

The sceptical view

A long-standing argument in economics, the efficient market hypothesis, holds that prices already reflect available information, so that neither approach can reliably outperform. The evidence is debated. It is a useful reminder that when an analysis seems obvious, thousands of other participants have probably seen the same thing.

Known weaknesses

Technical analysis Fundamental analysis
Subjectivity Two people can draw different lines on the same chart Two analysts can reach very different value estimates
Data Built entirely on past prices Reported data arrives late and can be revised or misstated
Typical failure Seeing patterns in noise Price staying away from the estimate for years

One hypothetical company, two descriptionsHypothetical illustration

A company’s share trades at 50. Its profit over the past year was 2.50 per share.

A fundamental description. The P/E ratio is 50 / 2.50 = 20. Similar companies trade at an average P/E of 15. At that ratio the share would be 15 x 2.50 = 37.50. The analyst notes that the market is paying more for each unit of this company’s profit than for its peers’, which may reflect expected growth or may reflect optimism.

A technical description. Over six months the price has made a series of higher highs and higher lows, from 40 to 50, and is above its rising 50-day moving average. The analyst notes an uptrend that remains intact while the most recent higher low, at 46, holds.

Both descriptions are accurate, and they do not conflict. One says the share is priced above its peers. The other says the price has been rising. Neither says what the price does next.

What it does NOT mean

  • Technical analysis does not predict prices. It describes past behaviour and frames scenarios. Its patterns fail regularly.
  • Fundamental analysis does not reveal a true value. It produces an estimate built on assumptions about an unknown future.
  • A low price relative to an estimate does not mean the price must rise. Markets can disagree with an estimate indefinitely, and the estimate may be wrong.
  • Using both methods does not create certainty. Two uncertain views that agree are still uncertain.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Treating the choice as a matter of loyalty. They are tools for different questions, not rival teams.
  2. Believing charts work the same without context. A pattern on an asset with almost no trading says very little.
  3. Applying share valuation ratios directly to tokens. Most tokens have no earnings and no claim on a company.
  4. Using analysis to justify a decision already made. Looking only for evidence that agrees is confirmation bias.

Limits

  • Both approaches rely on the past, in the form of past prices or past reports.
  • Research on whether either can produce consistent results is mixed.
  • This page is an overview. Each method contains many schools that disagree among themselves.

Key takeaway

Technical analysis studies price and volume, and fundamental analysis studies the asset behind the price. Each answers a different question, and neither turns an uncertain future into a known one.

Sources and further reading

  • Technical Analysis of the Financial Markets, John J. Murphy (1999). Chapter 1 sets out the premises of technical analysis and contrasts it with fundamental analysis
  • What Is Momentum Investing? (opens in a new tab), FINRA (2025). Contrasts technical analysis (price-based indicators and trends) with fundamental analysis (a company's finances and industry)
  • Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements (opens in a new tab), U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission. Explains the balance sheet, income statement and cash flow statement, the statements investors use to evaluate a company
  • Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners, Larry Harris (2003). Discusses informed traders, value estimation and market efficiency
