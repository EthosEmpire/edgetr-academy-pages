Assets & Asset Classes

An asset is something of economic value that can be owned and traded. Asset classes are groups of assets that share the same basic features and behave in broadly similar ways.

Why it matters

Market news is full of labels: equities, fixed income, commodities, FX, digital assets. Each label names an asset class, a family of things that are traded.

Knowing the families tells you what you are looking at when you open a chart. A share, a bond and a coin can all have a price of 100, and that is about all they have in common.

How it works

An asset is anything with economic value that can be owned. A house, a share of a company, a bar of gold and a bitcoin are all assets.

An asset class is a group of assets with similar features: what the holder legally owns, where any return comes from, how it is traded and what rules apply. Grouping assets is useful because members of a class tend to respond to similar forces.

In plain words

Think of vehicles. A bicycle, a lorry and a ferry all move people or goods, but nobody expects them to behave alike. You group them into classes because the class tells you what to expect: what fuel it needs, where it can travel, what licence applies. Asset classes do the same job for things with a price.

The main classes

Stocks (equities). A stock is a share of ownership in a company. Holders have a claim on the company’s profits and assets after its debts are paid. See Stocks & Shares.

Bonds (fixed income). A bond is a loan to a government or company. The issuer promises to pay interest and return the amount borrowed on a set date. See Bonds.

Cash and cash equivalents. Bank deposits and very short-term loans. Their price barely moves, but their purchasing power changes with inflation.

Commodities. Raw materials such as oil, gold, copper and wheat. See Commodities.

Currencies. National money, traded in pairs on the foreign exchange market. See Forex & Currency Pairs.

Real estate. Land and buildings. Property is traded far less often than the other classes and each unit is different.

Crypto assets. Coins and tokens recorded on a blockchain. This is the newest class and a very mixed one: it includes assets designed as money, as fuel for a network, as claims on a pool and more. See What Is Cryptocurrency? and Coins & Tokens.

Going deeper

Derivatives and funds are wrappers

Two things are often listed beside asset classes without quite being one.

A derivative is a contract whose value depends on another asset, called the underlying. Futures and options are derivatives. A gold future belongs with commodities in terms of what moves its price, but it is a contract, not gold. See What Are Derivatives?.

A fund pools many investors’ money and holds assets on their behalf. A fund that holds shares behaves like the stock asset class. See ETFs & Indexes.

Why classes matter: they move differently

Assets in the same class often rise and fall together, because they react to the same news. Assets in different classes sometimes move independently. How closely two assets move together is called correlation, covered in Diversification & Correlation.

Correlations are not fixed. Classes that usually move apart have at times fallen together, especially in periods of market stress.

The borders are fuzzy

Classification is a convention, and sources disagree at the edges. Some list currencies as a class and some do not. Regulators in different countries classify the same crypto asset differently. A class label is a starting point for understanding an asset, not a full description of it.

Three hypothetical assets, all priced at 100Hypothetical illustration

  • A share at 100. The company has 1,000,000 shares. One share is a one-millionth part of the company. If the company pays a dividend of 2 per share, a holder of 10 shares receives 20.
  • A bond at 100. It has a face value of 100 and pays 4% interest a year. A holder receives 4 a year and 100 back at maturity, provided the issuer pays as promised.
  • A coin at 100. The network has issued 5,000,000 coins. A holder owns units recorded on a blockchain. Whether holding them brings anything besides price change depends on the design of that particular coin.

The prices match. The rights, the sources of return and the risks do not.

What it does NOT mean

  • Asset classes are not a ranking. No class is better, safer or more profitable by nature. Each has its own risks.
  • Belonging to a class does not make assets identical. Two stocks, or two tokens, can differ more from each other than a typical stock differs from a typical bond.
  • A class label is not a legal definition. How an asset is regulated depends on the jurisdiction and on the asset’s specific features.
  • Different classes do not always move differently. Correlations change, and in stressed markets many assets have fallen together.
  • A derivative on an asset is not the asset. The contract adds its own terms, costs and risks.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Comparing unit prices across assets. A coin at 0.50 is not “cheaper” than a share at 500. The units are unrelated. Market capitalization is the usual way to compare size.
  2. Assuming every asset pays income. Dividends and interest exist for some classes. Commodities and many crypto assets pay nothing.
  3. Treating “crypto” as one thing. The class contains very different designs with very different risks.
  4. Forgetting the wrapper. A fund or derivative based on an asset adds fees, rules and counterparties of its own.

Limits

  • The groupings on this page are a common convention. Textbooks, regulators and firms draw the lines differently.
  • A class describes typical features. It cannot tell you how any single asset will behave.
  • Past relationships between classes are observations, not rules.

Key takeaway

An asset class is a family of assets that give the holder the same kind of thing: ownership, a loan, a good, money, a digital unit or a contract. The label tells you what kind of thing you are looking at, not how it will perform.
