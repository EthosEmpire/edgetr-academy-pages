Market Capitalization

Market capitalization is the current price of one unit multiplied by the number of units outstanding. It measures size at the latest price, not the amount of money invested.

Why it matters

Market capitalization, or market cap, is the standard way to compare the size of companies and of crypto assets. Rankings of the “largest” stocks and coins are sorted by it, and stock indexes are usually weighted by it.

It is also one of the most misread numbers in finance. Headlines such as “billions wiped off the market” make it sound like a pile of money. It is not a pile of money. It is a multiplication.

How it is calculated

Market capitalization = price per unit x number of units outstandingFor a company, units are shares outstanding. For a crypto asset, units are usually the coins in circulation.

If a company has 1,000,000 shares outstanding and the latest price is 50, its market cap is 50,000,000.

For crypto assets the usual convention multiplies the price by the circulating supply: the coins that exist and are available to the market. Coins that are locked or not yet created are left out. Counting them as well gives a different figure, the fully diluted valuation, explained in Market Cap & Fully Diluted Valuation. Supply definitions are covered in Token Supply.

In plain words

A street has 100 similar houses. One sells for 300,000. An estate agent might say the street is “worth” 100 x 300,000 = 30,000,000. Nobody paid 30,000,000. One house was sold, and its price was applied to all the others. Market cap is that estimate, recalculated at every trade.

Why unit price is not size

The price of one unit says nothing without the number of units. A coin priced at 0.50 is not “cheap” and one priced at 500 is not “expensive”. Both coins above have the same market cap. A company can change its unit price overnight with a stock split, and its market cap does not move.

This is why size comparisons use market cap, never the price per unit.

Market cap is not money invested

The price of an asset is set by the most recent trade, as explained in Buyers, Sellers & Price. Market cap takes that one price and applies it to every unit in existence, including the vast majority that did not trade.

So market cap can rise or fall by far more than the money that changed hands. When the price rises, no matching sum of new money has entered. When the price falls, no matching sum has left: every seller’s units went to a buyer who paid for them. What changed is the price at which everyone’s holdings are marked.

Going deeper

Size labels

Stocks are often described as large cap, mid cap or small cap. The cut-off values are conventions that differ between index providers and change over time. The same labels are used loosely for crypto assets.

Float and circulating supply

Some shares are held by founders or governments and rarely trade. The free float is the part actually available to the market. Many indexes weight companies by float-adjusted market cap. In crypto, the reported circulating supply depends on the data provider’s method and on information from the project, so two sites can show different market caps for the same coin.

Market cap and liquidity

A market cap figure assumes every unit could be valued at the last price. Whether units can actually be sold near that price depends on liquidity. An asset where very few units trade can show a large market cap from a handful of small trades at a high price.

Total market cap

Adding the market caps of all assets in a group gives a total, such as “total crypto market cap”. The same caution applies: it is the sum of many multiplications, not a count of money held in the market.

A hypothetical price fallHypothetical illustration

A coin has 10,000,000 units in circulation and trades at 20. Market cap: 10,000,000 x 20 = 200,000,000.

During one day, 50,000 units trade, and the price falls to 18.

  • New market cap: 10,000,000 x 18 = 180,000,000.
  • Change in market cap: 20,000,000 lower.
  • Money that changed hands: at most 50,000 x 20 = 1,000,000, since every trade was at 20 or below.

A headline could say “20 million wiped out”. No 20 million left anywhere. About 1 million moved from buyers to sellers, and the remaining 9,950,000 units did not trade at all. They are simply valued at the new price.

What it does NOT mean

  • Market cap is not the money invested in an asset. Holders bought at many different prices, and the total they paid is a different, usually unknown number.
  • A change in market cap is not money flowing in or out. It changes whenever the latest price changes, even on a very small trade.
  • Market cap is not what the asset could be sold for. Selling a large part of the supply would move the price.
  • A low unit price does not mean a small or cheap asset. Unit price depends on how many units exist.
  • A large market cap does not mean an asset is sound. It reports size at the latest price and nothing about quality or risk.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Comparing unit prices. “This coin is only 0.01, so it has more room” ignores the supply. Only the product of price and supply is comparable.
  2. Reasoning about money needed. “It would take X of new money to double the market cap” has no fixed answer. It depends on the orders in the book at that moment.
  3. Ignoring future supply. New shares or newly unlocked tokens raise the unit count. See Token Unlocks & Emissions.
  4. Trusting the figure for thinly traded assets. With little trading, the last price may reflect very few units.
  5. Mixing market cap with fully diluted valuation. They use different supply numbers. See Market Cap vs Fully Diluted Valuation.

Limits

  • Supply data for crypto assets is not standardised, and providers disagree.
  • Market cap ignores a company’s debts and cash, so it is not the full price of buying a business.
  • It is a snapshot at one price on one venue or an average of several. It changes continuously.

Key takeaway

Market cap is the latest price multiplied by the units outstanding. It is a useful measure of size and a poor measure of money: it can change by millions when only a few units trade.
