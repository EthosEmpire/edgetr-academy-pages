Supply & Demand

Supply is how much of something sellers offer at each price, demand is how much buyers want at each price, and the market price settles where the two balance.

Why it matters

Supply and demand is the oldest explanation of prices, and still the most useful. Every reason given for a price move, from an earnings report to a change in a coin’s issuance, works by changing how much people want to buy or sell at each price.

The idea also protects against a common trap: thinking that “low supply” or “high demand” by itself tells you where a price is heading.

How it works

Demand is the amount buyers are willing to buy at each possible price. For most things, buyers want more when the price is lower.

Supply is the amount sellers are willing to sell at each possible price. Sellers usually offer more when the price is higher.

The price at which the two amounts match is called the equilibrium price. In the table above it is 100. At 105, sellers offer 400 units and buyers want only 200, so some sellers lower their price to find a buyer. At 95, buyers want 400 and sellers offer only 200, so some buyers raise their bid. Both pressures push the price back toward 100.

In plain words

Concert tickets show both sides. If a small venue hosts a famous band, far more people want tickets than there are seats, and resale prices climb. If a huge stadium hosts an unknown band, seats go unsold and prices are cut. The band did not get better or worse. The balance did.

Shifts: when the balance changes

A price moves to a new level when one of the schedules shifts: buyers want more or less at every price, or sellers offer more or less at every price.

Change Typical effect on price, all else equal
Demand increases Higher
Demand decreases Lower
Supply increases Lower
Supply decreases Higher

The phrase “all else equal” carries a lot of weight. In real markets both sides shift at once, and the result depends on which shift is larger.

Going deeper

Supply and demand in an order book

In a trading venue, supply and demand are visible in the order book. The bids are the demand currently on display. The asks are the supply currently on display. A trade happens when the two meet, as described in Buyers, Sellers & Price.

The book is only a partial picture. Most people who would buy or sell at some price have not placed an order yet. Demand and supply also include those unseen intentions, which is why a book can change in an instant.

Two meanings of “supply”

For financial assets the word is used in two ways:

  • Units in existence. The number of shares a company has issued, or the number of coins created so far. See Token Supply and The 21 Million Limit.
  • Units for sale now. The amount holders are actually willing to sell at current prices.

The two can differ enormously. An asset can have billions of units in existence and very few offered for sale, or the reverse. Prices respond to the second meaning.

Demand is not need or popularity

In economics, demand means willingness and ability to buy at a given price. Many people liking an asset is not demand until they place orders with money behind them.

“Supply zones” and “demand zones” on charts

In technical analysis, some traders label chart areas where price previously fell away as supply zones and areas where it previously rose as demand zones. These are a way of describing support and resistance. They are interpretations of past price action, not measurements of actual supply or demand.

A hypothetical demand shiftHypothetical illustration

Use the schedule above. The market is balanced at 100, with 300 units wanted and 300 offered.

Some news makes buyers want 200 more units at every price.

  • At 100, buyers now want 300 + 200 = 500. Sellers still offer 300. There is a shortage of 200.
  • Buyers compete, and the price rises.
  • At 105, buyers want 200 + 200 = 400, and sellers offer 400. The two sides match again.

The new equilibrium is 105. The price rose by 5, the amount traded rose from 300 to 400, and the number of units in existence did not change at all.

What it does NOT mean

  • A limited supply does not make a price rise. Scarcity only matters relative to demand. Something can be rare and unwanted.
  • High demand does not mean the price keeps rising. A higher price itself reduces the amount buyers want and brings out more sellers.
  • The equilibrium price is not a fair or correct value. It is where the current buyers and sellers happen to balance.
  • Supply and demand cannot be read off a chart with precision. A chart shows where trades happened, not the full schedules behind them.
  • A fixed number of units does not mean a fixed amount for sale. Holders decide every day how much they offer.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Reasoning from one side only. “Supply is shrinking, so the price must go up” ignores what demand is doing.
  2. Mixing up units in existence with units for sale. Prices respond to what holders are willing to sell, not to a total on a statistics page.
  3. Treating a chart zone as a physical stock of orders. Past turning points show where the balance changed before. The orders that caused it may be long gone.
  4. Forgetting that price changes behaviour. A higher price attracts sellers and deters buyers, which is what stops moves from continuing indefinitely.

Limits

  • The simple model assumes many independent buyers and sellers. Thin markets with a few large participants behave less tidily.
  • Real schedules are invisible and change constantly. Nobody can observe the full supply and demand for an asset.
  • Financial assets can break the everyday pattern for a time: rising prices sometimes attract more buyers, a behaviour discussed in FOMO.
  • The model explains why prices change. It does not tell you when or by how much.

Key takeaway

Price settles where the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer, and it moves when that balance shifts. Supply or demand alone never tells the whole story.
