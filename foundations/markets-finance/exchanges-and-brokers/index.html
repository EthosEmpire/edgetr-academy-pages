Exchanges & Brokers

An exchange is a venue where buy and sell orders are matched under published rules. A broker is an intermediary that gives customers access to that venue and handles their orders.

  • Updated 21 November 2026

Why it matters

A beginner sees one screen with a “buy” button. Behind it there is a chain of organisations, each doing a specific job. Knowing who does what explains fees, delays and, above all, whom you are relying on.

It also explains one of the largest structural differences between traditional markets and crypto: how many of those jobs sit inside a single company.

How it works

An exchange is an organised marketplace. It operates the system that collects orders, matches buyers with sellers and publishes the resulting prices. It sets rules for who may trade and how. It does not take a side in the trades.

A broker is an intermediary acting on behalf of customers. In traditional markets, members of the public cannot connect to a stock exchange directly. They open an account with a broker, and the broker passes their orders on.

In plain words

An exchange is like an auction house: it provides the room, the rules and the hammer. A broker is like an agent who attends the auction for you because you are not allowed in the room. The agent bids as you instructed. The auction house only makes sure the sale is run properly.

What happens after the trade

A trade is an agreement. Two further steps complete it.

Clearing confirms the details and works out who owes what. In many markets a clearing house places itself between the two sides: it becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. If one side fails to deliver, the clearing house is responsible to the other. This reduces counterparty risk, the risk that the other party does not keep its side of the deal.

Settlement is the actual exchange of cash for the asset. Many stock markets settle one or two business days after the trade date. Ownership is usually recorded electronically at a central depository, with the broker or a custodian holding the assets on the customer’s behalf.

Going deeper

Brokers and dealers

A broker arranges trades for others as an agent. A dealer trades for its own account, buying from and selling to customers. Many firms do both and are called broker-dealers. A market maker is a kind of dealer.

Where an order actually goes

A broker does not always send an order to the main exchange. Depending on the country’s rules, it may route it to another trading venue or to a market maker that fills it directly. Regulators generally require brokers to seek favourable execution for customers, and the detailed rules differ between jurisdictions.

How brokers and exchanges earn money

Exchanges typically charge trading fees, listing fees and fees for market data. Brokers may charge commissions, earn interest on customer balances, add a margin to the spread or receive payments from the firms that execute their customers’ orders. “Zero commission” describes one fee, not the absence of all costs.

The crypto version

A centralized crypto exchange usually combines the roles: customers open accounts with it directly, it matches the trades, and it holds the coins. Trades between its customers are typically recorded on the exchange’s own internal ledger, and the blockchain is used only for deposits and withdrawals. A customer who leaves coins on the exchange is relying on that company to hold them, a point covered in Custodial & Non-Custodial Wallets.

A decentralized exchange removes the company from the middle. A user’s wallet interacts with a smart contract, and each trade settles on the blockchain. That removes one kind of reliance and introduces others, such as smart contract risk.

A hypothetical share purchaseHypothetical illustration

A customer uses a broker to buy 10 shares at a limit price of 50.

  1. The broker checks that the account holds enough cash: 10 x 50 = 500, plus a commission of 1.
  2. The broker sends the order to an exchange. A seller’s order at 50 is waiting, and the two are matched.
  3. The customer’s account shows 10 shares almost immediately and a cash reduction of 501.
  4. In the background, the clearing house records the trade. On the settlement date, 500 moves to the seller’s side and 10 shares move to the buyer’s side.

The customer dealt only with the broker. The seller is unknown to the buyer, and neither needs to trust the other, because the clearing house stands between them.

What it does NOT mean

  • An exchange does not set prices. It matches orders. The prices come from the participants.
  • A broker is not the other side of your trade by default. It acts as your agent, though some firms also deal from their own account, and must disclose it where rules require.
  • A trade on screen is not yet settled. Final transfer of cash and assets follows later in many markets.
  • “Exchange” does not mean the same thing in crypto. A crypto exchange is often broker, venue and custodian at once.
  • Being regulated does not remove risk. Rules reduce certain failures. They do not protect against a fall in price.

Common misunderstandings

  1. Thinking the app is the market. The app belongs to an intermediary. The market is where the orders meet.
  2. Assuming zero commission means zero cost. Spreads and other charges remain.
  3. Believing an account balance is the same as holding the asset yourself. In both traditional and crypto markets, an intermediary usually holds it for you.
  4. Assuming protections carry over. Investor protection schemes that apply to brokers in one country may not apply to crypto platforms or to firms elsewhere.

Limits

  • Market structure, settlement times and customer protections vary by country and asset type.
  • This page simplifies a system with many additional parties, such as custodians, transfer agents and alternative trading venues.
  • Rules for crypto platforms are still developing and differ widely between jurisdictions.

Key takeaway

An exchange is the venue where orders are matched, and a broker is the agent that takes your order there. In traditional markets those jobs, plus clearing and custody, are split between firms. In crypto, one company often does them all.
