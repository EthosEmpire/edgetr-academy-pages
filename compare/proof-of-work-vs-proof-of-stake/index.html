Proof of Work vs Proof of Stake

Proof of Work secures a blockchain with computation and energy. Proof of Stake secures it with locked-up coins. Both make it costly to rewrite the ledger.

The idea in one picture

A public blockchain has no central operator, so its participants need a way to agree on which transactions happened and in what order. That is consensus. It also needs a way to stop someone from creating thousands of fake participants and outvoting everyone. Both mechanisms solve this the same way: by tying influence to something scarce and costly.

In plain words

Imagine a lottery to decide who writes the next page of a shared record book.

In Proof of Work, tickets are earned by burning fuel. The more fuel you burn, the more tickets you hold.

In Proof of Stake, tickets are earned by placing a deposit. The larger the deposit, the more tickets, and cheating can cost part of it.

Either way, influence has a price, and the price is what protects the book.

How each one works

In Proof of Work, miners repeatedly hash candidate blocks, looking for a result below a target set by the network. Finding one takes an enormous number of attempts, and checking it takes one. The first miner to succeed publishes the block and collects the reward. The process is described in Proof of Work & Mining.

In Proof of Stake, validators deposit coins. The protocol selects one of them to propose each block, and others attest that it is valid. In Ethereum, the next block proposer is picked by a pseudorandom draw (RANDAO), weighted by stake. Honest participation earns rewards. Provable dishonesty, such as signing two conflicting blocks, can cause part of the deposit to be destroyed, a penalty called slashing. Details differ considerably from one Proof of Stake chain to another.

One percent of the scarce resourceHypothetical illustration

Two hypothetical networks, with made-up numbers.

  • Proof of Work. The network’s total computing power is 1,000 units. A miner runs 10 units, which is 10 / 1,000 = 1%. Over a long period, that miner can expect to find about 1% of the blocks.
  • Proof of Stake. The total stake is 1,000,000 coins. A validator has staked 10,000, which is 10,000 / 1,000,000 = 1%. Over a long period, that validator can expect to propose about 1% of the blocks.

In both systems the share of the scarce resource sets the share of the blocks, on average. In any short period the actual number varies with chance. The miner’s cost is ongoing electricity. The validator’s cost is capital that is locked and can be penalised.

When the distinction matters

Energy

Proof of Work consumes large amounts of electricity. That consumption is the security mechanism, not a side effect: rewriting history would require spending comparable energy again. Proof of Stake replaces that cost with capital at risk and uses a small fraction of the energy. Ethereum moved from Proof of Work to Proof of Stake in September 2022, an event known as the Merge, and its documentation reports a reduction in energy use of more than 99%.

What an attack costs

Attacking a Proof of Work chain requires controlling a majority of its computing power, which means hardware and electricity that exist outside the system. Attacking a Proof of Stake chain requires a large share of the staked coins, which exist inside it and can be slashed. Supporters of each design see its own form of cost as the more robust. The question is debated, and neither is immune.

Concentration

Both designs show concentration in practice. Mining tends to gather into large operations and pools, where power is cheap and hardware is bought in bulk. Staking tends to gather in large holders and in staking services that pool many users’ coins.

Common confusion

  • Miners and validators do not approve transactions by opinion. They follow rules that every node checks. A block that breaks the rules is rejected however much work or stake is behind it.
  • Staking is not the same as lending. Protocol staking locks coins to take part in consensus. Products that offer a yield under the name “staking” may work quite differently.
  • Consensus type does not determine speed. Throughput depends on many design choices besides the consensus mechanism.

What it does NOT mean

  • Neither mechanism is “the secure one”. Each has a different cost of attack and a different set of weaknesses. Security also depends on how large and how widely distributed the network is.
  • Proof of Stake does not mean free rewards. Staked coins can be locked, penalised and fall in value, and rewards are paid in the same coin.
  • Proof of Work does not mean wasteful by accident. The expense is the deterrent. Whether that expense is justified is a real and separate debate.

Key takeaway

Proof of Work ties influence over a blockchain to energy spent, and Proof of Stake ties it to capital locked and at risk. Both make cheating expensive in different ways, and each carries its own trade-offs in energy, concentration and complexity.
