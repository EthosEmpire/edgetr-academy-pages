Isolated vs Cross Margin

Isolated margin normally limits the loss on a position to the margin assigned to it. Cross margin backs every position with the shared account balance, so one position can draw on all of it.

Isolated margin

A fixed amount of margin is assigned to one position. If the position is liquidated, the loss normally stops at that amount.

vs

Cross margin

All positions share the account's available balance as margin. A losing position draws on it automatically.

The idea in one picture

A leveraged position is backed by collateral, called margin. The margin mode decides how much of the account that collateral includes. With isolated margin, a set amount is fenced off for one position. With cross margin, the available balance of the account stands behind every position at once.

In plain words

Isolated margin is a ship with watertight compartments. A hole floods one compartment and the rest stays dry. That compartment fills quickly.

Cross margin is one open hull. A leak takes far longer to matter, because there is more space to fill. If it is not stopped, the whole ship goes down.

How the mode changes liquidation

A position is liquidated when the collateral behind it no longer covers the loss plus a required minimum. The more collateral there is, the further price has to move before that happens.

Under isolated margin, the collateral is small and fixed, so the liquidation price sits close to the entry. Under cross margin, the collateral is the whole balance, so the liquidation price sits further away, and it shifts whenever the balance or other positions change.

One position, two modesHypothetical illustration

An account holds 1,000. A trader opens a long of 50 units at 100, a position worth 5,000. Numbers are hypothetical and simplified: fees, funding and the exchange’s maintenance requirement are ignored, so real liquidation would come somewhat earlier.

Isolated, with 500 assigned as margin. Each 1 fall in price loses 50. The 500 is used up after a fall of 10, at a price of 90. The other 500 in the account is not touched.

Cross. The full 1,000 backs the position. It is used up after a fall of 20, at a price of 80.

Now two price paths.

  • Price falls to 88, then recovers to 105. The isolated position was liquidated on the way down, near 90, for a loss of 500. The cross position survived the dip and is ahead by 50 x 5 = 250.
  • Price falls to 80 and keeps falling. The isolated position lost 500, and 500 remains. The cross position lost the entire 1,000.

The same trade ended better under cross margin on the first path and far worse on the second.

When the distinction matters

Several positions at once

Under cross margin, positions are linked through the shared balance. An unrealised gain in one adds to the margin available to the others, and a loss in one brings every position closer to liquidation. A single sharp move in one market can therefore close positions in unrelated markets. Under isolated margin, positions are normally kept apart, although with an automatic top-up, which some exchanges offer, an isolated position can draw on the wider account.

Knowing the worst case

With isolated margin, the amount at stake on a position is set when it is opened. With cross margin, the amount at stake is whatever the account holds, including funds that were not intended for that trade.

Being liquidated on noise

The closer liquidation price of isolated margin means a brief spike can close a position that would otherwise have recovered. This is the cost of the cap.

Defaults

Exchanges differ in which mode is the default, in whether the mode can be changed while a position is open, and in how liquidation is carried out. Some also offer portfolio or multi-asset margin systems with their own rules. The mode in force is a setting worth knowing before anything else about a leveraged position.

Common confusion

  • The margin mode is not the leverage setting. Leverage determines position size relative to margin. The mode determines which funds count as margin.
  • Cross margin does not lower the loss per unit of price movement. A position of 50 units loses 50 for each 1 of price under either mode. Only the point of liquidation differs.
  • Isolated does not mean capped in all conditions. In extreme moves, losses can exceed the assigned margin, and how the shortfall is handled depends on the exchange.
  • “Available balance” means the margin account. Funds held in a separate spot wallet are usually not included, though arrangements vary.

What it does NOT mean

  • Isolated margin does not mean safe. It limits the loss per position. Many positions, each losing its full margin, can still empty an account.
  • Cross margin does not mean a position is protected. A more distant liquidation price means a larger loss if it is reached.
  • Neither mode is a substitute for a planned exit. Liquidation is the exchange’s last resort, not a risk plan. See Stop Loss vs Liquidation.
  • Neither mode reduces leverage risk. They distribute it differently.

Key takeaway

Isolated margin fences off a fixed amount for each position, normally capping the loss and bringing liquidation closer. Cross margin puts the shared balance behind every position, moving liquidation further away and exposing the whole account.
