Bullish vs Bearish

The idea in one picture

The two words are opposites. Bullish is about prices going up, bearish about prices going down. The usual explanation of the imagery is that a bull strikes upward with its horns and a bear swipes downward with its paws.

In plain words

Think of a weather forecast. “Sunny” can describe the sky right now, or what someone expects tomorrow. Either way it is a description or an opinion. It is not the same thing as packing sunglasses. Bullish and bearish are the forecast. A position is what someone does, or does not do, about it.

Two uses of each word

Each word is used in two ways, and it helps to notice which one is meant.

A condition. A bull market is a period in which prices broadly rise over months or years. A bear market is a period in which they broadly fall. A common convention in stock markets calls a decline of 20% or more from a recent high a bear market. The threshold is a habit of speech and has no special force. These labels describe what has already happened.

An opinion. “I am bullish on this asset” means the speaker expects its price to rise. It is a statement about the speaker. It carries no information about what the price will do.

When the distinction matters

View and position are different things

Bullish and bearish describe an expectation or a condition. Long and short describe a position that has actually been opened. A person can be bullish and hold nothing. A person can be bearish on the next week and still hold an asset they intend to keep for years. The position words are covered in Long vs Short.

The timeframe changes the answer

A market can be bullish on one timeframe and bearish on another at the same moment. Without a timeframe, the words are incomplete.

Bullish and bearish at onceHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset has risen from 60 to 120 over the past year. In the last three weeks it has fallen from 120 to 105.

  • On the weekly chart the structure is a series of higher highs and higher lows. The fall from 120 to 105 is a decline of 15, or 12.5%, inside an advance of 60. This chart reads as bullish.
  • On the daily chart the last three weeks show lower highs and lower lows. This chart reads as bearish.

Someone who says “I am bullish” and someone who says “I am bearish” may both be describing this asset accurately, each on a different timeframe. Neither statement says what happens next.

Sentiment is not price

Bullish and bearish are also used for the mood of a crowd. Widespread optimism or pessimism is a fact about people. Prices have fallen while most participants were optimistic and risen while most were pessimistic. The link between mood and the longer rise and fall of markets is described in market cycles.

Common confusion

  • Bullish is not the same as long. One is a view, the other is a position. See the four words explained.
  • Bearish is not the same as negative about a project. A person can admire a technology and still expect its price to fall.
  • A bull market does not mean every asset rises. The term describes the broad market. Individual assets fall during bull markets and rise during bear markets.
  • A green candle is not a bull market. One candle, or one week, is not a sustained condition.
  • Neutral exists. Having no view is a view, and holding no position is always possible.

What it does NOT mean

  • Bullish does not mean “buy”. A view about direction is not a decision about whether, when or how much to risk.
  • Bearish does not mean “sell” or “open a short”. A falling market is a condition. What anyone does in it is a separate matter.
  • A bull market is not confirmation that it will continue. The label is applied to the past. Every bull market carried the label until it ended.
  • Someone else’s bullish or bearish statement is not information about the future. It tells you what that person expects, and possibly what they already hold.
  • Bullish is not “good” and bearish “bad”. Falling prices are unwelcome to holders and welcome to those waiting to buy. The words describe direction only.

Key takeaway

Bullish and bearish describe direction: rising or falling prices, as an observed condition or as an expectation. They always depend on the timeframe, and they are views, not positions and not instructions.
