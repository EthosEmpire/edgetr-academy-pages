Coin

The built-in asset of a blockchain. It is created by the network's own rules and is used to pay that network's fees.

vs

Token

An asset defined by a smart contract deployed on someone else's blockchain. The contract's code keeps the balances and sets the rules.

Both are covered in depth on one page

The idea in one picture

Both are digital assets recorded on a blockchain, and in casual speech “coin” is used for both. The technical difference is where the asset comes from. A coin is built into a blockchain. A token is created by a program running on one.

In plain words

A country issues its own currency: that is the coin. A shop inside that country prints gift vouchers: those are tokens. The vouchers depend on the country’s roads, laws and currency to function, and the shop decides the voucher’s rules. Anyone can open a shop. Founding a country is a much bigger undertaking.

How each one works

A coin exists because the protocol says so. Bitcoin’s rules define bitcoin, and Ethereum’s rules define ether. New units appear only as the protocol allows, for example as a reward to those who produce blocks.

A token exists because someone deployed a smart contract. The contract is essentially a ledger: a list of addresses and balances, with functions to transfer them. Shared standards, such as ERC-20 on Ethereum, make different tokens behave alike. Stablecoins and governance tokens are common kinds.

The fuller treatment is in Coins & Tokens.

When the distinction matters

Fees are normally paid in the coin

Almost every transaction on a chain pays a network fee in that chain’s native coin. This includes transactions that move tokens.

A token that cannot moveHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical wallet on Ethereum holds 500 units of a stablecoin token and 0 ether. The owner tries to send 100 of the tokens to another address.

The transfer needs a network fee, say 0.001 ether. The wallet has none, so the transaction cannot be sent. The 500 tokens are intact and, for the moment, immovable.

Once a small amount of ether is added, the transfer goes through. The tokens were never the problem. The chain normally accepts only its own coin as payment for the work.

Anyone can create a token

Launching a coin means building and sustaining a network. Deploying a token takes a short contract and a small fee, and requires nobody’s approval. The existence of a token therefore says nothing about the seriousness of whoever made it. Tokens can be given any name, including the name of a well-known asset, and the contract address is what actually identifies one.

The contract sets the rules

A token behaves exactly as its contract is written. Some contracts let the issuer create more units, pause transfers or freeze particular addresses. Some contain errors. This adds a layer of smart contract risk on top of whatever risk the host chain has. Malicious contracts, such as tokens that can be bought and not sold, are described under rug pulls and honeypots.

The same name on several chains

A token can be issued on many blockchains, with a separate contract on each. A coin can also appear on a foreign chain as a “wrapped” token that represents it, usually through a bridge. Sending an asset on the wrong network is a common and often permanent mistake.

Common confusion

  • “Altcoin” is not a technical category. It means any crypto asset other than bitcoin, whether coin or token.
  • The line can move. Some projects began as tokens on another chain and later launched their own blockchain.
  • A wrapped coin is a token. It is a contract’s representation of a coin, and it depends on whatever holds the original.

What it does NOT mean

  • Coin does not mean established, and token does not mean risky. A token does add smart contract risk, but the category itself does not describe quality. New blockchains fail, and long-standing tokens exist.
  • A token on a well-known chain is not vouched for by that chain. The host network runs any contract, without judging it.
  • Neither word says anything about value. They describe how an asset is built, not what it is worth.

Key takeaway

A coin is native to its own blockchain and pays that chain’s fees. A token is issued by a smart contract on an existing chain and follows whatever rules that contract contains. The difference is one of construction, not of quality.
