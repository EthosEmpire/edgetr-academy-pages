The idea in one picture

Both indicators sit in a panel below the price chart, and both are described as measuring momentum. They are built differently and answer different questions. The RSI asks how one-sided recent price changes have been. The MACD asks how far apart a fast and a slow average are, and whether that gap is widening or narrowing.

In plain words

The RSI is like a car’s rev counter. It has a fixed dial with a red zone, and it shows how hard the engine is working right now. It can sit in the red for a long time on a motorway.

The MACD is like comparing your speed over the last minute with your speed over the last ten. It shows whether you are speeding up or slowing down, and it has no dial limits at all.

How each is built

RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + RS), where RS = average gain / average lossCalculated over 14 periods by default. The result is always between 0 and 100.
MACD line = EMA(12) - EMA(26). Signal line = EMA(9) of the MACD line. Histogram = MACD line - signal line.Measured in the asset's own price units, with no upper or lower limit.

The RSI is bounded: whatever the asset, its value falls between 0 and 100. By convention, readings above 70 are called overbought and readings below 30 oversold. The MACD is unbounded: its value is a difference between two averages of price, so it scales with the price of the asset.

When the distinction matters

Comparing across assets

An RSI of 65 means the same thing on any chart. A MACD value of 4 does not. On an asset priced near 100 it is a sizeable gap between the averages. On an asset priced near 10,000 it is almost nothing. MACD values are meaningful relative to their own history on the same chart.

Trending and sideways markets

In a strong trend the RSI can remain above 70, or below 30, for long periods. The label “overbought” then describes persistent strength, not an imminent turn. The MACD, being built from trend-following averages, is more at home in trends and suffers in sideways markets, where its lines cross repeatedly without any lasting move behind them.

Lag

The RSI responds to each new close fairly directly. The MACD is an average of averages, and its signal line is a further average on top. It is smoother, and it reports changes later.

One market, two readingsHypothetical illustration

A hypothetical asset has risen steadily for six weeks and closes at 110.

  • RSI (14) reads 74. Recent gains have been much larger than recent losses. By convention this is above the overbought level.
  • MACD. The 12-period EMA is 108 and the 26-period EMA is 104, so the MACD line is 108 - 104 = 4. The signal line is 3.2. The histogram is 4 - 3.2 = 0.8, which is positive. The fast average is above the slow one and the gap is still widening.

The two readings look contradictory if they are treated as instructions: one “overbought”, one “positive”. They are not instructions. Both say the same thing in different units: the advance has been strong and one-sided. Neither says whether it continues.

Using both

The two are often shown together. Because both are calculated from the same closing prices, agreement between them is weaker evidence than it appears. This is discussed in combining indicators.

Common confusion

  • Overbought does not mean too high. It is the name of a range of RSI values. A market can stay there while price keeps rising.
  • A MACD crossing is not an event in the market. It is the moment one average of past prices passes another.
  • MACD is not bounded by anything. There is no “overbought level” on a MACD that applies across charts.
  • Divergence exists on both. Divergence between price and either indicator describes slowing momentum. It can persist for a long time without a reversal.
  • The RSI does not compare one asset with another. Despite its name, it measures an asset against its own recent history.

What it does NOT mean

  • Neither indicator predicts price. Both are arithmetic on past closes and contain no information that the price chart does not.
  • A reading is not a signal. Levels and crossings are conventions for describing the calculation.
  • Two indicators agreeing is not two pieces of evidence. They share the same input.
  • Default settings are not optimal settings. 14 and 12/26/9 are the originators’ conventions, and different settings give different readings from the same prices.

Key takeaway

The RSI is a bounded oscillator that shows how one-sided recent price changes have been, on a scale from 0 to 100. The MACD is an unbounded measure of the gap between two EMAs. They describe the same past prices in different ways, and neither forecasts.

Sources and further reading
