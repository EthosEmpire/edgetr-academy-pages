Layer 1

A base blockchain. It runs its own consensus, keeps its own ledger and is responsible for its own security.

vs

Layer 2

A system built on top of a Layer 1. It handles transactions away from the base chain and anchors the results to it.

The idea in one picture

A Layer 1 is a blockchain that stands on its own: Bitcoin and Ethereum are the best-known examples. A Layer 2 is a separate system built on top of one. It carries out transactions elsewhere and uses the Layer 1 as the final record. The fuller explanation is in Layer 1 & Layer 2.

In plain words

A Layer 1 is a courthouse. Everything recorded there is official, and it can only handle so many cases a day, so each one is slow and costly.

A Layer 2 is a group of people who settle their everyday dealings among themselves and file one summary with the courthouse. Far more gets done for far less. The courthouse still stands behind the result, as long as the summary is filed honestly and can be challenged.

Why Layer 2 exists

A public blockchain asks many independent computers to verify every transaction. That keeps it open and hard to tamper with, and it limits how many transactions fit in each block. When demand exceeds the space, network fees rise. Raising the limits would make the chain harder for ordinary participants to verify.

Layer 2 systems take a different route: move the activity off the base chain and keep the base chain as the anchor.

How a Layer 2 settles

On Ethereum the main approach is the rollup. A rollup executes transactions itself, then posts compressed data and a proof to the Layer 1. Optimistic rollups assume a batch is valid and allow a period in which it can be challenged. Zero-knowledge rollups post a cryptographic proof that the batch is valid.

On Bitcoin, the Lightning Network uses payment channels. Two parties lock funds on the base chain, exchange any number of payments between themselves, and record only the opening and closing on Bitcoin.

Sharing one base-chain costHypothetical illustration

Hypothetical numbers.

On the Layer 1, 1,000 separate transactions each pay a fee of 2.00. Total: 1,000 x 2.00 = 2,000.

On a rollup, the same 1,000 transactions are bundled. Posting the bundle to the Layer 1 costs 100 in total, or 100 / 1,000 = 0.10 per transaction. The rollup adds 0.02 each for its own processing. Fee per transaction: 0.10 + 0.02 = 0.12.

The saving comes from dividing one base-chain cost among many users. If the base chain becomes more expensive, or the bundle carries fewer transactions, the fee per transaction rises.

When the distinction matters

Where the security comes from

A Layer 1 is secured by its own consensus. A Layer 2 borrows much of its security from the base chain and adds assumptions of its own: that its contracts on the Layer 1 are correct, that the proof system works, and that data is available for anyone to check. How much is inherited differs from one design to another.

Operators and upgrades

Many rollups currently rely on a single operator, called a sequencer, to order transactions. If it stops, activity can pause, and a single operator can also delay or reorder transactions. Many also keep upgrade keys that allow a small group to change the contracts. These are forms of trust that the base chain does not require.

Moving assets across

Assets reach a Layer 2 through a bridge: they are locked in a contract on the Layer 1 and represented on the Layer 2. Withdrawing can take from minutes to about a week, depending on the design. An asset sent to the right address on the wrong network can be difficult or impossible to recover.

Common confusion

  • A Layer 2 is not a competing blockchain. New base chains that offer low fees are Layer 1s. A Layer 2 settles to another chain.
  • A sidechain is not strictly a Layer 2. A sidechain has its own consensus and its own security, connected by a bridge. The terms are often used loosely.
  • The same token exists separately on each network. A balance on a Layer 2 does not appear on the Layer 1 until it is withdrawn.

What it does NOT mean

  • Layer 2 does not mean “as secure as the Layer 1”. It inherits part of that security and adds risks from bridges, operators and smart contracts.
  • Layer 1 does not mean slow or outdated. The base layer is what everything above it depends on.
  • Lower fees do not mean lower risk. Cost and security are separate properties.
  • The layer says nothing about an asset’s value. It describes where transactions are processed and settled.

Key takeaway

A Layer 1 is a base blockchain with its own consensus and security. A Layer 2 processes transactions off that chain and settles back to it, gaining capacity and lower fees in exchange for additional components that have to be trusted.
