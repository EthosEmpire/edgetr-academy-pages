The idea in one picture

Both positions follow the same three steps: open, wait while price moves, close. The only difference is the order of buying and selling, and therefore which direction of price movement helps.

In plain words

Long is the everyday way of owning something: you buy it, and you are better off if it becomes worth more.

Short is the reverse: you sell something first at today’s price and buy it back later. You are better off if it becomes cheaper in the meantime.

How can you sell something you do not own?

This is the part that confuses almost everyone at first. There are two common mechanisms.

Borrowing (traditional short selling). The trader borrows the asset from a broker or lender, sells it immediately, and later buys the same amount back to return it. If the price fell, buying it back costs less than the sale brought in. If the price rose, buying it back costs more. A borrowing fee is paid for as long as the position is open.

Derivatives (most crypto shorts). On a perpetual futures market, nobody borrows anything. The trader opens a contract whose value moves opposite to the price. Collateral, called margin, is posted to cover possible losses, and funding payments are exchanged between longs and shorts.

When the distinction matters

The risk is not symmetrical

A long position without leverage can lose, at most, what was paid, because a price cannot fall below zero. A short position has no such ceiling: a price can keep rising, so the loss on a short is not capped in theory. In practice a short is closed, or liquidated, long before that, but this asymmetry is why short positions demand tighter risk control.

Shorts usually involve leverage or borrowing

Buying an asset on a spot market involves no borrowing. Shorting almost always involves margin, borrowing or a derivative. That adds costs (borrow fees, funding) and adds the possibility of liquidation.

Short squeezes

When price rises quickly, short sellers who are losing may be forced to buy back to close. Their buying pushes the price higher, which forces more shorts to close. This chain reaction is called a short squeeze. The mirror image for leveraged longs is a long squeeze or liquidation cascade.

The same 10% move from both sidesHypothetical illustration

An asset trades at 100. One trader opens a long, another opens a short, each for one unit and without leverage.

  • Price rises to 110. The long is up 10. The short is down 10.
  • Price falls to 90. The long is down 10. The short is up 10.

Every market has both sides present at once. One side’s gain in a derivative contract is the other side’s loss. Fees and funding are ignored here.

Common confusion

  • Long is not the same as bullish. Bullish is an opinion or a market condition. Long is a position. A trader can believe a market is bullish and hold no position at all. See Bullish vs Bearish.
  • Short is not the same as selling. Selling something you own simply closes a long. A short is a new position that gains from falling prices.
  • Short does not mean short-term. “Long” and “short” describe direction, not duration. A short position can be held for months, and a long for seconds.
  • Having no position is a position. Staying in cash is a valid third state. Nothing requires a trader to be long or short.

What it does NOT mean

  • “Price is falling” does not mean “open a short”. A falling market is a condition. Whether, where and how to take any position is a separate decision that depends on risk.
  • A long is not “safe” and a short “dangerous”. A leveraged long can be liquidated just as fast as a leveraged short.
  • Shorting is not betting against a project or company in a moral sense. Short positions are also used to hedge, meaning to reduce the risk of something already owned.
  • Neither direction is guaranteed an edge. Outcomes depend on entry, exit, size, cost and chance.

Key takeaway

Long gains when price rises, short gains when price falls. They are directions, not opinions and not recommendations, and choosing neither is always allowed.
